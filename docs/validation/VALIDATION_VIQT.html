Vocabulary IQ Test Validation: Item Response Theory

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

Description

Vocabulary is the classic marker of crystallised intelligence, the knowledge that builds up through schooling, reading and experience (Horn & Cattell, 1966). The Vocabulary IQ Test (VIQT) is a 45-item online test from the Open-Source Psychometrics Project. Each item lists five words, and the task is to pick the two words with the same meaning: in tiny, faded, new, large, big the answer is large = big. Respondents were told not to guess, that a wrong answer costs 0.35 points, and that they could answer don’t know instead.

The other tutorials on this site validate self-report questionnaires, where there are no right answers. An ability test is the natural place for item response theory (IRT): items differ in how hard they are and in how sharply they separate people who know a word from people who do not, and IRT puts items and people on one scale. This tutorial calibrates the 45 items with the mirt package (Chalmers, 2012) in 11,961 respondents. It compares the Rasch, two- and three-parameter models, shows how precise the test is at each ability level, tests item fit and differential item functioning, checks the scores against education, native language and age, and finally runs the calibrated items as a simulated computerised adaptive test.

Data, answer key and scoring

The data come from the Open-Source Psychometrics Project (VIQT_data.zip, 12,173 records collected online between July 2017 and March 2018). The file holds only the people who also completed an optional survey after the test (30 personality statements and five demographic questions), so it is a self-selected group of test takers.

Answer key. The codebook (codebook.txt in the zip) gives the correct answer of every item as a number. An answer is stored as a sum over the selected words: 1 for the first word, 2 for the second, 4, 8 and 16 for the fifth, so 3 means words 1 and 2 and 24 means words 4 and 5. Decoded this way, the codebook key gives a synonym pair for every item (see the item list below). Scoring the data with it reproduces the number of right answers that the test site computed (score_right in the data) for all 12,173 records; data/prepare_viqt.R checks this before it saves the data. The key comes from the codebook, not from the data.

Scoring. An item scores 1 when both correct words were chosen and 0 otherwise: a wrong pair, one or three words, don’t know or no answer at all. This is the number-right score the site reported. Don’t know counts as wrong, not as missing, because it is the answer the instructions asked for when a respondent did not know the pair. People answer don’t know to the items they find hard, so ignoring these answers would reward skipping hard items. The 0.35-point penalty of the site’s formula score is not used. In IRT a wrong answer and a don’t know both mean the respondent did not find the pair, and the model, not a fixed penalty, decides how much a lucky guess is worth (the three-parameter model below tests whether guessing needs its own parameter). The section Don’t know: wrong or missing? shows how much this choice matters.

Item statistics

The proportion correct is the classical item difficulty. The point-biserial correlation relates the item to the total score; the corrected correlation relates it to the total of the other 44 items, so the item does not correlate with itself. For 0/1 items, Cronbach’s alpha is the Kuder-Richardson formula 20 (KR-20; Kuder & Richardson, 1937): \(\text{KR-20} = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum p_j q_j}{\sigma^2_X}\right)\).

The lowest item-total correlations belong to the easiest items. The 10 items answered correctly by more than 95% of the respondents have corrected correlations of only 0.08 to 0.22. This comes from the statistic rather than the items: a point-biserial correlation cannot be large when almost everyone gives the same answer. Among the items of medium difficulty (20% to 80% correct), the weakest is Q7 (deal = sale, corrected r = 0.10). Only 47% choose the keyed pair, and the most common wrong answer is claim + recoup (18%). Deal and sale are loose synonyms (every sale is a deal, but not every deal is a sale), so the item seems to depend more on how a respondent reads the two words than on how many words they know. Another item to watch is Q26 (annoying = obnoxious): its most common wrong answer, clicker + counter (4%), is arguably a defensible pair as well, since a hand tally counter is also called a clicker.

Dimensionality

IRT models with one latent trait assume that one ability explains the correlations between the items. Pearson correlations between 0/1 items depend on the item difficulties, so dimensionality is checked on tetrachoric correlations, which estimate the correlation between the continuous tendencies assumed to underlie each pair of right/wrong answers.

The two factors do not split the items by content: both hold synonym pairs. They split them by difficulty. The 13 items that load at least .30 on the second factor, and more than on the first, are all among the 14 easiest items of the test (88% to 99% correct), and the two factors correlate 0.53. The second factor is not a native-language effect either: among native English speakers alone the second eigenvalue is still 4.1. Whether the IRT model, which handles difficulty directly, needs a second dimension is tested with its own fit statistics below.

Item Response Theory

Three models

All three models give the probability that a person with ability \(\theta\) answers item \(j\) correctly as a logistic curve:

\[P_j(\theta) = c_j + (1 - c_j)\,\frac{1}{1 + \exp[-a_j(\theta - b_j)]}\]

  • Rasch (1PL) (Rasch, 1960): items differ only in difficulty \(b_j\), the ability at which the probability is 50%; all items share one slope and \(c_j = 0\).
  • 2PL (Birnbaum, 1968): each item also has its own discrimination \(a_j\), the steepness of the curve.
  • 3PL: adds a lower asymptote \(c_j\), the chance that someone with very low ability gets the item right. Picking one of the ten possible pairs at random is right 1 time in 10, but the test discouraged guessing. The lower asymptote is notoriously hard to estimate, so it gets a weak prior, a beta(2, 17) distribution with mean 2/19 ≈ .11, close to that chance rate (the expbeta prior in mirt).

Ability follows a normal distribution with mean 0; its SD is fixed to 1 in the 2PL and 3PL, while the Rasch model estimates it in place of the common slope. The models are fitted by marginal maximum likelihood with the EM algorithm (for the 3PL, maximum a posteriori because of the prior).

Overall fit

The likelihood-ratio test compares models with each other; whether a model fits the data at all is tested with the limited-information statistic \(M_2\) (Maydeu-Olivares & Joe, 2006), which compares the observed proportions correct of every item and every pair of items with the model’s predictions.

Local independence

If one ability explains the items, the residuals of any two items (observed minus expected score) should be uncorrelated once ability is taken into account. Yen’s (1984) Q3 is that residual correlation. Its average is slightly negative by construction, so Christensen, Makransky and Horton (2017) suggest judging each Q3 against the average and flagging values more than 0.2 above it.

Information and precision

Each item contributes information \(I_j(\theta) = a_j^2 P_j(\theta)[1 - P_j(\theta)]\), which peaks at \(\theta = b_j\) with height \(a_j^2/4\). Test information is the sum over items, and the standard error of an ability estimate is \(SE(\theta) = 1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}\). Because ability has variance 1 in this sample, a standard error of 0.30 corresponds to a reliability of about \(1 - 0.30^2 = .91\).

Differential item functioning by native language

An item shows differential item functioning (DIF) when people of equal ability but from different groups have different chances of answering it correctly. The Mantel-Haenszel procedure (Holland & Thayer, 1988) compares native and non-native speakers within each number-right score group and expresses the common odds ratio \(\alpha_{MH}\) on the ETS delta scale, \(D = -2.35 \ln \alpha_{MH}\). Negative values mean the item is harder for non-native speakers than their total score predicts. ETS classifies items as A (negligible: |D| < 1 or not significant), C (large: |D| ≥ 1.5 and significantly above 1) or B (moderate, everything in between) (Dorans & Holland, 1993).

Adaptive Testing: A Simulation

A calibrated item bank can be given adaptively. A computerised adaptive test (CAT) chooses each next item to be as informative as possible at the current ability estimate and stops once the estimate is precise enough. The CAT with IRT and EAP section of this site builds the adaptive-testing functions in base R, adapted from the catR package (Magis & Raîche, 2012). Here the dichotomous versions run on the VIQT bank, with the 2PL parameters as a 4PL bank with \(c = 0\) and \(d = 1\). Each CAT starts at θ = 0, selects items by maximum Fisher information, estimates ability by EAP (normal prior, 33 quadrature points from -4 to 4; Bock & Mislevy, 1982), and stops when the standard error reaches 0.30 or the 45 items run out.

This is a post-hoc (real-data) simulation: the CAT gives each of 300 randomly chosen respondents the items it selects, and uses the answers that person actually gave to those items. The same respondents calibrated the items, which makes the results somewhat optimistic.

## Description

Vocabulary is the classic marker of **crystallised intelligence**, the knowledge that builds up through schooling, reading and experience (Horn & Cattell, 1966). The **Vocabulary IQ Test (VIQT)** is a 45-item online test from the Open-Source Psychometrics Project. Each item lists five words, and the task is to pick the **two words with the same meaning**: in *tiny, faded, new, large, big* the answer is *large* = *big*. Respondents were told not to guess, that a wrong answer costs 0.35 points, and that they could answer *don't know* instead.

The other tutorials on this site validate self-report questionnaires, where there are no right answers. An ability test is the natural place for **item response theory (IRT)**: items differ in how hard they are and in how sharply they separate people who know a word from people who do not, and IRT puts items and people on one scale. This tutorial calibrates the 45 items with the `mirt` package (Chalmers, 2012) in **`r big(n_resp)`** respondents. It compares the Rasch, two- and three-parameter models, shows how precise the test is at each ability level, tests item fit and differential item functioning, checks the scores against education, native language and age, and finally runs the calibrated items as a simulated computerised adaptive test.

### Data, answer key and scoring

The data come from the [Open-Source Psychometrics Project](https://openpsychometrics.org/_rawdata/) (`VIQT_data.zip`, 12,173 records collected online between July 2017 and March 2018). The file holds only the people who also completed an optional survey after the test (30 personality statements and five demographic questions), so it is a self-selected group of test takers.

**Answer key.** The codebook (`codebook.txt` in the zip) gives the correct answer of every item as a number. An answer is stored as a sum over the selected words: 1 for the first word, 2 for the second, 4, 8 and 16 for the fifth, so 3 means words 1 and 2 and 24 means words 4 and 5. Decoded this way, the codebook key gives a synonym pair for every item (see the item list below). Scoring the data with it reproduces the number of right answers that the test site computed (`score_right` in the data) for all 12,173 records; [`data/prepare_viqt.R`](https://github.com/sedzinfo/vignettes/blob/main/quarto/validation/data/prepare_viqt.R) checks this before it saves the data. The key comes from the codebook, not from the data.

**Scoring.** An item scores 1 when both correct words were chosen and 0 otherwise: a wrong pair, one or three words, *don't know* or no answer at all. This is the number-right score the site reported. *Don't know* counts as wrong, not as missing, because it is the answer the instructions asked for when a respondent did not know the pair. People answer *don't know* to the items they find hard, so ignoring these answers would reward skipping hard items. The 0.35-point penalty of the site's formula score is not used. In IRT a wrong answer and a *don't know* both mean the respondent did not find the pair, and the model, not a fixed penalty, decides how much a lucky guess is worth (the three-parameter model below tests whether guessing needs its own parameter). The section [*Don't know*: wrong or missing?](#dont-know) shows how much this choice matters.

### Item statistics

The proportion correct is the classical item difficulty. The point-biserial correlation relates the item to the total score; the corrected correlation relates it to the total of the other 44 items, so the item does not correlate with itself. ## Item Response Theory

### Three models

All three models give the probability that a person with ability $\theta$ answers item $j$ correctly as a logistic curve:

$$P_j(\theta) = c_j + (1 - c_j)\,\frac{1}{1 + \exp[-a_j(\theta - b_j)]}$$

- **Rasch (1PL)** (Rasch, 1960): items differ only in **difficulty** $b_j$, the ability at which the probability is 50%; all items share one slope and $c_j = 0$.
- **2PL** (Birnbaum, 1968): each item also has its own **discrimination** $a_j$, the steepness of the curve.
- **3PL**: adds a **lower asymptote** $c_j$, the chance that someone with very low ability gets the item right. Picking one of the ten possible pairs at random is right 1 time in 10, but the test discouraged guessing. The lower asymptote is notoriously hard to estimate, so it gets a weak prior, a beta(2, 17) distribution with mean 2/19 ≈ .11, close to that chance rate (the `expbeta` prior in `mirt`).

Ability follows a normal distribution with mean 0; its SD is fixed to 1 in the 2PL and 3PL, while the Rasch model estimates it in place of the common slope. The models are fitted by marginal maximum likelihood with the EM algorithm (for the 3PL, maximum a posteriori because of the prior).

### Information and precision

Each item contributes **information** $I_j(\theta) = a_j^2 P_j(\theta)[1 - P_j(\theta)]$, which peaks at $\theta = b_j$ with height $a_j^2/4$. Test information is the sum over items, and the standard error of an ability estimate is $SE(\theta) = 1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}$. Because ability has variance 1 in this sample, a standard error of 0.30 corresponds to a reliability of about $1 - 0.30^2 = .91$.

### Differential item functioning by native language

An item shows **differential item functioning (DIF)** when people of equal ability but from different groups have different chances of answering it correctly. The Mantel-Haenszel procedure (Holland & Thayer, 1988) compares native and non-native speakers within each number-right score group and expresses the common odds ratio $\alpha_{MH}$ on the ETS delta scale, $D = -2.35 \ln \alpha_{MH}$. Negative values mean the item is harder for non-native speakers than their total score predicts. ETS classifies items as A (negligible: |D| < 1 or not significant), C (large: |D| ≥ 1.5 and significantly above 1) or B (moderate, everything in between) (Dorans & Holland, 1993).

```{r dif_mh, fig.width=8, fig.height=9, dev='png', fig.retina=2}
focal <- df_viqt$engnat[has_lang] == "No"
score <- total[has_lang]
mh <- t(sapply(items, function(j) {
  y <- resp[has_lang, j]
  A <- tapply(y == 1 & !focal, score, sum); B <- tapply(y == 0 & !focal, score, sum)
  C <- tapply(y == 1 & focal, score, sum);  D <- tapply(y == 0 & focal, score, sum)
  N <- A + B + C + D
  R <- A * D / N; S <- B * C / N; P <- (A + D) / N; Q <- (B + C) / N
  # Robins-Breslow-Greenland variance of log(alpha_MH)
  v <- sum(P * R) / (2 * sum(R)^2) + sum(P * S + Q * R) / (2 * sum(R) * sum(S)) + sum(Q * S) / (2 * sum(S)^2)
  c(D = -2.35 * log(sum(R) / sum(S)), SE = 2.35 * sqrt(v))
}))

## Adaptive Testing: A Simulation

A calibrated item bank can be given **adaptively**. A computerised adaptive test (CAT) chooses each next item to be as informative as possible at the current ability estimate and stops once the estimate is precise enough. The [CAT with IRT and EAP](https://sedzinfo.github.io/vignettes/cat_irt_eap/) section of this site builds the adaptive-testing functions in base R, adapted from the `catR` package (Magis & Raîche, 2012). Here the [dichotomous versions](https://sedzinfo.github.io/vignettes/cat_irt_eap/eap_dichotomous.html) run on the VIQT bank, with the 2PL parameters as a 4PL bank with $c = 0$ and $d = 1$. Each CAT starts at θ = 0, selects items by maximum Fisher information, estimates ability by EAP (normal prior, 33 quadrature points from -4 to 4; Bock & Mislevy, 1982), and stops when the standard error reaches 0.30 or the 45 items run out.

This is a **post-hoc (real-data) simulation**: the CAT gives each of 300 randomly chosen respondents the items it selects, and uses the answers that person actually gave to those items. The same respondents calibrated the items, which makes the results somewhat optimistic.

```{r cat}
source(paste0(directory, "R/cat_eap_common.R"))
source(paste0(directory, "R/cat_eap_dichotomous.R"))
bank <- cbind(a = pars[, "a"], b = pars[, "b"], c = 0, d = 1)

run_cat <- function(x, se_stop = 0.30) {
  given <- integer(0); est <- 0; se <- Inf
  while (se > se_stop && length(given) < nrow(bank)) {
    given <- c(given, compute_next_item_dichotomous(est, bank, out = given)$item)
    est   <- compute_eap_dichotomous(bank[given, , drop = FALSE], x[given])
    se    <- compute_eap_se_dichotomous(est, bank[given, , drop = FALSE], x[given])
  }
  c(theta = est, se = se, items = length(given))
}

set.seed(42)
sim <- sample(n_resp, 300)
cat_res <- as.data.frame(t(sapply(sim, function(i) run_cat(resp[i, ]))))
cat_res$full_mirt <- theta[sim]
cat_res$full_base <- sapply(sim, function(i) compute_eap_dichotomous(bank, resp[i, ]))
cat_res$reached   <- cat_res$se <= 0.30
cat(sprintf("Full-test EAP, base-R functions against mirt: largest difference %.4f\n",
            max(abs(cat_res$full_base - cat_res$full_mirt))))
cat(sprintf("Reached SE <= 0.30: %.0f%% of simulees; items used: median %.0f, mean %.1f\nCorrelation of CAT and full-test abilities: %.3f\n",
            100 * mean(cat_res$reached), median(cat_res$items), mean(cat_res$items),
            cor(cat_res$theta, cat_res$full_mirt)))

cat_res$band <- cut(cat_res$full_mirt, c(-Inf, -1, 0, precise[2], Inf),
                    labels = c("below -1", "-1 to 0", sprintf("0 to %.2f", precise[2]),

  data.frame(n = nrow(d), reached = 100 * mean(d$reached), median_items = median(d$items),
             mean_abs_diff = mean(abs(d$theta - d$full_mirt)))))
knitr::kable(data.frame(band = rownames(cat_tab), cat_tab), digits = c(0, 0, 0, 0, 2), row.names = FALSE,

An adaptive version of the VIQT would save most items for average and below-average respondents, but it cannot fix the shortage of hard items; that needs new items.

Rosenberg Self-Esteem Validation: One Factor or a Wording Artefact?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Description

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE; Rosenberg, 1965) is probably the most widely used self-report scale in psychology: ten statements about global self-worth, rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Five are worded positively (I feel that I have a number of good qualities) and five negatively (At times I think I am no good at all); the negative ones are reversed before scoring.

Rosenberg intended one dimension, but factor analyses of the RSE keep finding two factors that separate the positively from the negatively worded items. Is that two kinds of self-esteem, positive self-regard and self-derogation, or a wording artefact: a method effect produced by how people answer negatively worded statements (Marsh, 1996; Marsh, Scalas & Nagengast, 2010)? Schmitt and Stults (1985) showed that a negatively-keyed factor can appear when only a small share of respondents answer without noticing the reversed wording. This tutorial uses 45,284 respondents to compare the competing models and to look at where the wording factor comes from.

Data and cleaning

The data come from the Open-Source Psychometrics Project (RSE.zip, 47,974 records). Only respondents who confirmed at the end that their answers were accurate and could be used for research are included. The codebook gives the gender codes as “1=male, 3=female, 3=other”; the data contain 0-3 with 2 the most frequent, so 2 is read as female. data/prepare_rse.R applies the rules below. Giving the same answer to all ten items is excluded because it is logically inconsistent: it means agreeing (or disagreeing) equally with I am a person of worth and I am a failure.

Competing Models

All models treat the four answer categories as ordinal (polychoric correlations, WLSMV estimation).

Model Description
One factor Rosenberg’s single dimension
Two factors Positive and negative self-esteem as two correlated traits
Negative method factor One self-esteem factor plus an uncorrelated factor on the negatively worded items
Positive method factor One self-esteem factor plus an uncorrelated factor on the positively worded items
Negative method + residuals The negative method model plus residual covariances for the two most similar pairs of items, chosen by modification index
Code

models <- list(
  "One factor"               = one_factor,
  "Two factors"              = "POS =~ Q1 + Q2 + Q4 + Q6 + Q7\nNEG =~ Q3 + Q5 + Q8 + Q9 + Q10",

The two-factor model fits better than the one-factor model, but its two factors correlate 0.87: far from separate traits. A model with one self-esteem factor and a method factor on the negatively worded items fits better still, without assuming two kinds of self-esteem, and better than a model with a method factor on the positive items. RMSEA stays high for every model; with ordinal data and few degrees of freedom it is known to be inflated, and here it reflects the near-duplicate wording of Q1-Q2 and Q6-Q7 (I feel that I am a person of worth / I have a number of good qualities; I take a positive attitude toward myself / I am satisfied with myself). Allowing those two residual covariances brings CFI, TLI and SRMR well within conventional cut-offs; RMSEA (0.085) remains slightly above .08.

Every item loads strongly on the general self-esteem factor. The method factor is weak for most negatively worded items and is carried mainly by Q9 (I certainly feel useless at times.) and Q10 (At times I think I am no good at all.): the two statements about feeling bad at times, which describe occasional states rather than a global judgement of oneself. Part of the “negative factor” is therefore shared content, not only reversed wording.

Where Does the Wording Factor Come From?

Schmitt and Stults (1985) showed that respondents who do not notice the reversed wording can produce a negative-item factor on their own. Here a respondent is flagged as agreeing with both directions when the mean of their answers, as given, is at least 3 (agree) on the positive items and on the negative items: on average they agree that they are a person of worth and that they are a failure.

Code 
compare <- function(data) {
  one <- fit_cfa(models[["One factor"]], data)
  two <- fit_cfa(models[["Two factors"]], data)
  nmf <- fit_cfa(models[["Negative method factor"]], data)
  s   <- standardizedSolution(nmf)
  c(cfi_one = fitMeasures(one, "cfi.scaled"),
    r_pos_neg = lavInspect(two, "cor.lv")["POS", "NEG"],
    method_share = mean(s$est.std[s$lhs == "NM" & s$op == "=~"]^2))
}
careless_tab <- rbind("All respondents" = compare(df), "Without those agreeing with both" = compare(df[!agree_both, ]))
knitr::kable(careless_tab, digits = 3,
             col.names = c("CFI, one factor", "Correlation of positive and negative factors",
                           "Mean variance of negative items from the method factor"))
CFI, one factor Correlation of positive and negative factors Mean variance of negative items from the method factor
All respondents 0.952 0.868 0.166
Without those agreeing with both 0.962 0.899 0.133

Removing the 3.4% of respondents who agree with both directions moves the results toward a single factor: the one-factor model fits better, the positive and negative factors correlate more strongly, and the method factor explains less variance. They account for part of the wording effect, but not for all of it; the rest is consistent with Marsh’s (1996) conclusion that the negative-item factor is a stable response style rather than a second kind of self-esteem.

Self-esteem rises steadily with age in both sexes. The gender gap is largest among teenagers (13-17: women 23.0, men 26.5) and has closed by middle age (40 and over: women 28.9, men 29.0). Robins, Trzesniewski, Tracy, Gosling and Potter (2002) described the same pattern in a large internet sample: self-esteem is low in adolescence, especially among girls, and rises gradually through adulthood. (They also found a decline in old age, which this sample, with few respondents over 65, cannot show.) These are cross-sectional differences between age groups in a self-selected online sample.

```{r reliability}
  col.names = c("", "Value"), row.names = FALSE)
knitr::kable(data.frame(item = items, wording = wording, r_drop = a$item.stats$r.drop),
             digits = 2, row.names = FALSE, col.names = c("Item", "Wording", "Corrected item-total r"))
```

In a two-factor solution the second factor is defined by the negatively worded items, but not all of them equally: it rests mainly on `r paste(names(sort(L[negative, "Negative items"], decreasing = TRUE))[1:2], collapse = " and ")`, while `r cross <- negative[L[negative, "Positive items"] > L[negative, "Negative items"]]; paste(paste(cross, collapse = " and "), if (length(cross) == 1) "loads" else "load")` more on the positive-items factor. ## Competing Models

All models treat the four answer categories as ordinal (polychoric correlations, WLSMV estimation).

| Model | Description |
|---|---|
| **One factor** | Rosenberg's single dimension |
| **Two factors** | Positive and negative self-esteem as two correlated traits |
| **Negative method factor** | One self-esteem factor plus an uncorrelated factor on the negatively worded items |
| **Positive method factor** | One self-esteem factor plus an uncorrelated factor on the positively worded items |
| **Negative method + residuals** | The negative method model plus residual covariances for the two most similar pairs of items, chosen by modification index |

```{r models}
one_factor <- "SE =~ Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + Q4 + Q5 + Q6 + Q7 + Q8 + Q9 + Q10"
models <- list(
  "One factor"               = one_factor,
  "Two factors"              = "POS =~ Q1 + Q2 + Q4 + Q6 + Q7\nNEG =~ Q3 + Q5 + Q8 + Q9 + Q10",
  "Negative method factor"   = paste(one_factor, "NM =~ Q3 + Q5 + Q8 + Q9 + Q10", "SE ~~ 0*NM", sep = "\n"),
  "Positive method factor"   = paste(one_factor, "PM =~ Q1 + Q2 + Q4 + Q6 + Q7", "SE ~~ 0*PM", sep = "\n"))
fits <- lapply(models, fit_cfa)

mi <- modindices(fits[["Negative method factor"]], sort. = TRUE)
mi <- mi[mi$op == "~~", ][1:2, ]
residual_pairs <- paste(mi$lhs, "~~", mi$rhs)
models[["Negative method + residuals"]] <- paste(c(models[["Negative method factor"]], residual_pairs), collapse = "\n")
fits[["Negative method + residuals"]] <- fit_cfa(models[["Negative method + residuals"]])
cat("Residual covariances added:", paste(residual_pairs, collapse = ", "), "\n")

fit_tab <- data.frame(model = names(fits), t(sapply(fits, fitMeasures, fit_names)), row.names = NULL)
knitr::kable(fit_tab, digits = 3, row.names = FALSE,
             col.names = c("Model", "CFI", "TLI", "RMSEA", "SRMR"))
r_pn <- lavInspect(fits[["Two factors"]], "cor.lv")["POS", "NEG"]
```

The two-factor model fits better than the one-factor model, but its two factors correlate `r sprintf("%.2f", r_pn)`: far from separate traits. A model with one self-esteem factor and a method factor on the negatively worded items fits better still, without assuming two kinds of self-esteem, and better than a model with a method factor on the positive items. RMSEA stays high for every model; with ordinal data and few degrees of freedom it is known to be inflated, and here it reflects the near-duplicate wording of `r paste(sub(" ~~ ", "-", residual_pairs), collapse = " and ")` (*I feel that I am a person of worth* / *I have a number of good qualities*; *I take a positive attitude toward myself* / *I am satisfied with myself*). Allowing those two residual covariances brings CFI, TLI and SRMR well within conventional cut-offs; RMSEA (`r sprintf("%.3f", fit_tab$rmsea.scaled[fit_tab$model == "Negative method + residuals"])`) remains slightly above .08.

### Loadings of the method-factor model

```{r methodloadings, fig.width=8, fig.height=4.5, dev='png', fig.retina=2}

lam <- std[std$op == "=~", c("lhs", "rhs", "est.std")]
lam$factor <- factor(ifelse(lam$lhs == "SE", "Self-esteem", "Negative wording"),
                     levels = c("Self-esteem", "Negative wording"))
lam$rhs <- factor(lam$rhs, levels = items)
ggplot(lam, aes(x = rhs, y = est.std, fill = factor)) +
  geom_col(position = position_dodge(width = 0.8), width = 0.75) +
  geom_text(aes(label = sprintf("%.2f", est.std)), position = position_dodge(width = 0.8),
            vjust = -0.4, size = 2.8) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("Self-esteem" = "#2166ac", "Negative wording" = "#d6604d"), name = NULL) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 1)) +
  labs(x = NULL, y = "Standardised loading") +
  theme_bw(base_size = 10) + theme(legend.position = "top") +
  ggtitle("One self-esteem factor and a negative-wording method factor")
nm  <- lam[lam$factor == "Negative wording", ]
top <- as.character(nm$rhs[order(-nm$est.std)][1:2])
```

Every item loads strongly on the general self-esteem factor. The method factor is weak for most negatively worded items and is carried mainly by `r top[1]` (*`r text[[top[1]]]`*) and `r top[2]` (*`r text[[top[2]]]`*): the two statements about feeling bad **at times**, which describe occasional states rather than a global judgement of oneself. Part of the "negative factor" is therefore shared content, not only reversed wording.

## Where Does the Wording Factor Come From?

Schmitt and Stults (1985) showed that respondents who do not notice the reversed wording can produce a negative-item factor on their own. Here a respondent is flagged as **agreeing with both directions** when the mean of their answers, as given, is at least 3 (*agree*) on the positive items **and** on the negative items: on average they agree that they are a person of worth and that they are a failure.

```{r careless}
answered <- df_rse[, items]
agree_both <- rowMeans(answered[, positive]) >= 3 & rowMeans(answered[, negative]) >= 3
cat(sprintf("Respondents agreeing with both directions: %s (%.1f%%)\n",
            format(sum(agree_both), big.mark = ","), 100 * mean(agree_both)))

compare <- function(data) {
  one <- fit_cfa(models[["One factor"]], data)
  two <- fit_cfa(models[["Two factors"]], data)
  nmf <- fit_cfa(models[["Negative method factor"]], data)
  s   <- standardizedSolution(nmf)
  c(cfi_one = fitMeasures(one, "cfi.scaled"),
    r_pos_neg = lavInspect(two, "cor.lv")["POS", "NEG"],
    method_share = mean(s$est.std[s$lhs == "NM" & s$op == "=~"]^2))
}
careless_tab <- rbind("All respondents" = compare(df), "Without those agreeing with both" = compare(df[!agree_both, ]))
knitr::kable(careless_tab, digits = 3,
             col.names = c("CFI, one factor", "Correlation of positive and negative factors",
                           "Mean variance of negative items from the method factor"))
```

Removing the `r sprintf("%.1f%%", 100 * mean(agree_both))` of respondents who agree with both directions moves the results toward a single factor: the one-factor model fits better, the positive and negative factors correlate more strongly, and the method factor explains less variance. They account for part of the wording effect, but not for all of it; the rest is consistent with Marsh's (1996) conclusion that the negative-item factor is a stable response style rather than a second kind of self-esteem.

## Self-Esteem by Gender and Age

```{r gender}
mf <- df_rse$gender %in% c("Male", "Female")
g  <- droplevels(df_rse$gender[mf])
m  <- tapply(total[mf], g, mean); v <- tapply(total[mf], g, var); n <- tapply(total[mf], g, length)
d_gender <- unname((m["Male"] - m["Female"]) / sqrt(sum((n - 1) * v) / (sum(n) - 2)))
knitr::kable(data.frame(gender = names(m), n = format(as.vector(n), big.mark = ","),
                        mean = as.vector(m), sd = sqrt(as.vector(v))),
             digits = 2, row.names = FALSE, col.names = c("Gender", "Respondents", "Mean total", "SD"))
cat(sprintf("Cohen's d (men minus women): %.2f\n", d_gender))
```

Men score higher than women (*d* = `r sprintf("%.2f", d_gender)`). Kling, Hyde, Showers and Buswell's (1999) meta-analysis found a small difference in the same direction (*d* = 0.21).

```{r age, fig.width=8, fig.height=4.5, dev='png', fig.retina=2}

ggplot(age_tab, aes(x = age, y = x, colour = gender)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.5, size = 1.2) +
  geom_smooth(method = "loess", span = 0.5, se = FALSE, linewidth = 1) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(Male = "#2166ac", Female = "#d6604d"), name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Age", y = "Mean total score (10-40)") +
  theme_bw(base_size = 10) + theme(legend.position = "top") +
  ggtitle("Self-esteem by age and gender", subtitle = "Ages with at least 30 respondents of that gender; lines: loess")
young <- tapply(total[mf & df_rse$age <= 17], g[df_rse$age[mf] <= 17], mean)
adult <- tapply(total[mf & df_rse$age >= 40], g[df_rse$age[mf] >= 40], mean)
```

Self-esteem rises steadily with age in both sexes. The gender gap is largest among teenagers (13-17: women `r sprintf("%.1f", young["Female"])`, men `r sprintf("%.1f", young["Male"])`) and has closed by middle age (40 and over: women `r sprintf("%.1f", adult["Female"])`, men `r sprintf("%.1f", adult["Male"])`). Robins, Trzesniewski, Tracy, Gosling and Potter (2002) described the same pattern in a large internet sample: self-esteem is low in adolescence, especially among girls, and rises gradually through adulthood. (They also found a decline in old age, which this sample, with few respondents over 65, cannot show.) These are cross-sectional differences between age groups in a self-selected online sample.

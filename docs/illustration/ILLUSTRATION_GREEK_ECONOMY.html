What is this?

What does the Greek economy live on? This story follows the money through the economy and across its borders, and the food through its farms, seas and ports:

  1. What does Greece produce?
  2. Who buys it?
  3. How much does Greece sell abroad, and how much does it buy?
  4. What does it sell and buy, and with whom?
  5. Can Greece feed itself?

The data come from three places:

  • Eurostat, the statistical office of the EU: the national accounts, which split the economy into what each industry produces and into who spends the money, and the balance of payments, which records what Greece sells and buys abroad in services, such as tourism and shipping.
  • Comext, Eurostat’s database of trade in goods, which records every good that crosses the borders of an EU country: what it is, where it comes from or goes to, and what it is worth.
  • FAOSTAT, the statistics of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the UN, whose food balance sheets weigh, for each food, what a country produces, imports, exports and eats.

They are downloaded when the document is knitted, so the charts show the latest figures.

# Comext, Eurostat's database of trade in goods, from its own API (the eurostat package does not
# read it): a JSON-stat dataset, its values in one vector in the order of its dimensions, the last
# changing fastest
comext <- function(filters, id = "DS-045409") {
  query <- paste(paste0(rep(names(filters), lengths(filters)), "=", unlist(filters)), collapse = "&")
  x <- jsonlite::fromJSON(paste0("https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/api/comext/dissemination/statistics/1.0/data/", id, "?", query))
  codes <- lapply(x$id, function(d) names(sort(unlist(x$dimension[[d]]$category$index))))
  grid <- expand.grid(rev(codes), stringsAsFactors = FALSE)   # the first dimension changes fastest
  names(grid) <- rev(x$id)
  grid$values <- NA
  grid$values[as.integer(names(x$value)) + 1] <- unlist(x$value)
  grid <- grid[!is.na(grid$values), ]
  grid$time <- as.integer(grid$time)
  grid
}

# what Greece produces of a food, % of what it uses (production / domestic supply): its self-sufficiency
self_sufficiency <- function(code, year = fao_year) {
  produced <- balance(code, "Production", year)
  100 * (if (is.na(produced)) 0 else produced) / balance(code, "Domestic supply quantity", year)
}

# horizontal bars of two series side by side for each label, the labels from the top in their order
paired_bars <- function(labels, first, second, names, colors, hover) {
  y <- factor(labels, levels = rev(labels))

    # plotly draws the first series at the bottom of each pair, so the second goes in first

}

Of the rest, Greece imports far more machinery, electrical equipment and cars than it exports, and sells aluminium, medicines and food: fruit, preserved fruit and vegetables, cheese, fish and olive oil.

So, would Greece go hungry without imports? No, but it would eat differently. Greece has about 3,380 calories of food a day for each person who lives there: food available, not all of it eaten. So, what does it mean?

The Greek economy is one of services, consumption and imports.

  1. Services: trade, transport, hotels and restaurants make 26% of the economy, against 19% in the EU; manufacturing 11%, against 16%.
  2. Consumption: households spend 69% of GDP, the EU’s 53%; investment is 17%, the EU’s 22%.
  3. Trade: Greece sells tourism and shipping, and buys goods: €22.5 billion more services sold than bought, €34.1 billion more goods bought than sold in 2025. Its largest trade in goods is oil, bought crude and sold refined.
  4. Food: about as much sold as bought, in money. Greece produces far more olive oil, fruit and vegetables than it eats, and depends on imports for cereals, meat, fish, sugar and animal feed.

## What is this?

What does the Greek economy live on? This story follows the money through the economy and across its borders, and the food through its farms, seas and ports:

1. **What does Greece produce?**
2. **Who buys it?**
3. **How much does Greece sell abroad, and how much does it buy?**
4. **What does it sell and buy, and with whom?**
5. **Can Greece feed itself?**

The data come from three places:

- **Eurostat**, the statistical office of the EU: the national accounts, which split the economy into what each industry produces and into who spends the money, and the balance of payments, which records what Greece sells and buys abroad in services, such as tourism and shipping.
- **Comext**, Eurostat's database of trade in goods, which records every good that crosses the borders of an EU country: what it is, where it comes from or goes to, and what it is worth.
- **FAOSTAT**, the statistics of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the UN, whose *food balance sheets* weigh, for each food, what a country produces, imports, exports and eats.

They are downloaded when the document is knitted, so the charts show the latest figures.

Milk is the least certain of these figures. **So, would Greece go hungry without imports?** No, but it would eat differently. Greece has about `r format(round(kcal_total, -1), big.mark = ",")` calories of food a day for each person who lives there: food available, not all of it eaten. And the figure flatters Greece, as its pigs and chickens eat feed made of imported soybeans and maize. For a country that sells olive oil, fruit and feta and buys meat, cereals and feed, what matters is to sell enough food to pay for the food it lacks, and in `r trade_year` Greece's food exports were worth `r percent(100 * sum(food$exports) / sum(food$imports))` of its food imports.

## So, what does it mean?

**The Greek economy is one of services, consumption and imports.**

1. **Services**: trade, transport, hotels and restaurants make `r percent(greece[["G-I"]])` of the economy, against `r percent(eu[["G-I"]])` in the EU; manufacturing `r percent(greece[["C"]])`, against `r percent(eu[["C"]])`.
2. **Consumption**: households spend `r percent(account("P31_S14_S15"))` of GDP, the EU's `r percent(account("P31_S14_S15", "EU27_2020"))`; investment is `r percent(account("P5G"))`, the EU's `r percent(account("P5G", "EU27_2020"))`.
3. **Trade**: Greece sells tourism and shipping, and buys goods: `r billion(services_gap)` more services sold than bought, `r billion(goods_gap)` more goods bought than sold in `r gdp_year`. Its largest trade in goods is oil, bought crude and sold refined.
4. **Food**: about as much sold as bought, in money. Greece produces far more olive oil, fruit and vegetables than it eats, and depends on imports for cereals, meat, fish, sugar and animal feed.

Where the World Burns: Wildfires on Every Continent Since 2002

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Wildfires on every continent in satellite data: how much of Africa, Europe and the rest of the world burns each year, whether the world burns more than it used to, what burns, when each continent burns, and the worst years.
Published

November 26, 2026

What is this?

Every summer the fires of California, Canada, Australia or Greece make the news. They are a small part of the land that burns in the world: most of it burns in Africa, every year, in dry seasons that seldom make the news at all. This story compares the continents and asks five questions:

  1. Where in the world does land burn?
  2. Is the world burning more than it used to?
  3. What burns: forest, savanna, grassland or fields?
  4. When does each continent burn?
  5. Which were the worst years?

Greece Burning looks at one country close up; this page looks at the whole world.

The data come from the Global Wildfire Information System (GWIS), a joint initiative of the Group on Earth Observations and the EU’s Copernicus programme, funded mainly by the European Commission (GWIS). Our World in Data adds up its figures by continent (Wildfires), and they are downloaded from there when the page is rendered:

  • The land burnt each year since 2002 in every country, mapped by NASA’s MODIS sensors on the Terra and Aqua satellites, which find land that has just burnt in their images (the product MCD64A1, Giglio et al. 2018), with the kind of land that burnt: forest, savanna, shrubland and grassland, cropland or other land (GWIS country profiles).
  • The land burnt each week since 2012, from MODIS and the finer VIIRS sensors, and the carbon dioxide that the fires released each year since 2003 (GWIS seasonal trends).
  • The land area of each country, from the World Bank (land area), and the continent it is in (continents).

A hectare is a square of 100 metres by 100, so a million hectares are 10,000 square kilometres.

The satellites count every fire. They map the land that burnt, whatever started the fire: a wildfire, a farmer burning the stubble of a field, or a fire set on purpose to clear dry grass. And they miss some: small fires, and fires hidden by cloud or by the trees above them (Our World in Data).

by_group <- function(x, time = "year") {
  values <- setdiff(names(x), c("entity", "code", time))
  europe <- x[x$entity == "Europe", ]
  russia <- x[x$entity == "Russia", ]
  russia <- russia[match(europe[[time]], russia[[time]]), ]
  rest <- europe
  rest[values] <- europe[values] - russia[values]
  rest$entity <- "Europe without Russia"
  others <- c("Africa", "Asia", "North America", "Oceania", "South America", "Russia")
  y <- rbind(x[x$entity %in% others, ], rest)
  names(y)[names(y) == "entity"] <- "group"
  y
}

# a rank test of a trend over the years: the chance, in 100, of a pattern as strong as this one
# without a trend
chance <- function(x, years) {
  p <- cor.test(years, x, method = "kendall", exact = FALSE)$p.value
  if (p < 0.01) "less than 1" else as.character(round(100 * p))
}

The continents are those of Our World in Data, with Russia on its own. Our World in Data counts all of Russia in Europe. But most of Russia is in Asia, and Russia burns 5 times as much land as the rest of Europe put together: as part of Europe, it would hide everything else.

The weekly figures, and the carbon dioxide, stop at 2024. From 2012 to 2024, the weekly figures of a year added up to between 93% and 120% of the yearly MODIS figure for the world; in 2025, to 81%. From April to September 2025 they add up to 141 million hectares, against 200 to 261 in the same months of the years before, while the yearly MODIS figure for 2025, 333 million hectares, is within the range of the years before it (331 to 454). The weekly figures may have changed the way they are made; until the two series agree again, this page uses them, and the carbon dioxide that comes with them, only up to 2024.

2. Is the world burning more than it used to?

Less, by about a fifth. In 2002–2006 fires burnt 450 million hectares a year, in 2021–2025 353: 22% less. And the fall is not just a matter of a few good years at the end: a rank test of all the years finds it too (the chance of a pattern as strong as this one without a trend is less than 1 in 100).

This is not new. Satellites found the same from 1998 to 2015: the land burnt in the world fell by about a quarter, most of all in the savannas, and mainly because farming spread and grew more intensive (Andela et al. 2017).

So, what does it mean?

  1. Most of the world’s fire is African. Africa has 67% of the land that burns; the land it burns in a year is as large as 9% of the continent. Europe without Russia burns 0.4% of its land a year.
  2. The world burns less than it did: 22% less in 2021–2025 than in 2002–2006, 63% of the fall in Africa.
  3. Forest is a small part of what burns, and it is not clearly burning less. In North America more of it burns.
  4. Every continent burns in its own dry months, and Africa has two seasons, one on each side of the equator.
  5. The worst years come where fire is rarer. North America released 2.8 times its average carbon dioxide in 2023; Africa’s worst year, only 1.2 times.

What this does not show. The satellites map the land that burnt, not what started the fire, and they miss small fires and fires under cloud or trees; the fires here include those that people set on purpose. A continent is large, and its average hides countries that burn far more or far less. The carbon dioxide is an estimate, from GWIS’s figures up to 2024. And hectares do not count the people, the homes and the animals lost in the fires, or the smoke that people breathe far from them.

## What is this?

Every summer the fires of California, Canada, Australia or Greece make the news. They are a small part of the land that burns in the world: most of it burns in Africa, every year, in dry seasons that seldom make the news at all. This story compares the continents and asks five questions:

1. **Where in the world does land burn?**
2. **Is the world burning more than it used to?**
3. **What burns: forest, savanna, grassland or fields?**
4. **When does each continent burn?**
5. **Which were the worst years?**

[Greece Burning](ILLUSTRATION_GREEK_WILDFIRES.html) looks at one country close up; this page looks at the whole world.

The data come from the **Global Wildfire Information System** (GWIS), a joint initiative of the Group on Earth Observations and the EU's Copernicus programme, funded mainly by the European Commission ([GWIS](https://gwis.jrc.ec.europa.eu/)). Our World in Data adds up its figures by continent ([Wildfires](https://ourworldindata.org/wildfires)), and they are downloaded from there when the page is rendered:

- **The land burnt each year since 2002 in every country**, mapped by NASA's MODIS sensors on the Terra and Aqua satellites, which find land that has just burnt in their images (the product MCD64A1, [Giglio et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2018.08.005)), with the kind of land that burnt: forest, savanna, shrubland and grassland, cropland or other land ([GWIS country profiles](https://gwis.jrc.ec.europa.eu/apps/country.profile/downloads)).
- **The land burnt each week since 2012**, from MODIS and the finer VIIRS sensors, and the **carbon dioxide** that the fires released each year since 2003 ([GWIS seasonal trends](https://gwis.jrc.ec.europa.eu/apps/gwis.statistics/seasonaltrend)).
- **The land area of each country**, from the World Bank ([land area](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/land-area-km)), and the continent it is in ([continents](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/continents-according-to-our-world-in-data)).

A **hectare** is a square of 100 metres by 100, so a million hectares are 10,000 square kilometres.

**The satellites count every fire.** They map the land that burnt, whatever started the fire: a wildfire, a farmer burning the stubble of a field, or a fire set on purpose to clear dry grass. And they miss some: small fires, and fires hidden by cloud or by the trees above them ([Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/annual-area-burnt-by-wildfires-gwis)).

  values <- setdiff(names(x), c("entity", "code", time))

leaders <- function(g, n = 2) {
  x <- countries_weekly[countries_weekly$group == g & month_of(countries_weekly$w) %in% months_of(g), ]
  s <- sort(tapply(x$area_ha, x$entity, sum), decreasing = TRUE)
  and_list(paste0(with_the(names(s)[1:n]), " (", percent(100 * s[1:n] / sum(s)), ")"))
}
# the share of a country's burnt land in some months
in_months <- function(name, months) {
  x <- countries_weekly[countries_weekly$entity == name, ]
  percent(100 * sum(x$area_ha[month_of(x$w) %in% months]) / sum(x$area_ha))
}

## So, what does it mean?

1. **Most of the world's fire is African.** Africa has `r percent(in_group("Africa", "world_share"))` of the land that burns; the land it burns in a year is as large as `r percent(in_group("Africa", "land_share"))` of the continent. Europe without Russia burns `r percent(in_group("Europe without Russia", "land_share"), 1)` of its land a year.
2. **The world burns less than it did**: `r percent(-of_change("World", "change"))` less in `r min(last_five)`–`r max(last_five)` than in `r min(first_five)`–`r max(first_five)`, `r percent(africa_part)` of the fall in Africa.
3. **Forest is a small part of what burns, and it is not clearly burning less.** In North America more of it burns.
4. **Every continent burns in its own dry months**, and Africa has two seasons, one on each side of the equator.
5. **The worst years come where fire is rarer.** North America released `r one(of_worst("North America", "times"))` times its average carbon dioxide in 2023; Africa's worst year, only `r one(of_worst("Africa", "times"))` times.

**What this does not show.** The satellites map the land that burnt, not what started the fire, and they miss small fires and fires under cloud or trees; the fires here include those that people set on purpose. A continent is large, and its average hides countries that burn far more or far less. The carbon dioxide is an estimate, from GWIS's figures up to `r last_season`. And hectares do not count the people, the homes and the animals lost in the fires, or the smoke that people breathe far from them.

For one country close up, see [Greece Burning](ILLUSTRATION_GREEK_WILDFIRES.html); for the heat that dries out the land, [Athens Is Getting Hotter](ILLUSTRATION_ATHENS_HEAT.html); for the temperature of the world, the [NASA GISS Global Temperature Anomaly](ILLUSTRATION_CLIMATE_HIGHCHARTER.html).

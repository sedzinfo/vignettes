The Greeks Who Left: How Many, Where To, and Who Came Back

Emigration from Greece since the debt crisis, in Eurostat data: how many left, who they were, where they went, who came back, and whether it was a brain drain.
Published

November 27, 2026

What is this?

From 2010 until August 2018, Greece lived on rescue loans, through three programmes (ESM). In those years many people left the country. This story asks five questions about them:

  1. How many people left Greece, and when?
  2. Who left? Their age and sex, and whether they were Greek citizens or foreigners.
  3. Where did they go?
  4. Did they come back?
  5. Was it a brain drain? Did those who left have more education than those who stayed?

Fewer and Older shows how births, deaths and migration change the size and the age of Greece’s population. This story follows the people who left.

The data come from Eurostat, the statistical office of the EU, and are downloaded when the page is rendered:

  • Greece’s figures on the people who leave the country and those who arrive, each year, from ELSTAT, the Hellenic Statistical Authority: by age and sex (migr_emi2), by citizenship (migr_emi1ctz, migr_imm1ctz), by country of birth (migr_imm3ctb) and by where they went (migr_emi5nxt); with the population (demo_pjan) and unemployment (une_rt_a).
  • The figures of the countries they moved to: the Greek citizens who arrive in each European country and those who leave it (migr_imm1ctz, migr_emi1ctz), and those who live there (migr_pop1ctz).
  • The Labour Force Survey, a survey of households in the countries of the EU and EFTA, which asks people where they were born and what education they have (lfst_lmbpcobed, edat_lfs_9912).

Most of these figures are estimates. In the statistics of the EU, an emigrant is someone who moves abroad for at least 12 months (Eurostat). Greece does not count them: nobody has to tell the authorities that they are leaving, so ELSTAT calls its data on emigration “essentially non-existent” and estimates it instead. For 2011 to 2021, emigration is the number of people who must have left for the population, as revised after the census of 2021, to add up with the births, the deaths and the people who arrived. Since 2022, it is worked out from the number of people that Germany counts arriving from Greece (ELSTAT). The countries the emigrants go to count the people who arrive, but not all in the same way: Norway, for example, counts as migrants the people who move for six months (Eurostat).

# a Eurostat dataset, without its missing values, in the order of the years
eurostat_values<-function(id,filters) {
  x<-get_eurostat(id,filters=filters,time_format="num",cache=FALSE)
  x<-as.data.frame(x[!is.na(x$values),])
  result<-x[order(x$time),]
  return(result)
}
# the years that Eurostat flags as a break in one series (flag b); the eurostat package leaves the
# flags out when it filters, so they come from Eurostat's own API, in JSON-stat
breaks_in<-function(id,filters) {
  api<-"https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/api/dissemination/statistics/1.0/data/"
  query<-paste(paste0(names(filters),"=",unlist(filters)),collapse="&")
  x<-jsonlite::fromJSON(paste0(api,id,"?",query))
  years<-names(sort(unlist(x$dimension$time$category$index)))
  flags<-unlist(x$status)
  result<-as.numeric(years[as.integer(names(flags)[flags=="b"])+1])
  return(result)
}

About 41,000 a year in the 2000s; more than 100,000 a year in 2011–2019.

From 2000 to 2009, between 37,433 and 46,993 people left Greece each year. Then came the crisis: 62,041 left in 2010, 106,398 in 2011 and 135,073 in 2012, the most since the figures start in 1991: 12.2 of every 1,000 people who lived in Greece. In the years of the rescue loans, from 2010 to 2018, 1,018,000 people left, 527,000 of them Greek citizens.

The number has fallen every year since 2018, to 78,014 in 2024, 1.9 times the average of the 2000s. It rose with unemployment, which went from 9.8% of the labour force in 2009 to 27.8% in 2013; both have fallen since, unemployment to 8.9% in 2025 (Eurostat, une_rt_a).

# the age of a Eurostat age code: Y_LT1 is under one, Y_OPEN and Y_GE100 are the oldest, and the
# codes of totals, such as TOTAL, give NA
age_of<-function(code) {
  age<-suppressWarnings(as.numeric(sub("^Y","",code)))
  age[code=="Y_LT1"]<-0
  age[code%in%c("Y_OPEN","Y_GE100")]<-100
  result<-age
  return(result)
}
group_names<-c("0–14","15–19","20–24","25–29","30–34","35–39","40–44","45–49","50–54","55–59",
               "60–64","65+")
age_group<-function(code) {
  result<-cut(age_of(code),c(-1,14,19,24,29,34,39,44,49,54,59,64,Inf),labels=group_names)
  return(result)
}
# the people who left out of every 1,000 of the same sex and age group who lived in Greece on
# 1 January
rates<-function(year,sex) {
  out<-emigration[emigration$time==year&emigration$sex==sex,]
  living<-population[population$time==year&population$sex==sex,]
  result<-1000*tapply(out$values,age_group(out$age),sum)/
          tapply(living$values,age_group(living$age),sum)
  return(result)
}
rate<-function(year,sex,group) {
  result<-rates(year,sex)[[group]]
  return(result)
}
# the age that half of the people are younger than or as old as
median_age<-function(x) {
  x<-x[!is.na(age_of(x$age)),]
  x<-x[order(age_of(x$age)),]
  result<-age_of(x$age)[which(cumsum(x$values)>=sum(x$values)/2)[1]]
  return(result)
}

Germany counted 153,000 Greek citizens arriving, 51% of all those counted in Europe, and the Netherlands 37,000, 12%. The United Kingdom has figures only for 2015, when 12,148 Greek citizens arrived there, the most after Germany; Eurostat’s figures for the United Kingdom stop with its withdrawal from the EU (Eurostat).

The other 247,000, 45% of those who left, were not counted arriving in any European country. They may have gone to countries outside Europe; to European countries that have no figures on Greek citizens arriving, Cyprus, Ireland, Malta, Poland, Portugal and Spain; or to countries in years without figures, such as the United Kingdom after 2015. And part of the gap is the difference between Greece’s estimate and the counts of the other countries. The figures cannot tell how much of each.

What the data cannot tell:

  • Who left when. The survey counts the people who live abroad, whenever they left, not those who left in a given year.
  • Where they studied. It does not say whether the degree was earned in Greece or abroad.
  • All the countries. It covers the EU and EFTA countries: not the United Kingdom, which is left out of this series, nor the countries outside Europe.
  • Exact numbers. The survey asks a sample of households, so its figures move from year to year more than the people do.
  • What it cost. No statistic here says whether those who left would have found work in Greece, or what their education cost the country.

So, what does it mean?

  1. The crisis drove a wave of emigration. By ELSTAT’s estimates, 1,018,000 people left Greece in the years of the rescue loans, 527,000 of them Greek citizens. In 2012, 12.2 of every 1,000 people who lived in Greece left.
  2. They were young, and many were men. In 2012, 44 of every 1,000 men aged 25 to 29 left.
  3. Germany took the most: 51% of the Greek citizens counted arriving in Europe from 2014 to 2024. For 45% of those who left, the figures do not say where they went.
  4. The flow has turned, a little. Since 2023, more Greek citizens come to Greece than leave it, and in 2024 more Greek citizens left Germany than moved there. But 29,012 more Greek citizens in 2023 and 2024 make up only 9% of the 328,000 lost from 2010 to 2022.
  5. It was partly a brain drain. The people born in Greece with higher education who live elsewhere in the EU and EFTA are 2.8 times as many as in 2010, and those abroad now have more education than those at home.

Most of these numbers are estimates: Greece does not count the people who leave, and the countries they go to count them in their own ways. The direction is clear; the exact numbers are not.

## What is this?

From 2010 until August 2018, Greece lived on rescue loans, through three programmes ([ESM](https://www.esm.europa.eu/assistance/greece/greece-successfully-concludes-esm-programme)). In those years many people left the country. This story asks five questions about them:

1. **How many people left Greece, and when?**
2. **Who left?** Their age and sex, and whether they were Greek citizens or foreigners.
3. **Where did they go?**
4. **Did they come back?**
5. **Was it a brain drain?** Did those who left have more education than those who stayed?

[Fewer and Older](ILLUSTRATION_GREEK_POPULATION.Rmd) shows how births, deaths and migration change the size and the age of Greece's population. This story follows the people who left.

The data come from **Eurostat**, the statistical office of the EU, and are downloaded when the page is rendered:

- **Greece's figures** on the people who leave the country and those who arrive, each year, from ELSTAT, the Hellenic Statistical Authority: by age and sex (migr_emi2), by citizenship (migr_emi1ctz, migr_imm1ctz), by country of birth (migr_imm3ctb) and by where they went (migr_emi5nxt); with the population (demo_pjan) and unemployment (une_rt_a).
- **The figures of the countries they moved to**: the Greek citizens who arrive in each European country and those who leave it (migr_imm1ctz, migr_emi1ctz), and those who live there (migr_pop1ctz).
- **The Labour Force Survey**, a survey of households in the countries of the EU and EFTA, which asks people where they were born and what education they have (lfst_lmbpcobed, edat_lfs_9912).

**Most of these figures are estimates.** In the statistics of the EU, an emigrant is someone who moves abroad for at least 12 months ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/migr_immi_esms.htm)). Greece does not count them: nobody has to tell the authorities that they are leaving, so ELSTAT calls its data on emigration "essentially non-existent" and estimates it instead. For 2011 to 2021, emigration is the number of people who must have left for the population, as revised after the census of 2021, to add up with the births, the deaths and the people who arrived. Since 2022, it is worked out from the number of people that Germany counts arriving from Greece ([ELSTAT](https://www.statistics.gr/en/statistics/-/publication/SPO15/-)). The countries the emigrants go to count the people who arrive, but not all in the same way: Norway, for example, counts as migrants the people who move for six months ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/migr_immi_esms.htm)).

```{r brain_numbers}
working_age<-born_abroad[born_abroad$age=="Y15-64"&born_abroad$time>=2005,]
known<-working_age[working_age$isced11%in%names(education_names),]
share<-aggregate(values~time,known,sum)
names(share)<-c("year","known")
share$abroad<-100*by_year(known[known$isced11=="ED5-8",],share$year)/share$known
share$home<-by_year(born_home[born_home$isced11=="ED5-8",],share$year)
share<-share[!is.na(share$home),]
behind<-share$year[share$abroad<=share$home]
ahead_since<-max(behind)+1
first_share<-min(share$year)
last_share<-max(share$year)
higher<-function(where,year) {
  result<-share[[where]][share$year==year]
  return(result)
}
```

**Partly. The people born in Greece who live elsewhere in Europe used to have less education than those at home; now they have more, and many more of them have a degree.**

No statistic follows the people who left, so none can say exactly how much education they had. The nearest is Eurostat's Labour Force Survey, which counts the people born in Greece, aged 20 to 64, who live in another EU or EFTA country, by the highest level of education they finished.

From `r rescue[1]` to `r edu_latest`, the number of them with higher education, a university degree or similar, rose from about `r thousands(1000*level("ED5-8",rescue[1]))` to `r thousands(1000*level("ED5-8",edu_latest))`: `r one(level("ED5-8",edu_latest)/level("ED5-8",rescue[1]))` times as many. The number with at most lower secondary schooling hardly changed: about `r thousands(1000*level("ED0-2",rescue[1]))` in `r rescue[1]` and `r thousands(1000*level("ED0-2",edu_latest))` in `r edu_latest`.

**What the data cannot tell:**

- **Who left when.** The survey counts the people who live abroad, whenever they left, not those who left in a given year.
- **Where they studied.** It does not say whether the degree was earned in Greece or abroad.
- **All the countries.** It covers the EU and EFTA countries: not the United Kingdom, which is left out of this series, nor the countries outside Europe.
- **Exact numbers.** The survey asks a sample of households, so its figures move from year to year more than the people do.
- **What it cost.** No statistic here says whether those who left would have found work in Greece, or what their education cost the country.

## So, what does it mean?

1. **The crisis drove a wave of emigration.** By ELSTAT's estimates, `r thousands(left_rescue)` people left Greece in the years of the rescue loans, `r thousands(left_greek_rescue)` of them Greek citizens. In `r peak_year`, `r one(per_1000(peak_year))` of every 1,000 people who lived in Greece left.
2. **They were young, and many were men.** In `r peak_year`, `r round(rate(peak_year,"M","25–29"))` of every 1,000 men aged 25 to 29 left.
3. **Germany took the most**: `r percent(share_of_counted("DE"))` of the Greek citizens counted arriving in Europe from `r since` to `r latest`. For `r percent(100*not_counted/left_window)` of those who left, the figures do not say where they went.
4. **The flow has turned, a little.** Since `r turn`, more Greek citizens come to Greece than leave it`r if(left_germany_first) paste0(", and in ",latest," more Greek citizens left Germany than moved there") else ""`. But `r number(gained)` more Greek citizens in `r and_list(after_turn)` make up only `r percent(100*gained/lost)` of the `r thousands(lost)` lost from `r rescue[1]` to `r turn-1`.
5. **It was partly a brain drain.** The people born in Greece with higher education who live elsewhere in the EU and EFTA are `r one(level("ED5-8",edu_latest)/level("ED5-8",rescue[1]))` times as many as in `r rescue[1]`, and those abroad now have more education than those at home.

Most of these numbers are estimates: Greece does not count the people who leave, and the countries they go to count them in their own ways. The direction is clear; the exact numbers are not.

What is this?

Greece has had fewer people every year since 2011. The official projections say that the fall will go on for the rest of the century, and that the people will be older. This story asks what that means for the economy, and for the public debt that the economy has to carry:

  1. How many people will Greece have?
  2. Why is the population falling?
  3. How old will it be?
  4. Who will work, and how much will they produce?
  5. What will pensions and health care cost?
  6. What happens to the debt?

The data come from two places:

  • Eurostat, the statistical office of the EU: the population since 1960, births, deaths, migration, employment, productivity and the debt, and EUROPOP2023, its projections of the population of every EU country to 2100. They are downloaded when the document is knitted, so the charts show the latest figures.
  • The European Commission’s 2024 Ageing Report, which projects what pensions, health care, long-term care and education will cost in each EU country until 2070, with the same population projections, and how fast each economy can grow. The report is a PDF, so its figures are typed in below, with the table they come from.

A projection is not a forecast. EUROPOP2023 starts from the population of 2022 and shows what happens if births, deaths and migration follow the paths that Eurostat assumed in 2023. Besides this baseline, it has variants, with more or fewer migrants, none at all, fewer births or longer lives, which show how much the result depends on these assumptions.

# the age of a Eurostat age code: Y_LT1 is under one, Y_OPEN and Y_GE100 are the oldest,
# and the codes of totals, such as TOTAL, give NA
age_of <- function(code) {
  age <- suppressWarnings(as.numeric(sub("^Y", "", code)))
  age[code == "Y_LT1"] <- 0
  age[code %in% c("Y_OPEN", "Y_GE100")] <- 100
  age
}
# the people of each age group by year, from a dataset by single year of age
by_age_group <- function(x) {
  x$age <- age_of(x$age)
  x <- x[!is.na(x$age), ]
  x$group <- cut(x$age, c(-1, 19, 64, 79, Inf), labels = names(groups))
  counts <- aggregate(values ~ time + group, x, sum)
  wide <- reshape(counts, idvar = "time", timevar = "group", direction = "wide")
  names(wide) <- sub("^values\\.", "", names(wide))
  names(wide)[names(wide) == "time"] <- "year"
  wide$total <- rowSums(wide[names(groups)])
  wide[order(wide$year), ]
}

1. How many people will Greece have?

Fewer every year: about 9.0 million in 2050 and 7.6 million in 2075, against 10.4 million today.

The population of Greece grew from 8.30 million in 1960 to 11.12 million in 2011. Since then it has fallen, to 10.37 million on 1 January 2026. In the baseline projection of Eurostat it keeps falling: to 8.96 million in 2050, 7.60 million in 2075 and 7.28 million in 2100, fewer than in 1960.

2. Why is the population falling?

Fewer children

A population keeps its size without migration when each woman has about 2.1 children. Greek women had 2.23 children each in 1980, and fewer than 2.1 in every year since 1981. # GDP and the debt ratio year by year, from the last year of the debt to 2070. GDP grows with the
# number of people aged 20 to 64 who work and with what each of them produces (the people at work
# over 64 are left out); section 6 explains the debt.
debt_now <- debt("GD", "PC_GDP")
start_rate <- debt("D41PAY", "MIO_EUR") / debt("GD", "MIO_EUR", debt_year - 1)   # interest paid / debt a year earlier
rate_to <- 0.03       # the average interest rate on the debt in ten years
inflation <- 0.02     # a year
surplus <- 2.5        # the primary surplus, % of GDP
project <- function(productivity = 0.01, variant = "BSL", employment_to = rate_eu, shrink = TRUE, extra_pensions = FALSE) {
  years <- debt_year:2070
  people <- future[future$projection == variant, ]
  working <- people$working[match(years, people$year)]
  if (!shrink) working[] <- working[1]
  in_work <- working * rate_path(years, employment_to)
  real_growth <- in_work[-1] / in_work[-length(in_work)] * (1 + productivity) - 1
  growth <- (1 + real_growth) * (1 + inflation) - 1
  k <- seq_along(growth)
  interest <- start_rate + (rate_to - start_rate) * pmin(1, k / 10)
  primary <- rep(surplus, length(k))
  if (extra_pensions) primary <- primary - approx(constant_benefit$year, constant_benefit$extra, years[-1])$y
  ratio <- debt_now
  for (j in k) ratio[j + 1] <- ratio[j] * (1 + interest[j]) / (1 + growth[j]) - primary[j]
  gdp <- 100 * cumprod(c(1, 1 + real_growth))
  population <- people$total[match(years, people$year)]
  data.frame(year = years, gdp = gdp, gdp_person = gdp * population[1] / population, debt_gdp = ratio)
}

Greece spends more on pensions than almost any EU country: 14.5% of GDP in 2022, against 11.4% in the EU. Health care costs more as people grow older, but the report expects only a small rise in Greece: from 5.4% of GDP in 2022 to 6.0% in 2050. On paper, the cost of ageing is under control in Greece; in practice, that depends on pensions falling behind wages for decades.

6. What happens to the debt?

The debt ratio of next year is this year’s, grown by the interest rate and shrunk by the growth of GDP in euros, minus the primary surplus, the budget before interest:

\[d_{t} = d_{t-1} \times \frac{1 + i_t}{1 + g_t} - pb_t\]

The growth of GDP in euros is the growth of the number of workers, plus the growth of what each of them produces, plus inflation. A shrinking workforce takes something off it every year, so the debt shrinks more slowly against GDP.

The scenarios start from the debt of 2025, 145.4% of GDP, and assume, unless stated otherwise:

  • the people aged 20 to 64 of the baseline projection, of whom 71% work in 2025, rising to the EU’s 76% in ten years;
  • output per worker growing by 1% a year;
  • inflation of 2% a year, the target of the European Central Bank;
  • an average interest rate on the debt rising from 2.1% to 3% in ten years, as cheap loans are replaced by new borrowing at market rates;
  • a primary surplus of 2.5% of GDP every year, as in the steady scenario of the story on the Greek debt.

They are illustrations of how the population affects the debt, not forecasts.

So, what does it mean?

Greece will still be here in 2075, smaller and older: about 7.6 million people in the baseline projection, fewer than in 1960, and 33% of them aged 65 or over. A smaller population is not by itself a disaster: what matters for the economy and for the debt is how many people work, and what they produce.

  1. Productivity matters most. Output per worker has to grow by about 1.1% a year just to keep GDP from shrinking, and it has not grown since 2000. Between flat productivity and the 1.6% that the Commission assumes lie 61 points of GDP of debt in 2050.
  2. More people in work: fewer Greek women and fewer people over 55 work than in the rest of the EU, which leaves room to grow.
  3. Migration has already given Greece 100,000 more people than projected, and of all the variants of the projection it is the one that changes the population most.
  4. Pensions: the reforms keep the cost of ageing down by letting pensions fall behind wages. Undoing that would cost up to 4.0 points of GDP a year.
  5. Births take the longest: a child born today starts working in about twenty years, so more births would add workers only from the late 2040s.

The debt can keep falling while the population shrinks, but more slowly, and only if productivity grows: the one thing Greece has not managed since 2000.

## What is this?

Greece has had fewer people every year since 2011. The official projections say that the fall will go on for the rest of the century, and that the people will be older. This story asks what that means for the economy, and for the public debt that the economy has to carry:

1. **How many people will Greece have?**
2. **Why is the population falling?**
3. **How old will it be?**
4. **Who will work, and how much will they produce?**
5. **What will pensions and health care cost?**
6. **What happens to the debt?**

The data come from two places:

- **Eurostat**, the statistical office of the EU: the population since 1960, births, deaths, migration, employment, productivity and the debt, and **EUROPOP2023**, its projections of the population of every EU country to 2100. They are downloaded when the document is knitted, so the charts show the latest figures.
- **The European Commission's *2024 Ageing Report***, which projects what pensions, health care, long-term care and education will cost in each EU country until 2070, with the same population projections, and how fast each economy can grow. The report is a PDF, so its figures are typed in below, with the table they come from.

**A projection is not a forecast.** EUROPOP2023 starts from the population of 2022 and shows what happens if births, deaths and migration follow the paths that Eurostat assumed in 2023. Besides this *baseline*, it has *variants*, with more or fewer migrants, none at all, fewer births or longer lives, which show how much the result depends on these assumptions.

## 4. Who will work, and how much will they produce?

What an economy produces is the number of people who work, times what each of them produces. Greece has a problem with both.

### What it means for the economy

```{r scenarios}
# GDP and the debt ratio year by year, from the last year of the debt to 2070. GDP grows with the
# number of people aged 20 to 64 who work and with what each of them produces (the people at work
# over 64 are left out); section 6 explains the debt.
debt_now <- debt("GD", "PC_GDP")
start_rate <- debt("D41PAY", "MIO_EUR") / debt("GD", "MIO_EUR", debt_year - 1)   # interest paid / debt a year earlier
rate_to <- 0.03       # the average interest rate on the debt in ten years
inflation <- 0.02     # a year
surplus <- 2.5        # the primary surplus, % of GDP
project <- function(productivity = 0.01, variant = "BSL", employment_to = rate_eu, shrink = TRUE, extra_pensions = FALSE) {
  years <- debt_year:2070
  people <- future[future$projection == variant, ]
  working <- people$working[match(years, people$year)]
  if (!shrink) working[] <- working[1]
  in_work <- working * rate_path(years, employment_to)
  real_growth <- in_work[-1] / in_work[-length(in_work)] * (1 + productivity) - 1
  growth <- (1 + real_growth) * (1 + inflation) - 1
  k <- seq_along(growth)
  interest <- start_rate + (rate_to - start_rate) * pmin(1, k / 10)
  primary <- rep(surplus, length(k))
  if (extra_pensions) primary <- primary - approx(constant_benefit$year, constant_benefit$extra, years[-1])$y
  ratio <- debt_now
  for (j in k) ratio[j + 1] <- ratio[j] * (1 + interest[j]) / (1 + growth[j]) - primary[j]
  gdp <- 100 * cumprod(c(1, 1 + real_growth))
  population <- people$total[match(years, people$year)]
  data.frame(year = years, gdp = gdp, gdp_person = gdp * population[1] / population, debt_gdp = ratio)
}
scenarios <- list(

  no_migration = list(variant = "NMIGR"),
  rate_stays = list(employment_to = rate_now),
  rate_high = list(employment_to = rate_high),
  pensions = list(extra_pensions = TRUE)
)

  no_migration = "No migration (EUROPOP2023 variant)",

# the scenarios of the charts

# the growth of productivity that keeps GDP as it is until 2050, at an employment rate, % a year

  100 * (ratio^(-1 / (2050 - job_year)) - 1)
}
```

To keep the economy from shrinking, what each worker produces has to grow as fast as the number of workers falls: by `r format(round(needed(rate_eu), 1), nsmall = 1)`% a year until 2050 if the employment rate reaches the EU's, by `r format(round(needed(rate_now), 1), nsmall = 1)`% if it stays as it is. The European Commission assumes, in its Ageing Report, that productivity in Greece will grow by 1.6% a year until 2070, faster than in the EU (1.4%), while the hours worked fall by 0.6% a year, so that GDP grows by 1.1% a year, against 1.3% in the EU. All of Greece's growth would come from productivity, growing for decades at a pace that it reached only in the years before 2008.

## 5. What will pensions and health care cost?

Greece spends more on pensions than almost any EU country: `r percent(ar("pensions", 2022), 1)` of GDP in 2022, against `r percent(ar("pensions_eu", 2022), 1)` in the EU. With many more older people for each person of working age, one would expect that to rise steeply. The Commission projects that it will not: `r percent(ar("pensions", 2050), 1)` of GDP in 2050 and `r percent(ar("pensions", 2070), 1)` in 2070, the largest fall in the EU.

Why? The Greek pension fiche of the report breaks the change from 2022 to 2070 down (Table 8):

- **Ageing** alone would add 7.7 points of GDP.
- **Smaller pensions against wages** take 6.6 points off. Pensions rise with prices, while wages rise with prices and productivity, so the average pension falls from `r percent(ar("benefit_ratio", 2022), 1)` of the average wage in 2022 to `r percent(ar("benefit_ratio", 2050), 1)` in 2050 and `r percent(ar("benefit_ratio", 2070), 1)` in 2070 (in the EU, from `r percent(ar("benefit_ratio_eu", 2022), 1)` to `r percent(ar("benefit_ratio_eu", 2070), 1)`).

With `r million(ahead("oldest", 2050))` people aged 80 and over in 2050, against `r million(age_past("oldest"))` in `r ages_now`, and fewer children for each of them, that will be harder.

Altogether, the report puts what Greece spends on pensions, health care, long-term care and education at `r percent(ar("total", 2022), 1)` of GDP in 2022 and `r percent(ar("total", 2070), 1)` in 2070, while in the EU it rises from `r percent(ar("total_eu", 2022), 1)` to `r percent(ar("total_eu", 2070), 1)`. **On paper, the cost of ageing is under control in Greece; in practice, that depends on pensions falling behind wages for decades.**

## 6. What happens to the debt?

The debt ratio of next year is this year's, grown by the interest rate and shrunk by the growth of GDP in euros, minus the primary surplus, the budget before interest:

$$d_{t} = d_{t-1} \times \frac{1 + i_t}{1 + g_t} - pb_t$$

The growth of GDP in euros is the growth of the number of workers, plus the growth of what each of them produces, plus inflation. A shrinking workforce takes something off it every year, so the debt shrinks more slowly against GDP.

The scenarios start from the debt of `r debt_year`, `r round(debt_now, 1)`% of GDP, and assume, unless stated otherwise:

- the people aged 20 to 64 of the baseline projection, of whom `r percent(rate_now)` work in `r job_year`, rising to the EU's `r percent(rate_eu)` in ten years;
- output per worker growing by 1% a year;
- inflation of 2% a year, the target of the European Central Bank;
- an average interest rate on the debt rising from `r percent(100 * start_rate, 1)` to 3% in ten years, as cheap loans are replaced by new borrowing at market rates;
- a primary surplus of `r surplus`% of GDP every year, as in the steady scenario of the story on the Greek debt.

**They are illustrations of how the population affects the debt, not forecasts.**

## So, what does it mean?

**Greece will still be here in 2075, smaller and older**: about `r million(ahead("total", 2075), 1)` people in the baseline projection, `r if (ahead("total", 2075) < past("population", 1960)) "fewer than in 1960" else "about as many as in 1960"`, and `r percent(100 * aged_65(2075) / ahead("total", 2075))` of them aged 65 or over. A smaller population is not by itself a disaster: what matters for the economy and for the debt is how many people work, and what they produce.

1. **Productivity** matters most. Output per worker has to grow by about `r format(round(needed(rate_eu), 1), nsmall = 1)`% a year just to keep GDP from shrinking, and it has not grown since 2000. Between flat productivity and the 1.6% that the Commission assumes lie `r debt_in("flat") - debt_in("fast")` points of GDP of debt in 2050.
2. **More people in work**: fewer Greek women and fewer people over 55 work than in the rest of the EU, which leaves room to grow.
3. **Migration** has already given Greece `r thousands(compare$difference[4])` more people than projected, and of all the variants of the projection it is the one that changes the population most.
4. **Pensions**: the reforms keep the cost of ageing down by letting pensions fall behind wages. Undoing that would cost up to `r one(max(constant_benefit$extra))` points of GDP a year.
5. **Births** take the longest: a child born today starts working in about twenty years, so more births would add workers only from the late 2040s.

The debt can keep falling while the population shrinks, but more slowly, and only if productivity grows: the one thing Greece has not managed since 2000.

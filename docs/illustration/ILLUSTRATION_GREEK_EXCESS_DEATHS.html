How Many More Died? Excess Deaths in Greece Since 2015

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Weekly deaths in Greece in Eurostat data, against the deaths a normal year would bring: the pandemic, the heatwaves, who died, how Greece compares with the EU, and why fewer people have died since 2024.

What is this?

Counting the deaths of a disease is hard: doctors must decide what each person died of, and countries decide it in different ways. Counting all deaths is easy, and every EU country reports them to Eurostat week by week. The deaths above what a normal week would bring are the excess deaths: they include the deaths of the disease that nobody recorded as such, and the deaths that a heatwave or an overwhelmed hospital caused.

This story compares the deaths of every week in Greece since 2015 with the deaths a normal year would have brought, and answers seven questions:

  1. How many people die in Greece in a normal week?
  2. How many more died during the pandemic?
  3. Which weeks were the worst?
  4. Does the heat kill too?
  5. Who died?
  6. How does Greece compare with the EU?
  7. What came after the pandemic?

The deaths come from Eurostat, the temperatures of Athens from ERA5-Land, a reconstruction of the weather of every day by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. Both are downloaded when the page is rendered, so a new render brings in the latest weeks.

# a Eurostat dataset, without its missing values; the time as text: "2024-W05", "2024-05"
eurostat_values<-function(id,filters) {
  x<-get_eurostat(id,filters=filters,time_format="raw",cache=FALSE)
  result<-as.data.frame(x[!is.na(x$values),])
  result$time<-as.character(result$time)
  return(result)
}
# the Monday that an ISO week ("2024-W05") starts on: week 1 is the week with 4 January in it
week_monday<-function(time) {
  january_4<-as.Date(paste0(substr(time,1,4),"-01-04"))
  result<-january_4-(as.integer(format(january_4,"%u"))-1)+7*(as.integer(substr(time,7,8))-1)
  return(result)
}

The deaths a normal year would bring

A normal week has more deaths in winter, when flu and the cold strike, than in summer, and the deaths of a normal year grow slowly as the population gets older. So the expected deaths of each week come from a model of the five years before the pandemic, 2015–2019: each week of the year has its own level, and every year adds the same share to all of them. It is a Poisson regression, the usual model for counts, with a dispersion that is allowed to be larger than Poisson’s. The years 53 weeks long count their last week as week 52.

Code 
# the expected deaths of every week, from the weeks of 2015-2019, and how far a normal week may
# stray from them: twice the standard deviation of the model
expect<-function(x) {
  x$season<-factor(pmin(x$week,52))
  before<-x[x$year<=2019,]
  model<-glm(deaths~year+season,family=quasipoisson,data=before)
  x$expected<-predict(model,newdata=x,type="response")
  spread<-2*sqrt(summary(model)$dispersion*x$expected)
  x$low<-x$expected-spread
  x$high<-x$expected+spread
  x$excess<-x$deaths-x$expected
  x$percent<-100*x$excess/x$expected
  result<-list(weeks=x,growth=100*(exp(coef(model)[["year"]])-1))
  return(result)
}
fitted<-expect(weekly)
weekly<-fitted$weeks

1. How many people die in Greece in a normal week?

About 2,340. In 2015–2019 Greece recorded 121,669 deaths in a year of 52 weeks. The worst weeks of a normal year come in winter, with flu and the cold: for a week of January the model expects about 3,020 deaths, for a week of September about 2,020. And as the population ages, the expected deaths grow by about 0.8% a year.

2. How many more died during the pandemic?

About 38,400 in 2020–2022, 10% more than a normal three years would have brought. The worst year was 2021, with 18,380 deaths more than expected (+15%). In the chart, the gray band is the range in which a normal week falls; the red line is what happened.

4. Does the heat kill too?

Yes. Every dot of the chart is a week of June to September since 2015: how hot it was in Athens, where more than a third of the people of Greece live, and how many more people died in all of Greece than expected.

5. Who died?

The same model, fitted to each age group, gives the excess deaths of 2020–2022 by age. The youngest groups are put together, as they have few deaths.

Code 
groups<-list(`Under 40`=c("Y_LT10","Y10-19","Y20-29","Y30-39"),`40–49`="Y40-49",
             `50–59`="Y50-59",`60–69`="Y60-69",`70–79`="Y70-79",`80–89`="Y80-89",
             `90 and over`="Y_GE90")
ages<-do.call(rbind,lapply(names(groups),function(group) {
  parts<-lapply(groups[[group]],function(code) {
    x<-by_age[by_age$age==code,c("time","values")]
    names(x)[2]<-"deaths"
    x$year<-as.integer(substr(x$time,1,4))
    x$week<-as.integer(substr(x$time,7,8))
    expect(x)$weeks
  })
  y<-do.call(rbind,parts)
  y<-y[y$year%in%2020:2022,]
  data.frame(age=group,deaths=sum(y$deaths),expected=sum(y$expected))
}))

In numbers, most of the excess deaths were of old people: 25,775 of the 35,606 were of people aged 70 or more. But against what was expected for each age, the pandemic years hit people aged 60 to 79 hardest (+18%), more than those aged 80 or more (+6%), whose deaths are many in any year. The groups add up to a little less than the total of question 2, as each group has a model of its own and a few deaths have no age.

6. How does Greece compare with the EU?

Eurostat publishes its own measure for every country: the deaths of each month against the average of the same month in 2016–2019. It has no trend for the ageing of the population, so it counts a little more excess than the model above. Averaged over the 36 months of 2020–2022:

7. What came after the pandemic?

In 2025, 121,566 people died in Greece, against 121,669 in an average year of 2016–2019: about as many, although the population is older. Eurostat’s measure, which compares each month with 2016–2019 and has no trend, averaged -0.1% for Greece in 2025. The model above expects the deaths to grow by 0.8% a year as the population ages, so it counts 7,646 deaths fewer than expected (-5.9%).

Two things can keep the deaths of the years after a pandemic below the trend:

  • Some of the deaths came early. The pandemic and the heatwaves took the lives of many people who were already very old or very ill. Some of them would have died in the years that followed; as they died earlier, fewer people die in those years. Demographers call this mortality displacement.
  • The trend of 2015–2019 may not hold. The model lets the expected deaths grow by 0.8% a year, as they did before the pandemic. If people now live longer than that trend assumes, the expected deaths are too high, and part of the gap is the model’s error, not fewer deaths.

### The deaths a normal year would bring

A normal week has more deaths in winter, when flu and the cold strike, than in summer, and the deaths of a normal year grow slowly as the population gets older. So the expected deaths of each week come from a model of the five years before the pandemic, 2015–2019: each week of the year has its own level, and every year adds the same share to all of them. It is a Poisson regression, the usual model for counts, with a dispersion that is allowed to be larger than Poisson's. The years 53 weeks long count their last week as week 52.

```{r expected}
# the expected deaths of every week, from the weeks of 2015-2019, and how far a normal week may
# stray from them: twice the standard deviation of the model
expect<-function(x) {
  x$season<-factor(pmin(x$week,52))
  before<-x[x$year<=2019,]
  model<-glm(deaths~year+season,family=quasipoisson,data=before)
  x$expected<-predict(model,newdata=x,type="response")
  spread<-2*sqrt(summary(model)$dispersion*x$expected)
  x$low<-x$expected-spread
  x$high<-x$expected+spread
  x$excess<-x$deaths-x$expected
  x$percent<-100*x$excess/x$expected
  result<-list(weeks=x,growth=100*(exp(coef(model)[["year"]])-1))
  return(result)
}
fitted<-expect(weekly)
weekly<-fitted$weeks
# the deaths of every year: what was expected, what happened, and the difference
years<-data.frame(year=sort(unique(weekly$year)))
years$weeks<-as.vector(table(weekly$year)[as.character(years$year)])
years$deaths<-as.vector(tapply(weekly$deaths,weekly$year,sum))
years$expected<-as.vector(tapply(weekly$expected,weekly$year,sum))
years$excess<-years$deaths-years$expected
years$percent<-100*years$excess/years$expected
at<-function(column,year) {
  result<-years[[column]][years$year==year]
  return(result)
}
pandemic<-years[years$year%in%2020:2022,]
last_full<-max(years$year[years$weeks>=52])
# the weeks with the most and the fewest expected deaths in 2019
normal<-weekly[weekly$year==2019,]
peak<-normal[which.max(normal$expected),]
trough<-normal[which.min(normal$expected),]
# the deaths of the last full year against an average year of 2016-2019, % (all 52 weeks long),
# and Eurostat's measure for the same year
before_average<-mean(years$deaths[years$year%in%2016:2019])
after_change<-100*(at("deaths",last_full)/before_average-1)
eurostat_last<-mean(eurostat_excess$values[eurostat_excess$geo=="EL"&
                                             substr(eurostat_excess$time,1,4)==last_full])
```

## 7. What came after the pandemic?

**In `r last_full`, `r number(at("deaths",last_full))` people died in Greece, against `r number(before_average)` in an average year of 2016–2019: `r if(abs(after_change)<2) "about as many" else if(after_change<0) paste(percent(-after_change),"fewer") else paste(percent(after_change),"more")`, although the population is older.** Eurostat's measure, which compares each month with 2016–2019 and has no trend, averaged `r percent(eurostat_last,1,sign=TRUE)` for Greece in `r last_full`. The model above expects the deaths to grow by `r percent(fitted$growth,1)` a year as the population ages, so it counts `r if(at("excess",last_full)<0) paste(number(-at("excess",last_full)),"deaths fewer") else paste(number(at("excess",last_full)),"deaths more")` than expected (`r percent(at("percent",last_full),1,sign=TRUE)`).

Two things can keep the deaths of the years after a pandemic below the trend:

- **Some of the deaths came early.** The pandemic and the heatwaves took the lives of many people who were already very old or very ill. Some of them would have died in the years that followed; as they died earlier, fewer people die in those years. Demographers call this *mortality displacement*.
- **The trend of 2015–2019 may not hold.** The model lets the expected deaths grow by `r percent(fitted$growth,1)` a year, as they did before the pandemic. If people now live longer than that trend assumes, the expected deaths are too high, and part of the gap is the model's error, not fewer deaths.

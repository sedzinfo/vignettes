Greece Burning: How Much, When and Where Wildfires Burn

Wildfires in Greece in official and satellite data: the area burnt each year since 1980, the worst years, the fire season, a map of every large fire since 2016, and the neighbours.
Published

November 26, 2026

What is this?

In August 2023 a fire near Alexandroupolis burnt more than 96,000 hectares, the largest single fire in Europe since the 1980s (JRC, 10 April 2024). A hectare is a square of 100 metres by 100; that fire burnt more than 960 square kilometres. Was it a sign that Greece burns more than it used to? This story asks five questions:

  1. Is Greece burning more than it used to?
  2. Which were the worst years, and why?
  3. Is the fire season getting longer?
  4. Where do the fires burn?
  5. How does Greece compare with its neighbours?

The data come from three places, besides the boundaries of the regions from GISCO and the area of each country from Eurostat (reg_area3):

  • National statistics: the area burnt and the number of forest fires in each country, every year since 1980, which the countries report and the Joint Research Centre (JRC) of the European Commission collects (country totals). Those of Greece count forest land, “wooded and non-wooded” (JRC 2025).
  • EFFIS, the European Forest Fire Information System, run by the JRC: satellites outline the land that burns. It has the area burnt in each country every year since 2006, in fires of about 30 hectares or more (statistics portal), and the outline of every fire mapped in Greece since 2016, at first of about 30 hectares or more and, since 2020, smaller ones too (rapid damage assessment).
  • NASA FIRMS: the hot spots that two satellites, Terra and Aqua, have seen in Greece since 2003 (FIRMS, with one file for each country and year).

They do not count the same things. The national statistics count forest land; the satellites outline all the land that burnt, farmland included, but miss the small fires. In 2007, for example, Greece counted 225,734 hectares of forest fires, and another 88,093 in fires outside forest land, 313,826 in all (JRC 2008); EFFIS mapped about 272,000 hectares, in fires of about 30 hectares or more. Where the two disagree, the page says which one it shows.

Years of heat, drought or wind, when a few fires became huge. In the official record, the five worst years were:

  • 2007, the worst year for forest fires ever recorded in Greece: 225,734 hectares of forest land. Three heatwaves in a row, with more than 46 °C in some places, a long drought and strong winds; at the end of August five fires in the Peloponnese burnt 170,000 hectares and two in Evia 25,000. 69 civilians, 9 seasonal firefighters and 2 pilots died (JRC 2008).
  • 2000: 145,033 hectares, nearly three times the average of the two decades before (European Commission 2001).
  • 2023: 136,499 hectares, after the warmest July in 64 years and a heatwave from 12 to 26 July. In July there were fires in Attica, Rhodes, Evia, Corfu and near Volos, and from the middle of August in Evros and Rodopi. 23 civilians and one firefighter died (JRC 2024).
  • 1988: 110,501 hectares.
  • 2021: 108,418 hectares, in a summer that approached 46 °C, with the most notable heatwave, in length, for 35 years. Most of the large fires came between 3 and 11 August, among them those of Attica, Evia, Eastern Mani and Ancient Olympia (JRC 2022).

The deadliest fire was not among the largest. In July 2018 a fire of just over 1,000 hectares at Mati, in Attica, killed 100 people, in a year that was “relatively light” for the area burnt (JRC 2019).

A big-fire day here is one on which the fires gave off 2,000 megawatts or more. In 2003–2013 there were 7.4 of them a year, in 2015–2025 8.2; 6% and 4% of them came outside the months from June to September. The first big-fire day of the year came on 29 June in 2003–2013 and 12 July in 2015–2025, and the last on 27 August and 5 September (medians of the years). Nothing in these numbers says that the season has become longer.

That is not the last word. The authors of Greece’s chapter in the JRC’s report for 2025 write that climate change has “systematically extended the fire season” (JRC 2026), and hot days do come earlier and last later, at least in Athens, as Athens Is Getting Hotter shows. But the hot spots show what did burn, not how much could have: in years without a spark in a dangerous week, or with firefighters who put a fire out in time, a longer dangerous season leaves no trace in them. There are other limits too:

  • A hot spot is not a fire. It is a square about a kilometre across in which the satellite saw heat (NASA’s frequently asked questions): a big fire makes many, and farm burning and other sources of heat make some too.
  • The satellites see a fire only when they pass over, without cloud. Their orbits have changed as well: since 2020 NASA has not been able to keep Terra’s passing time from drifting earlier (NASA), so the two periods were not seen at exactly the same hours.
  • 23 years are few, and the fires of a year depend on the weather of a few weeks.

So, what does it mean?

  1. Greece does not burn steadily more, but it burns in bursts. Five years, 1988, 2000, 2007, 2021 and 2023, have 36% of everything that burnt from 1980 to 2024, and there is no trend in the total.
  2. A bad year is made of a few enormous fires. The ten largest fires made up 46% of the area of the 1,793 fires outlined since 2016.
  3. The satellites see no longer season of big fires, though the heat that makes a fire dangerous does last longer, and the Greek authors of the JRC’s report say that the season has.
  4. Attica burns the most for its size: more than a fifth of its land burnt at least once since 2016.
  5. Greece is second to Portugal. Every year it burns about 0.38% of its land on average, against 0.19% in Spain and 0.19% in Italy.

What this does not show. It does not say what started the fires, how well they were fought, or what the climate will do to the next ones. The national statistics and the satellites do not count the same things, the satellites miss the small fires, and the outlines show where a fire burnt, not how severely. And the fires are counted in hectares here, which do not measure the people, the homes and the animals lost in them.

## What is this?

In August 2023 a fire near Alexandroupolis burnt more than 96,000 hectares, the largest single fire in Europe since the 1980s ([JRC, 10 April 2024](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/jrc-news-and-updates/wildfires-2023-among-worst-eu-century-2024-04-10_en)). A hectare is a square of 100 metres by 100; that fire burnt more than 960 square kilometres. Was it a sign that Greece burns more than it used to? This story asks five questions:

1. **Is Greece burning more than it used to?**
2. **Which were the worst years, and why?**
3. **Is the fire season getting longer?**
4. **Where do the fires burn?**
5. **How does Greece compare with its neighbours?**

The data come from three places, besides the [boundaries of the regions](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/gisco/geodata/statistical-units/territorial-units-statistics) from GISCO and the area of each country from Eurostat ([reg_area3](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/reg_area3/default/table?lang=en)):

- **National statistics:** the area burnt and the number of forest fires in each country, every year since 1980, which the countries report and the Joint Research Centre (JRC) of the European Commission collects ([country totals](https://forest-fire.emergency.copernicus.eu/applications/data-and-services)). Those of Greece count forest land, "wooded and non-wooded" ([JRC 2025](https://doi.org/10.2760/0649290)).
- **EFFIS**, the European Forest Fire Information System, run by the JRC: satellites outline the land that burns. It has the area burnt in each country every year since 2006, in fires of about 30 hectares or more ([statistics portal](https://forest-fire.emergency.copernicus.eu/apps/effis.statistics)), and the outline of every fire mapped in Greece since 2016, at first of about 30 hectares or more and, since 2020, smaller ones too ([rapid damage assessment](https://forest-fire.emergency.copernicus.eu/about-effis/technical-background/rapid-damage-assessment)).
- **NASA FIRMS**: the hot spots that two satellites, Terra and Aqua, have seen in Greece since 2003 ([FIRMS](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/firms), with [one file for each country and year](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/country/)).

**They do not count the same things.** The national statistics count forest land; the satellites outline all the land that burnt, farmland included, but miss the small fires. In 2007, for example, Greece counted 225,734 hectares of forest fires, and another 88,093 in fires outside forest land, 313,826 in all ([JRC 2008](https://doi.org/10.2788/76413)); EFFIS mapped about 272,000 hectares, in fires of about 30 hectares or more. Where the two disagree, the page says which one it shows.

## 3. Is the fire season getting longer?

```{r season_days}

Nothing in these numbers says that the season has become longer.

That is not the last word. The authors of Greece's chapter in the JRC's report for 2025 write that climate change has "systematically extended the fire season" ([JRC 2026](https://doi.org/10.2760/3888324)), and hot days do come earlier and last later, at least in Athens, as [Athens Is Getting Hotter](ILLUSTRATION_ATHENS_HEAT.html) shows. But the hot spots show what did burn, not how much could have: in years without a spark in a dangerous week, or with firefighters who put a fire out in time, a longer dangerous season leaves no trace in them. There are other limits too:

- **A hot spot is not a fire.** It is a square about a kilometre across in which the satellite saw heat ([NASA's frequently asked questions](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/firms/faq)): a big fire makes many, and farm burning and other sources of heat make some too.
- **The satellites see a fire only when they pass over, without cloud.** Their orbits have changed as well: since 2020 NASA has not been able to keep Terra's passing time from drifting earlier ([NASA](https://terra.nasa.gov/about/terra-orbital-drift-information)), so the two periods were not seen at exactly the same hours.
- **`r length(hot_spot_years)` years are few**, and the fires of a year depend on the weather of a few weeks.

## So, what does it mean?

1. **Greece does not burn steadily more, but it burns in bursts.** Five years, `r and_list(sort(worst))`, have `r percent(100 * sum(national$area[national$year %in% worst]) / sum(national$area))` of everything that burnt from 1980 to `r last_total`, and there is no trend in the total.
2. **A bad year is made of a few enormous fires.** The ten largest fires made up `r percent(100 * sum(largest$area) / sum(fires$area))` of the area of the `r number(nrow(fires))` fires outlined since `r first_year`.
3. **The satellites see no longer season of big fires**, though the heat that makes a fire dangerous does last longer, and the Greek authors of the JRC's report say that the season has.
4. **Attica burns the most for its size**: more than a fifth of its land burnt at least once since `r first_year`.
5. **Greece is second to Portugal.** Every year it burns about `r two(in_country("Greece", "mean"))`% of its land on average, against `r two(in_country("Spain", "mean"))`% in Spain and `r two(in_country("Italy", "mean"))`% in Italy.

**What this does not show.** It does not say what started the fires, how well they were fought, or what the climate will do to the next ones. The national statistics and the satellites do not count the same things, the satellites miss the small fires, and the outlines show where a fire burnt, not how severely. And the fires are counted in hectares here, which do not measure the people, the homes and the animals lost in them.

For the heat that dries out the land, see [Athens Is Getting Hotter](ILLUSTRATION_ATHENS_HEAT.html); for the temperature of the world, the [NASA GISS Global Temperature Anomaly](ILLUSTRATION_CLIMATE_HIGHCHARTER.html).

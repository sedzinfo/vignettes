Is the Sea Rising at Piraeus? Sea Level in Greece, the Mediterranean and the World

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
The tide gauges of the Greek harbours since 1969 and the satellites since 1993: how much the sea has risen at Piraeus, how Greece compares with the Mediterranean and the world, why the land matters, and whether the rise is speeding up.

What is this?

The sea rises as the world warms: water expands as it gets warmer, and the melting glaciers and ice sheets of the land pour more of it into the oceans. This story asks what that means for Greece, a country of more than 13,000 kilometres of coast, with six questions:

  1. How is the height of the sea measured?
  2. Is the sea rising at Piraeus?
  3. How does Piraeus compare with the Mediterranean and the world?
  4. Is the sea rising everywhere in Greece?
  5. Is the rise speeding up?
  6. How much higher could the sea be by 2100?

The data come from the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level (PSMSL), which keeps the records of the tide gauges of the world, and from the Laboratory for Satellite Altimetry of NOAA, the weather and oceans agency of the United States. Both are downloaded when the page is rendered.

# the lines of a file on the web, tried again a few times: the servers sometimes refuse a connection
# or take too long to answer
read_lines<-function(url,tries=4) {
  for(k in seq_len(tries)) {
    result<-tryCatch(readLines(url,warn=FALSE),error=function(e) NULL)
    if(!is.null(result))
      return(result)
    Sys.sleep(10)
  }
  stop("Could not download ",url)
}

# a tide gauge of the PSMSL: the mean sea level of every month, in millimetres above a fixed mark of
# the gauge. Missing months are -99999; months that the PSMSL flags for attention are left out
gauge_months<-function(id) {
  url<-paste0("https://psmsl.org/data/obtaining/rlr.monthly.data/",id,".rlrdata")
  x<-read.table(text=read_lines(url),sep=";",strip.white=TRUE,
                col.names=c("time","mm","missing_days","flags"),
                colClasses=c("numeric","numeric","integer","character"))
  result<-x[x$mm>-99999&x$flags=="000",]
  result$year<-floor(result$time)
  return(result)
}
# the mean of every year with at least ten good months
gauge_years<-function(months) {
  if(nrow(months)==0)
    return(data.frame(year=integer(0),mm=numeric(0)))
  counts<-table(months$year)
  result<-aggregate(mm~year,months,mean)
  result<-result[counts[as.character(result$year)]>=10,]
  return(result)
}
# the rise of a straight line through the yearly means, mm a year
rise<-function(x,from=-Inf,to=Inf) {
  x<-x[x$year>=from&x$year<=to,]
  result<-unname(coef(lm(mm~year,x))[2])
  return(result)
}

# NOAA's satellite record of the mean sea level since the end of 1992, with the seasons taken out,
# mm: a column for each satellite, a row for each pass over the ocean
altimetry<-function(region) {
  url<-paste0("https://www.star.nesdis.noaa.gov/socd/lsa/SeaLevelRise/slr/slr_sla_",region,
              "_free_ref_90.csv")
  x<-read.csv(text=read_lines(url),comment.char="#")
  passes<-rowSums(!is.na(x[-1]))>0
  result<-data.frame(time=x$year[passes],mm=rowSums(x[passes,-1],na.rm=TRUE))
  result$year<-floor(result$time)
  return(result)
}
world_passes<-altimetry("gbl")
mediterranean_passes<-altimetry("mds")
# the mean of every year that the satellites saw from beginning to end
satellite_years<-function(passes) {
  span<-tapply(passes$time,passes$year,function(t) max(t)-min(t))
  result<-aggregate(mm~year,passes,mean)
  result<-result[span[as.character(result$year)]>0.9,]
  return(result)
}
world<-satellite_years(world_passes)
mediterranean<-satellite_years(mediterranean_passes)
last_satellite<-max(world$year)
# the rise since 2000 at Piraeus, in the Mediterranean and in the world, over the same years
rates<-c(Piraeus=rise(piraeus,from,last_satellite),Mediterranean=rise(mediterranean,from),
         World=rise(world,from))
# a comparison in words: "faster than", "about as fast as" or "slower than"
compared<-function(a,b) {
  result<-if(abs(a-b)<0.5) "about as fast as" else if(a>b) "faster than" else "slower than"
  return(result)
}
# the same for two amounts: "more than", "about the same as" or "less than"
compared_amount<-function(a,b) {
  result<-if(abs(a-b)<0.5) "about the same as" else if(a>b) "more than" else "less than"
  return(result)
}

# yearly means in centimetres above their own average of 2000-2019, so that a tide gauge and the
# satellites can share a chart
rebase<-function(x) {
  result<-data.frame(year=x$year,cm=(x$mm-mean(x$mm[x$year>=2000&x$year<=2019]))/10)
  return(result)
}
# every year from the first to the last, with NA where a year is missing, so the line breaks there
all_years<-function(x) {
  result<-merge(data.frame(year=min(x$year):max(x$year)),x,all.x=TRUE)
  return(result)
}
# short labels of numbers
mm_year<-function(x) {
  result<-paste0(format(round(x,1),nsmall=1,trim=TRUE)," mm a year")
  return(result)
}

1. How is the height of the sea measured?

With tide gauges since the 19th century, and with satellites since 1992. A tide gauge sits in a harbour and measures the height of the water against a fixed mark on land, every few minutes; the average of a month or a year smooths out the tides, the waves and the weather. Since the end of 1992 satellites have also measured the height of the whole ocean, by timing a radar pulse from the satellite to the sea and back.

The two measure different things. A satellite measures the sea against the centre of the Earth. A tide gauge measures the sea against the land it stands on, and in Greece the land moves: earthquakes lift it or drop it, and soft ground under some cities sinks. For the people of a harbour, the tide gauge measures what matters.

2. Is the sea rising at Piraeus?

Yes, since about 2000; before that it hardly rose at all. The tide gauge of Piraeus has measured the sea since 1969. From 1969 to 1988, when its record runs with few gaps, a straight line through its yearly means falls by 0.8 mm a year. From 2000 to 2025 it rises by 3.3 mm a year. The years in between are left out of both lines: several are missing, and some look doubtful (the PSMSL flags all the months of 1990, as the weather of that year disturbed the gauge). In all, the sea at Piraeus stood 2.9 cm higher in 2016–2025 than in 1969–1988.

A single harbour moves up and down more from year to year than the average of a whole sea, with the winds, the air pressure and the currents of each year; that is why Piraeus zigzags around the smoother lines of the satellites. And the tide gauge measures the sea against the land: the land of Piraeus seems to have moved little.

At all 13 harbours the sea rose against the land; the median rise was 3.2 mm a year, about the same as the 2.9 mm a year of the Mediterranean as a whole. The differences come mostly from the land. Greece lies where the African plate dives under the Aegean, and it is one of the most active corners of Europe for earthquakes (see Where the Earth Shakes).

# the acceleration of the world's rise: a parabola through all the passes, mm a year, every year
acceleration<-2*unname(coef(lm(mm~time+I(time^2),world_passes))[3])

The world’s seas now rise by about 3.9 mm a year, against 2.8 mm a year in the 1990s. A parabola through all the passes of the satellites gives an acceleration of about 0.09 mm a year, every year. The Mediterranean moves more from decade to decade than the whole ocean, so its last ten years say less about the long run; but they too show a sea that rises faster than before.

6. How much higher could the sea be by 2100?

That depends mostly on how much more greenhouse gas the world emits. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) expects the world’s seas to stand, by 2100, 28 to 55 cm higher than in 1995–2014 if emissions fall to zero soon after 2050, and 63 to 101 cm higher if they keep rising fast. With very high emissions it cannot rule out a rise approaching two metres, because nobody knows yet how fast the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland could break up (IPCC 2021, B.5.3). The seas will keep rising for centuries after that, whatever happens to emissions, as the deep ocean warms slowly.

For Greece, with its coasts, ports, beaches and low deltas, even half a metre more means storms reaching further inland, and beaches that shrink; and where the land sinks, the sea rises faster still.

# NOAA's satellite record of the mean sea level since the end of 1992, with the seasons taken out,
# mm: a column for each satellite, a row for each pass over the ocean
altimetry<-function(region) {
  url<-paste0("https://www.star.nesdis.noaa.gov/socd/lsa/SeaLevelRise/slr/slr_sla_",region,
              "_free_ref_90.csv")
  x<-read.csv(text=read_lines(url),comment.char="#")
  passes<-rowSums(!is.na(x[-1]))>0
  result<-data.frame(time=x$year[passes],mm=rowSums(x[passes,-1],na.rm=TRUE))
  result$year<-floor(result$time)
  return(result)
}
world_passes<-altimetry("gbl")
mediterranean_passes<-altimetry("mds")
# the mean of every year that the satellites saw from beginning to end
satellite_years<-function(passes) {
  span<-tapply(passes$time,passes$year,function(t) max(t)-min(t))
  result<-aggregate(mm~year,passes,mean)
  result<-result[span[as.character(result$year)]>0.9,]
  return(result)
}
world<-satellite_years(world_passes)
mediterranean<-satellite_years(mediterranean_passes)
last_satellite<-max(world$year)
# the rise since 2000 at Piraeus, in the Mediterranean and in the world, over the same years
rates<-c(Piraeus=rise(piraeus,from,last_satellite),Mediterranean=rise(mediterranean,from),
         World=rise(world,from))
# a comparison in words: "faster than", "about as fast as" or "slower than"
compared<-function(a,b) {
  result<-if(abs(a-b)<0.5) "about as fast as" else if(a>b) "faster than" else "slower than"
  return(result)
}
# the same for two amounts: "more than", "about the same as" or "less than"
compared_amount<-function(a,b) {
  result<-if(abs(a-b)<0.5) "about the same as" else if(a>b) "more than" else "less than"
  return(result)
}

# yearly means in centimetres above their own average of 2000-2019, so that a tide gauge and the
# satellites can share a chart
rebase<-function(x) {
  result<-data.frame(year=x$year,cm=(x$mm-mean(x$mm[x$year>=2000&x$year<=2019]))/10)
  return(result)

# every year from the first to the last, with NA where a year is missing, so the line breaks there
all_years<-function(x) {
  result<-merge(data.frame(year=min(x$year):max(x$year)),x,all.x=TRUE)
  return(result)
}

## 1. How is the height of the sea measured?

**With tide gauges since the 19th century, and with satellites since 1992.** A tide gauge sits in a harbour and measures the height of the water against a fixed mark on land, every few minutes; the average of a month or a year smooths out the tides, the waves and the weather. Since the end of 1992 satellites have also measured the height of the whole ocean, by timing a radar pulse from the satellite to the sea and back.

The two measure different things. A satellite measures the sea against the centre of the Earth. A tide gauge measures **the sea against the land it stands on**, and in Greece the land moves: earthquakes lift it or drop it, and soft ground under some cities sinks. For the people of a harbour, the tide gauge measures what matters.

---

## 2. Is the sea rising at Piraeus?

**Yes, since about `r from`; before that it hardly rose at all.** The tide gauge of Piraeus has measured the sea since `r min(piraeus$year)`. From `r min(piraeus$year)` to 1988, when its record runs with few gaps, a straight line through its yearly means `r if(rise(piraeus,to=1988)<0) "falls" else "rises"` by `r mm_year(abs(rise(piraeus,to=1988)))`. From `r from` to `r max(piraeus$year)` it rises by **`r mm_year(rates[["Piraeus"]])`**. The years in between are left out of both lines: several are missing, and some look doubtful`r if(flagged_1990) " (the PSMSL flags all the months of 1990, as the weather of that year disturbed the gauge)"`. In all, the sea at Piraeus stood `r format(round(climb,1),nsmall=1)` cm higher in `r max(piraeus$year)-9`–`r max(piraeus$year)` than in `r min(piraeus$year)`–1988.

## 5. Is the rise speeding up?

**Yes.** The satellites have now watched the oceans for more than thirty years, long enough to see the rise grow faster. Straight lines through the first and the last ten years of their record:

```{r speed}
first_ten<-1993:2002
last_ten<-(last_satellite-9):last_satellite
speeds<-data.frame(sea=c("World","Mediterranean"),
                   first=c(rise(world,min(first_ten),max(first_ten)),
                           rise(mediterranean,min(first_ten),max(first_ten))),
                   last=c(rise(world,min(last_ten),last_satellite),
                          rise(mediterranean,min(last_ten),last_satellite)))
knitr::kable(data.frame(sea=speeds$sea,first=mm_year(speeds$first),last=mm_year(speeds$last)),
             col.names=c("Sea","1993–2002",paste0(min(last_ten),"–",last_satellite)),align="lrr")
# the acceleration of the world's rise: a parabola through all the passes, mm a year, every year
acceleration<-2*unname(coef(lm(mm~time+I(time^2),world_passes))[3])
```

The world's seas now rise by about `r mm_year(speeds$last[1])`, against `r mm_year(speeds$first[1])` in the 1990s. A parabola through all the passes of the satellites gives an acceleration of about `r format(round(acceleration,2),nsmall=2)` mm a year, every year. The Mediterranean moves more from decade to decade than the whole ocean, so its last ten years say less about the long run; but they too show a sea that rises faster than before.

## 6. How much higher could the sea be by 2100?

That depends mostly on how much more greenhouse gas the world emits. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) expects the world's seas to stand, by 2100, **28 to 55 cm** higher than in 1995–2014 if emissions fall to zero soon after 2050, and **63 to 101 cm** higher if they keep rising fast. With very high emissions it cannot rule out a rise approaching two metres, because nobody knows yet how fast the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland could break up (IPCC 2021, B.5.3). The seas will keep rising for centuries after that, whatever happens to emissions, as the deep ocean warms slowly.

For Greece, with its coasts, ports, beaches and low deltas, even half a metre more means storms reaching further inland, and beaches that shrink; and where the land sinks, the sea rises faster still.

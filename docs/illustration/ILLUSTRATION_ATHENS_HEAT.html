Athens Is Getting Hotter: Hot Days, Tropical Nights and Heatwaves Since 1955

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Daily temperatures at Athens Hellinikon since 1955 in NOAA’s GHCN-Daily: warming stripes, days of 35 °C, nights above 20 °C, a longer hot season and the worst heatwaves.
Published

November 26, 2026

What is this?

Summers in Athens feel hotter than they used to. This data story checks that feeling against the measurements of one weather station, which has measured the highest and the lowest temperature of the day since 1955, with some gaps, and asks five questions:

  1. Is Athens hotter than it used to be?
  2. How many hot days does a summer have?
  3. Do the nights still cool down?
  4. Is the hot season longer?
  5. Which were the worst heatwaves?

The station is at Hellinikon, on the coast south of the city centre, where Athens had its airport until 2001. Its record comes from NOAA, the US agency for weather and climate data, which publishes it in the Global Historical Climatology Network Daily (GHCN-Daily), an archive of daily measurements from weather stations around the world (Menne et al. 2012). It is downloaded when the page is knitted, so the charts show the latest days in NOAA’s archive.

The words of this page:

  • a hot day reaches 35 °C or more, and a very hot day 40 °C or more;
  • on a tropical night the temperature stays above 20 °C, as in the climate change indices of the ETCCDI, an international team of experts; on a very warm night it stays above 25 °C;
  • a heatwave is three days or more in a row above 37 °C (section 5 says why 37);
  • summer is June, July and August.

The data

One station since 1955

GHCN-Daily lists its stations with where they are, and in the Athens area it has only one: Hellinikon, station GR000016716. It is also what this story needs, a long record from one place. The new airport, Eleftherios Venizelos, replaced Hellinikon in 2001 (Wikipedia), so its own record is much shorter, and joining the two would join two different places. The station at Hellinikon kept measuring after the airport moved.

NOAA keeps one file per station, with a row for each day:

TMAX and TMIN are the highest and the lowest temperature of the day, in tenths of a degree. Each comes with three flags, separated by commas (NOAA’s description of the flags): how it was measured, whether it failed one of NOAA’s quality checks, and where NOAA got it. Until 2000 the maximum comes from NOAA’s International collection, data that weather services sent to NOAA, and until 2022 the minimum from the European Climate Assessment & Dataset (Klein Tank et al. 2002). After that, both come from the reports that the station sends every hour or every few hours to the weather services of the world, as NOAA’s Global Summary of the Day (GSOD) collects them.

A change of source can put a step into a record, the same as moving a station. But GSOD also has the station’s reports for the years before, so in the last year before each change the two can be compared, day by day:

Code 
# GSOD of the same station (167160 99999) for a year: degrees Fahrenheit, 9999.9 when missing. A
# "*" flag marks a value worked out from the readings of the day instead of reported: left out
gsod <- function(year) {
  x <- read.csv(paste0(
    "https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/data/global-summary-of-the-day/access/",
    year,
    "/16716099999.csv"
  ))
  reported <- function(value, flag) ifelse(value > 9000 | trimws(flag) == "*", NA, value)
  data.frame(
    date = as.Date(x$DATE),
    gsod_max = (reported(x$MAX, x$MAX_ATTRIBUTES) - 32) * 5 / 9,
    gsod_min = (reported(x$MIN, x$MIN_ATTRIBUTES) - 32) * 5 / 9
  )
}
gsod_years <- lapply(setNames(unique(year_before), unique(year_before)), gsod)

# the two sources on the days that both have: the average gap between them, and the days on
# which they are more than 1 °C apart
compare <- function(measure) {
  year <- year_before[[measure]]
  both <- merge(days, gsod_years[[as.character(year)]], by = "date")
  gap <- if (measure == "maximum") both$gsod_max - both$tmax else both$gsod_min - both$tmin
  data.frame(
    measure = measure,
    year = year,
    days = sum(!is.na(gap)),
    average_gap = mean(abs(gap), na.rm = TRUE),
    apart = sum(abs(gap) > 1, na.rm = TRUE)
  )
}
overlap <- rbind(compare("maximum"), compare("minimum"))

They are the same measurements: on the days that both have, they differ by 0.07 °C or less on average, and by more than 1 °C on 7 days out of 546. The change of source is not a step in the record.

A check without gaps: ERA5-Land

One station can mislead: the place around it changes, and it misses days. So this story also downloads ERA5-Land, a reconstruction of the weather of every day since 1950 by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (Muñoz-Sabater et al. 2021; data: Muñoz Sabater 2019). It is a model of the land surface, driven by the weather of ERA5, the Centre’s reconstruction of the weather of the whole world, and it does not use the measurements of any station directly. It describes squares of land 9 km across, so it cannot see what happens around one station: it is a check on the station, not a replacement for it. It comes through the historical weather API of Open-Meteo (Zippenfenig 2023), for the nearest point of its grid that Open-Meteo counts as land:

The weak point of the record is its gaps. The station’s reports often leave out the maximum of the day: in 2013, only 4 days have one. A year with many missing days would seem to have fewer hot days than it had, so a year counts only if at least 80% of its days in the season that matters have a measurement: June to August for the summer average and the hot days, May to October for the warm nights, when almost all of them come. How much can the missing days change the average of a summer? ERA5-Land has every day, so it can tell: for each summer kept, its average over the days that the station has is at most 0.2 °C away from its average over all the days of that summer. Counts are another matter: a missing day may hide a hot day or a tropical night, so the counts of the years with missing days are, if anything, too low.

Each stripe below is one summer, from 1955 on the left to 2025 on the right, coloured by its average temperature, day and night together: blue for the cooler summers, red for the warmer ones. They follow Ed Hawkins’ warming stripes (#ShowYourStripes), which show in the same way how the world, a country or a city has warmed. As there, the colours change from blue to red at the average of 1961–2010, and reach their darkest three standard deviations away from it, a measure of how much summers differ from one another (theirs is that of 1901–2000, here it is that of 1961–2010): here 2.8 °C below and above 26.6 °C. Grey stripes are the summers with too many missing days.

  words <- c("second", "third", "fourth", "fifth", "sixth", "seventh", "eighth", "ninth", "tenth")
  if (k <= 10) paste("the", words[k - 1], "warmest") else paste0("the ", k, "th warmest")
}

The station and ERA5-Land agree. The warm and the cool summers are the same in both (their correlation is 0.94, where 1 would be a perfect match), and both have warmed: compared with 1961–1990, the summers of 2011–2025 that the station can count were 2.4 °C warmer at the station and 1.6 °C warmer in ERA5-Land. The summers that the station cannot count do not change the picture: over all the summers of 2011–2025, ERA5-Land was 1.6 °C warmer.

The station warmed by 0.8 °C more. Part of the difference may come from the place around it. Cities cool more slowly than the countryside at night (Founda and Santamouris 2017), and the station’s own surroundings have changed: the airport around it closed in 2001, and since 2020 the old airfield has been a building site (Wikipedia). NOAA’s file gives the station the same place for the whole record, so it does not say whether the station itself moved within the old airport. ERA5-Land cannot see any of this, and it warmed too: the warming is not only the station’s.

ERA5-Land also has the summer of 2026, which NOAA’s record of the station does not reach yet: it was 1.5 °C warmer than its summers of 1961–1990, the 13th warmest of its 72 summers since 1955.

What this does not show. NOAA’s record of the station has gaps, worst in the 2010s, and ends on 24 August 2025; ERA5-Land, which goes on to the present, is a model, not a measurement.

## What is this?

Summers in Athens feel hotter than they used to. This data story checks that feeling against the measurements of one weather station, which has measured the highest and the lowest temperature of the day since 1955, with some gaps, and asks five questions:

1. **Is Athens hotter than it used to be?**
2. **How many hot days does a summer have?**
3. **Do the nights still cool down?**
4. **Is the hot season longer?**
5. **Which were the worst heatwaves?**

The station is at Hellinikon, on the coast south of the city centre, where Athens had its airport until 2001. Its record comes from NOAA, the US agency for weather and climate data, which publishes it in the [Global Historical Climatology Network Daily](https://doi.org/10.7289/V5D21VHZ) (GHCN-Daily), an archive of daily measurements from weather stations around the world ([Menne et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1175/JTECH-D-11-00103.1)). It is downloaded when the page is knitted, so the charts show the latest days in NOAA's archive.

**The words of this page:**

- a **hot day** reaches 35 °C or more, and a **very hot day** 40 °C or more;
- on a **tropical night** the temperature stays above 20 °C, as in the [climate change indices of the ETCCDI](https://etccdi.pacificclimate.org/list_27_indices.shtml), an international team of experts; on a **very warm night** it stays above 25 °C;
- a **heatwave** is three days or more in a row above 37 °C (section 5 says why 37);
- **summer** is June, July and August.

## The data

### One station since 1955

GHCN-Daily lists [its stations](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/ghcn/daily/ghcnd-stations.txt) with where they are, and in the Athens area it has only one: Hellinikon, station `GR000016716`. It is also what this story needs, a long record from one place. The new airport, Eleftherios Venizelos, replaced Hellinikon in 2001 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellinikon_International_Airport)), so its own record is much shorter, and joining the two would join two different places. The station at Hellinikon kept measuring after the airport moved.

NOAA keeps [one file per station](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/data/global-historical-climatology-network-daily/access/GR000016716.csv), with a row for each day:


`TMAX` and `TMIN` are the highest and the lowest temperature of the day, in tenths of a degree. Each comes with three flags, separated by commas ([NOAA's description of the flags](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/ghcn/daily/readme.txt)): how it was measured, whether it failed one of NOAA's quality checks, and where NOAA got it. The values that failed a check are left out, and the days missing from the file are added as empty rows:

```{r days}

days$tmin[failed_min] <- NA
cbind(left_out = c(maximum = sum(failed_max), minimum = sum(failed_min)))
```

So the record runs from `r date_text(first_day)` to `r date_text(last_day)`, the latest day that NOAA has for this station. NOAA checks the values but does not correct them for new instruments or new ways of measuring ([NOAA](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/global-historical-climatology-network-daily)): the record is what the station measured.

Until `r year_before[["maximum"]]` the maximum comes from NOAA's International collection, data that weather services sent to NOAA, and until `r year_before[["minimum"]]` the minimum from the European Climate Assessment & Dataset ([Klein Tank et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.773)). After that, both come from the reports that the station sends every hour or every few hours to the weather services of the world, as NOAA's [Global Summary of the Day](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/data/global-summary-of-the-day/access/) (GSOD) collects them.

A change of source can put a step into a record, the same as moving a station. But GSOD also has the station's reports for the years before, so in the last year before each change the two can be compared, day by day:

```{r gsod}
# GSOD of the same station (167160 99999) for a year: degrees Fahrenheit, 9999.9 when missing. A
# "*" flag marks a value worked out from the readings of the day instead of reported: left out
gsod <- function(year) {
  x <- read.csv(paste0(
    "https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/data/global-summary-of-the-day/access/",
    year,
    "/16716099999.csv"
  ))
  reported <- function(value, flag) ifelse(value > 9000 | trimws(flag) == "*", NA, value)
  data.frame(
    date = as.Date(x$DATE),
    gsod_max = (reported(x$MAX, x$MAX_ATTRIBUTES) - 32) * 5 / 9,
    gsod_min = (reported(x$MIN, x$MIN_ATTRIBUTES) - 32) * 5 / 9
  )
}
gsod_years <- lapply(setNames(unique(year_before), unique(year_before)), gsod)

# the two sources on the days that both have: the average gap between them, and the days on
# which they are more than 1 °C apart
compare <- function(measure) {
  year <- year_before[[measure]]
  both <- merge(days, gsod_years[[as.character(year)]], by = "date")
  gap <- if (measure == "maximum") both$gsod_max - both$tmax else both$gsod_min - both$tmin
  data.frame(
    measure = measure,
    year = year,
    days = sum(!is.na(gap)),
    average_gap = mean(abs(gap), na.rm = TRUE),
    apart = sum(abs(gap) > 1, na.rm = TRUE)
  )
}
overlap <- rbind(compare("maximum"), compare("minimum"))
knitr::kable(
  overlap,
  digits = 2,
  col.names = c("Measure", "Year", "Days in both", "Average gap (°C)", "Days over 1 °C apart")
)
```

They are the same measurements: on the days that both have, they differ by `r two(max(overlap$average_gap))` °C or less on average, and by more than 1 °C on `r sum(overlap$apart)` days out of `r sum(overlap$days)`. The change of source is not a step in the record.

### A check without gaps: ERA5-Land

One station can mislead: the place around it changes, and it misses days. So this story also downloads ERA5-Land, a reconstruction of the weather of every day since 1950 by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts ([Muñoz-Sabater et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-13-4349-2021); data: [Muñoz Sabater 2019](https://doi.org/10.24381/cds.e2161bac)). It is a model of the land surface, driven by the weather of ERA5, the Centre's reconstruction of the weather of the whole world, and it does not use the measurements of any station directly. It describes squares of land 9 km across, so it cannot see what happens around one station: it is a check on the station, not a replacement for it. It comes through the [historical weather API of Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api) ([Zippenfenig 2023](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7970649)), for the nearest point of its grid that Open-Meteo counts as land:

The weak point of the record is its gaps. The station's reports often leave out the maximum of the day: in `r fewest$year`, only `r fewest$days` days have one. A year with many missing days would seem to have fewer hot days than it had, so a year counts only if at least `r 100 * enough`% of its days in the season that matters have a measurement: June to August for the summer average and the hot days, May to October for the warm nights, when almost all of them come. Of the `r nrow(years)` years from `r min(years$year)` to `r max(years$year)`, this leaves out `r nrow(years) - dropped$kept[1]` for the summer average, `r nrow(years) - dropped$kept[2]` for the hot days and `r nrow(years) - dropped$kept[3]` for the tropical nights:

How much can the missing days change the average of a summer? ERA5-Land has every day, so it can tell: for each summer kept, its average over the days that the station has is at most `r one(largest_shift)` °C away from its average over all the days of that summer. Counts are another matter: a missing day may hide a hot day or a tropical night, so the counts of the years with missing days are, if anything, too low.

## 1. Is Athens hotter than it used to be?

```{r periods}
# three periods: 1961-1990, the usual reference for the climate of a place, and the two after it
last_year <- max(years$year)
recent <- paste0("2011–", last_year)
period_of <- function(year) {
  cut(year, c(1960, 1990, 2010, last_year), labels = c("1961–1990", "1991–2010", recent))
}
years$period <- period_of(years$year)
by_period <- years |>
  filter(!is.na(period)) |>
  group_by(period) |>
  summarise(
    summers = sum(!is.na(summer)),
    summer_average = mean(summer, na.rm = TRUE),
    hot_years = sum(!is.na(hot)),
    hot_days = mean(hot, na.rm = TRUE),
    very_hot_days = sum(very_hot, na.rm = TRUE),
    night_years = sum(!is.na(tropical)),
    tropical_nights = mean(tropical, na.rm = TRUE),
    very_warm_nights = mean(very_warm, na.rm = TRUE)

base <- by_period$summer_average[1]
now <- by_period$summer_average[3]
```

**Yes. The summers of `r recent` were `r one(now - base)` °C warmer than those of 1961–1990.**

Each stripe below is one summer, from `r min(years$year)` on the left to `r last_year` on the right, coloured by its average temperature, day and night together: blue for the cooler summers, red for the warmer ones. They follow Ed Hawkins' [warming stripes](https://showyourstripes.info/) (#ShowYourStripes), which show in the same way how the world, a country or a city has warmed. As there, the colours change from blue to red at the average of 1961–2010, and reach their darkest three standard deviations away from it, a measure of how much summers differ from one another (theirs is that of 1901–2000, here it is that of 1961–2010): here `r one(3 * sd(years$summer[years$year %in% 1961:2010], na.rm = TRUE))` °C below and above `r one(mean(years$summer[years$year %in% 1961:2010], na.rm = TRUE))` °C. Grey stripes are the summers with too many missing days.

**The station and ERA5-Land agree.** The warm and the cool summers are the same in both (their correlation is `r two(together)`, where 1 would be a perfect match), and both have warmed: compared with 1961–1990, the summers of `r recent` that the station can count were `r one(station_warming)` °C warmer at the station and `r one(era5_warming)` °C warmer in ERA5-Land. The summers that the station cannot count do not change the picture: over all the summers of `r recent`, ERA5-Land was `r one(era5_recent)` °C warmer.

**The station warmed by `r one(station_warming - era5_warming)` °C more.** Part of the difference may come from the place around it. Cities cool more slowly than the countryside at night ([Founda and Santamouris 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-11407-6)), and the station's own surroundings have changed: the airport around it closed in 2001, and since 2020 the old airfield has been a building site ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellinikon_International_Airport)). NOAA's file gives the station the same place for the whole record, so it does not say whether the station itself moved within the old airport. ERA5-Land cannot see any of this, and it warmed too: the warming is not only the station's.

ERA5-Land also has the summer of `r era5_latest$year`, which NOAA's record of the station does not reach `r if (era5_latest$year > last_year) "yet" else "in full"`: it was `r one(era5_latest$summer - era5_base)` °C warmer than its summers of 1961–1990, `r place_text(era5_place)` of its `r nrow(era5_summers)` summers since `r min(era5_summers$year)`.

3. **The nights cool down less**: `r round(by_period$very_warm_nights[3])` very warm nights a year instead of `r round(by_period$very_warm_nights[1])`.
4. **The hot season is longer**: its warm nights start earlier and end later, `r round(longer)` days longer in all.
5. **Heatwaves come more often and last longer**: the longest at Hellinikon lasted `r ten$days[1]` days, in `r format(ten$start[1], "%Y")`.

**What this does not show.** It is one station, at the edge of the city and by the sea; the centre of Athens and the plains inland are hotter by day, and some of the station's warming may come from the city around it, which this record cannot separate from the climate. NOAA's record of the station has gaps, worst in the 2010s, and ends on `r date_text(last_day)`; ERA5-Land, which goes on to the present, is a model, not a measurement.

For the world as a whole, see the [NASA GISS Global Temperature Anomaly](ILLUSTRATION_CLIMATE_HIGHCHARTER.html); for more of what a weather station measures, hour by hour, the [Airport Weather Analysis](ILLUSTRATION_AIRPORT_WEATHER.html) of the new Athens airport; and for the fires that burn in Greek summers, [Greece Burning](ILLUSTRATION_GREEK_WILDFIRES.html).

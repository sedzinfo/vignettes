Where tornadoes happen: the United States state by state since 1950, how Canada’s count compares, when they strike, how strong and how deadly they are, the worst events, and whether there are more of them.
Published

November 27, 2026

What is this?

A tornado is a narrow, violently rotating column of air that reaches from a storm cloud to the ground. People think of the American Plains, and the United States does report more tornadoes than any other country we can count. But there is no single open database of the world’s tornadoes, so this story does what the data allow: it counts the United States closely, sets Canada beside it, and says plainly what cannot be compared. It asks five questions:

  1. Where do tornadoes happen?
  2. When do they strike?
  3. How strong and how deadly are they?
  4. Which were the worst?
  5. Are there more of them than there used to be?

This is one of a series on natural disasters: see the overview and Floods.

The data come from two places, downloaded when the page is rendered:

  • The United States: the tornado database of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s Storm Prediction Center (SPC), one row for each tornado since 1950, with its date and time, the state, the start and end points, the length and width of its path, the rating, and the people injured and killed (SPC severe weather database). It is made of the reports that the National Weather Service’s offices send in (format description). The file used here is the one with single tracks: a tornado that crosses a state line is one row, in the state where it touched down.
  • Canada: the event summaries of the Northern Tornadoes Project, founded at Western University in 2017, which investigates tornadoes by ground, drone, aerial and satellite surveys (Northern Tornadoes Project).

The rating of a tornado is not a measurement of its wind. Since February 2007 the National Weather Service rates a tornado on the Enhanced Fujita scale, from EF0 to EF5, from the damage that it leaves: an EF0 is estimated at 65 to 85 miles per hour (the gust of three seconds), an EF1 at 86 to 110, an EF2 at 111 to 135, an EF3 at 136 to 165, an EF4 at 166 to 200 and an EF5 at more than 200 (National Weather Service). Before 2007 the original Fujita scale (F0 to F5) was used, in the same way. This page writes EF0 to EF5 for both. A tornado is rated by what it destroys, so one that crosses empty land can get a low rating whatever its wind.

Europe is missing, on purpose. The European Severe Weather Database, kept by the European Severe Storms Laboratory, lets a guest browse only recent events; the full data need a request for restricted access (ESWD). A study of its reports from 1950 to 2015 finds that tornadoes do occur in Europe, with injuries, deaths and damage, “although their reported frequencies and intensities are lower compared with the United States” (Antonescu et al. 2017). That is all this page can say about Europe from what it has read.

Some tornadoes have no rating. 1,546 of the 73,458 tornadoes (2.1%) are coded -9, “unknown”. For 1953 to 1982 the SPC has filled in an estimated rating, from the property loss and the length of the path, for 1,863 tornadoes that had none; the file marks them. The 1,546 that remain unrated come from 2007 on, and from 2019 on they are 10% to 20% of the tornadoes of a year. The documentation does not say why. This page counts them in the totals, shows them apart in the tables, and, where a trend depends on the ratings, tests what happens if all of them were strong (section 5).

Times are those of the sun. The SPC converts the time of a tornado to central standard time, whatever the state (37 tornadoes are on another clock). A tornado in Georgia at 17:00 central standard time struck at six in the evening by the clock there. To compare places, section 2 turns each time into the sun’s time at the place where the tornado began, from its longitude: an hour for every 15 degrees.

The EF2 and stronger tornadoes fell from 189 a year to 142 (by chance less than 1 in 100), the EF3 and stronger from 50 to 28 (less than 1 in 100). Their peak year was 1973. But ratings are judgements of damage, they changed from one scale to the other in 2007, and from 2007 the unrated tornadoes grew in number, so this page does not conclude that the strong tornadoes have become rarer. The unrated tornadoes matter for the count of EF1 and above: if every one of them is counted in it, the chance of a pattern as strong as this one without a trend becomes 1 in 100, against 6. What changed more is how the tornadoes come. The days with at least one tornado of EF1 or above fell from 120.4 a year to 110.0, while the days with ten or more of them rose from 6.7 to 17.9 (the chances of the two patterns, less than 1 and less than 1 in 100). That is what a study of the database found, after setting aside the weakest tornadoes: the mean annual number “has remained relatively constant”, but the variability has increased since the 1970s, with fewer days with tornadoes and more days with many (Brooks et al. 2014).

So, what does it mean?

  1. A few states have the most tornadoes for their size, and they are not the largest. Mississippi, Alabama and Illinois had the most per area from 2006 to 2025, and Texas, the state with the most tornadoes, only 24th. Canada’s project finds about 112 a year, against 1,346 in the United States, but the two are not counted the same way.
  2. They come in the afternoon and evening, mostly in spring. 22% of them began in May, and each part of the country has its own busiest month.
  3. Most are weak and most deaths come from the strongest. EF4 and EF5 tornadoes were 0.9% of the tornadoes and 61% of the deaths.
  4. The worst years and days are far from the average. 27 April 2011 had 210 tornadoes in one day, and 2011 killed 553 people.
  5. More are counted, but that is not the same as more. The tornadoes of EF2 and above did not rise; what has changed is that the tornadoes of EF1 and above come on fewer days and in bigger groups.

What this does not show. There is no worldwide count: the data cover the United States and, in outline, Canada, and say nothing of the many countries where tornadoes are not systematically recorded, so this page cannot say that the United States has the most tornadoes in the world, only the most that are counted. Reports depend on who is there to see a tornado, and the ratings on what it destroyed. Averages by state hide the differences inside a state, and a rank test says whether a pattern is likely to be chance, not what causes it. And the counts say nothing of the people who live where tornadoes land.

## What is this?

A tornado is a narrow, violently rotating column of air that reaches from a storm cloud to the ground. People think of the American Plains, and the United States does report more tornadoes than any other country we can count. But there is no single open database of the world's tornadoes, so this story does what the data allow: it counts the United States closely, sets Canada beside it, and says plainly what cannot be compared. It asks five questions:

1. **Where do tornadoes happen?**
2. **When do they strike?**
3. **How strong and how deadly are they?**
4. **Which were the worst?**
5. **Are there more of them than there used to be?**

This is one of a series on natural disasters: see the [overview](ILLUSTRATION_NATURAL_DISASTERS.html) and [Floods](ILLUSTRATION_WORLD_FLOODS.html).

The data come from two places, downloaded when the page is rendered:

- **The United States**: the tornado database of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's **Storm Prediction Center** (SPC), one row for each tornado since 1950, with its date and time, the state, the start and end points, the length and width of its path, the rating, and the people injured and killed ([SPC severe weather database](https://www.spc.noaa.gov/wcm/#data)). It is made of the reports that the National Weather Service's offices send in ([format description](https://www.spc.noaa.gov/wcm/data/SPC_severe_database_description.pdf)). The file used here is the one with single tracks: a tornado that crosses a state line is one row, in the state where it touched down.
- **Canada**: the event summaries of the **Northern Tornadoes Project**, founded at Western University in 2017, which investigates tornadoes by ground, drone, aerial and satellite surveys ([Northern Tornadoes Project](https://www.uwo.ca/ntp/)).

**The rating of a tornado** is not a measurement of its wind. Since February 2007 the National Weather Service rates a tornado on the **Enhanced Fujita scale**, from EF0 to EF5, from the damage that it leaves: an EF0 is estimated at 65 to 85 miles per hour (the gust of three seconds), an EF1 at 86 to 110, an EF2 at 111 to 135, an EF3 at 136 to 165, an EF4 at 166 to 200 and an EF5 at more than 200 ([National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/oun/efscale)). Before 2007 the original **Fujita scale** (F0 to F5) was used, in the same way. This page writes EF0 to EF5 for both. A tornado is rated by what it destroys, so one that crosses empty land can get a low rating whatever its wind.

**Europe is missing, on purpose.** The European Severe Weather Database, kept by the European Severe Storms Laboratory, lets a guest browse only recent events; the full data need a request for restricted access ([ESWD](https://eswd.eu/)). A study of its reports from 1950 to 2015 finds that tornadoes do occur in Europe, with injuries, deaths and damage, "although their reported frequencies and intensities are lower compared with the United States" ([Antonescu et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1175/bams-d-16-0171.1)). That is all this page can say about Europe from what it has read.

```{r chart_style}
# a rank test of a trend over the years: the chance, in 100, of a pattern as strong as this one
# without a trend
chance<-function(x,years) {
  p<-cor.test(years,x,method="kendall",exact=FALSE)$p.value
  result<-if(p<0.01) "less than 1" else as.character(round(100*p))
  return(result)
}
# a date as "27 April 2011"
long_date<-function(x) {
  result<-trimws(format(as.Date(x),"%e %B %Y"))
  return(result)
}
```

```{r trend_numbers}
ef1_days[is.na(ef1_days)]<-0
changes<-rbind(trend_row("All tornadoes",by_year$n),trend_row("EF1 or above",by_year$ef1),
               trend_row("EF2 or above",by_year$ef2),trend_row("EF3 or above",by_year$ef3),
               trend_row("People killed",by_year$deaths),
               trend_row("Days with an EF1 or above",ef1_days$days),
               trend_row("Days with ten or more EF1 or above",ef1_days$big_days))
changes$change<-100*(changes$end/changes$start-1)
of_change<-function(name,column) {
  result<-changes[[column]][changes$name==name]
  return(result)
}
# what if every unrated tornado were EF1 or above?
chance_if_strong<-chance(by_year$ef1+by_year$unrated,by_year$yr)
before_2007<-by_year$yr<2007
chance_before<-chance(by_year$ef1[before_2007],by_year$yr[before_2007])
chance_after<-chance(by_year$ef1[!before_2007],by_year$yr[!before_2007])
ef2_peak<-by_year$yr[which.max(by_year$ef2)]
ef0<-by_year$n-by_year$ef1-by_year$unrated
ef0_start<-count_after(ef0,first_ten)
ef0_end<-count_after(ef0,last_ten)
```

**More are reported, but the rise is mostly in the weak tornadoes.** In `r min(first_ten)`–`r max(first_ten)` the SPC database had `r number(of_change("All tornadoes","start"))` tornadoes a year, in `r min(last_ten)`–`r max(last_ten)` `r number(of_change("All tornadoes","end"))`: `r percent(of_change("All tornadoes","change"))` more, and a rank test of all the years finds the rise (the chance of a pattern as strong as this one without a trend is `r of_change("All tornadoes","chance")` in 100). Much of the rise is in the weakest tornadoes: EF0 tornadoes went from `r number(ef0_start)` a year to `r number(ef0_end)`. A study of the database found that the doubling of the count from the 1950s to the 2000s "is likely not related to meteorological causes alone", and that the count of F1 and stronger tornadoes was steadier than that of F2 and stronger ([Verbout et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1175/waf910.1)); another says that changes in how tornadoes are reported "have made it difficult" to read the trend, and leaves out the weakest ([Brooks et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1257460)). Counting from EF1 up, the rise is smaller: `r number(of_change("EF1 or above","start"))` a year at the start and `r number(of_change("EF1 or above","end"))` at the end (`r change_text(of_change("EF1 or above","change"))`), and the test does not clearly find a trend (`r of_change("EF1 or above","chance")` in 100).

## So, what does it mean?

1. **A few states have the most tornadoes for their size, and they are not the largest.** `r and_list(top_density)` had the most per area from `r since` to `r last_year`, and `r by_count$state[1]`, the state with the most tornadoes, only `r which(by_density$st==by_count$st[1])`th. Canada's project finds about `r number(canada_a_year)` a year, against `r number(us_a_year)` in the United States, but the two are not counted the same way.
2. **They come in the afternoon and evening, mostly in spring.** `r percent(all_months[peak_month])` of them began in `r month.name[peak_month]`, and each part of the country has its own busiest month.
3. **Most are weak and most deaths come from the strongest.** EF4 and EF5 tornadoes were `r percent(violent_tor_share,1)` of the tornadoes and `r percent(violent_death_share)` of the deaths.
4. **The worst years and days are far from the average.** `r long_date(record_day$date)` had `r number(record_day$n)` tornadoes in one day, and `r worst_years$yr[1]` killed `r number(worst_years$deaths[1])` people.
5. **More are counted, but that is not the same as more.** The tornadoes of EF2 and above did not rise; what has changed is that the tornadoes of EF1 and above come on fewer days and in bigger groups.

**What this does not show.** There is no worldwide count: the data cover the United States and, in outline, Canada, and say nothing of the many countries where tornadoes are not systematically recorded, so this page cannot say that the United States has the most tornadoes in the world, only the most that are counted. Reports depend on who is there to see a tornado, and the ratings on what it destroyed. Averages by state hide the differences inside a state, and a rank test says whether a pattern is likely to be chance, not what causes it. And the counts say nothing of the people who live where tornadoes land.

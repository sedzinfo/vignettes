Where in the world floods happen, kill and displace the most people, when each part of the world floods, the worst floods on record, and whether floods have become more frequent or deadlier, from two open flood records.
Published

November 27, 2026

What is this?

Wherever rain falls and a river runs, a flood is possible, so the records show floods in every part of the world. But they do not happen equally often, and they do not kill equally often. This story asks five questions:

  1. Where in the world do the large floods happen?
  2. Where do floods kill the most people?
  3. When does each part of the world flood?
  4. Which were the worst floods?
  5. Are floods more frequent or deadlier than they were?

It is one of a series on natural hazards: see Natural Disasters for the overview, and Tornadoes for another hazard of the weather.

No single record of floods is complete, so the page uses two, which count different things, and downloads them when it is rendered:

  • The Global Flood Records of the DFO Flood Observatory (University of Colorado), one row for each large flood from January 1985 to December 2023: when it started and ended, the countries it hit, its estimated cause, the people killed and the people displaced (DFO, data). DFO gives a flood an entry when it “appears to be large”: significant damage to buildings or farmland, a long time since the last similar flood, or deaths. It builds the record from news and government reports (DFO notes on the archive). The rows say where the flood was, not how many people lived there.
  • EM-DAT, the international disaster database of the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters (CRED, UCLouvain), as yearly totals for each country since 2000: floods that are confirmed by at least two sources and meet one of its entry criteria, which are 10 or more deaths, 100 or more people affected, a call for international help or a declaration of an emergency (EM-DAT FAQ). The totals come from the Humanitarian Data Exchange.
  • The population and the land area of each country, and the continent it is in, from Our World in Data (population, land area, continents).

What counts as a flood. EM-DAT uses “flood” as a general term for water that overflows a river onto dry land, for higher-than-normal water along a coast or around a lake, and for flash floods, where water ponds where the rain fell; it includes glacial lake outburst floods (Our World in Data). Dead in EM-DAT means the dead plus the missing; in DFO, the missing are not counted. Affected in EM-DAT means injured, affected or homeless (EM-DAT). A tropical cyclone is a large storm that forms over warm sea and brings wind, heavy rain and a rise of the sea; a monsoon is a season of heavy rain that comes with a seasonal change in the wind.

The archive and the countries. The DFO archive has 5,503 records; 2 of them start in January 2024, after the end of the years it covers, and are left out. 424 records name more than one country, and a flood counts once for each country it hit. 18 records name only Czechoslovakia, Serbia Montenegro, Soviet Union and Yugoslavia, which no longer exist as countries, and are left out of the figures by country.

Tsunamis are not floods. DFO’s records have 5 tsunamis: 4 with the cause “tsunami”, and the record of 26 December 2004 in Thailand, which DFO calls a “tidal surge” and gives 160,000 dead: the date of the earthquake and tsunami in the Indian Ocean, which left 227,898 people dead or missing in all the countries on its shores (NOAA). EM-DAT counts the Thai tsunami with the earthquakes, with 8,345 dead and missing. These 5 are left out, which leaves 5,496 floods from 1985 to 2023.

EM-DAT: the years, and the empty cells. The yearly figures of EM-DAT that this page uses start in 2000, and they are those of the version of 25 November 2026. 2026 is not over, and its 61 floods so far are left out: the figures run from 2000 to 2025. In 24% of the rows of floods (a country, a year and a kind of flood) no number of dead is given, and in 6% no number of people affected. EM-DAT cannot tell an unreported number from none (EM-DAT FAQ), so a total here is a minimum, and the rows without a figure are counted as zero. A flood that hit several countries is a record in each. 5 rows for former countries are left out. EM-DAT includes 7 country-years of glacial lake outburst floods, which are kept.

So, what does it mean?

  1. Asia has the most large floods. 43% of the large floods of the record, and the countries with the most are the United States, China and India. Per square kilometre, Haiti and Sri Lanka lead.
  2. A few large countries have most of the dead, and other countries have the most for each person. The ten countries with the most dead have 63% of the deaths in EM-DAT since 2000; per million people, Haiti leads, with 12.6 a year.
  3. Every part of the world has its wet season. Asia floods in June to September, South America and Oceania in the first months of the year.
  4. A few floods kill most of the people. The 10 deadliest floods have 60% of the dead of DFO’s floods, and 5 of them came with a cyclone.
  5. The records do not show a clear rise in floods or in flood deaths since 2000. Older counts rise and fall with the way floods were recorded.

What this does not show. Neither record is a count of all floods: DFO records the large floods that the news and government reports tell of, and EM-DAT those that meet its entry criteria, so a country with little news coverage or few reports may have more floods than it shows. A flood that kills nobody, in a country that does not ask for help, may appear in neither. The counts are of floods, not of how long they lasted or how much land they covered, and the estimates of the dead, the missing and the displaced differ between sources. Nothing here says what causes floods, or whether climate change is changing them: for that, a record must be built to measure it. And the table of the worst floods leaves out the records that did not agree with the other source, which may include a true record that EM-DAT lists differently.

## What is this?

Wherever rain falls and a river runs, a flood is possible, so the records show floods in every part of the world. But they do not happen equally often, and they do not kill equally often. This story asks five questions:

1. **Where in the world do the large floods happen?**
2. **Where do floods kill the most people?**
3. **When does each part of the world flood?**
4. **Which were the worst floods?**
5. **Are floods more frequent or deadlier than they were?**

It is one of a series on natural hazards: see [Natural Disasters](ILLUSTRATION_NATURAL_DISASTERS.html) for the overview, and [Tornadoes](ILLUSTRATION_WORLD_TORNADOES.html) for another hazard of the weather.

No single record of floods is complete, so the page uses two, which count different things, and downloads them when it is rendered:

- **The Global Flood Records of the DFO Flood Observatory** (University of Colorado), one row for each *large* flood from January 1985 to December 2023: when it started and ended, the countries it hit, its estimated cause, the people killed and the people displaced ([DFO](https://floodobservatory.colorado.edu/wiki/FloodRecords), [data](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19288171)). DFO gives a flood an entry when it "appears to be large": significant damage to buildings or farmland, a long time since the last similar flood, or deaths. It builds the record from news and government reports ([DFO notes on the archive](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://floodobservatory.colorado.edu/Archives/ArchiveNotes.html)). The rows say where the flood was, not how many people lived there.
- **EM-DAT**, the international disaster database of the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters (CRED, UCLouvain), as yearly totals for each country since 2000: floods that are confirmed by at least two sources and meet one of its entry criteria, which are 10 or more deaths, 100 or more people affected, a call for international help or a declaration of an emergency ([EM-DAT FAQ](https://doc.emdat.be/docs/additional-resources-and-tutorials/faq/)). The totals come from the [Humanitarian Data Exchange](https://data.humdata.org/dataset/emdat-country-profiles).
- **The population and the land area of each country**, and the continent it is in, from Our World in Data ([population](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/population), [land area](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/land-area-km), [continents](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/continents-according-to-our-world-in-data)).

**What counts as a flood.** EM-DAT uses "flood" as a general term for water that overflows a river onto dry land, for higher-than-normal water along a coast or around a lake, and for flash floods, where water ponds where the rain fell; it includes glacial lake outburst floods ([Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/natural-disasters-deaths)). **Dead** in EM-DAT means the dead plus the missing; in DFO, the missing are not counted. **Affected** in EM-DAT means injured, affected or homeless ([EM-DAT](https://doc.emdat.be/docs/data-structure-and-content/impact-variables/human/)). A **tropical cyclone** is a large storm that forms over warm sea and brings wind, heavy rain and a rise of the sea; a **monsoon** is a season of heavy rain that comes with a seasonal change in the wind.

**The archive and the countries.** The DFO archive has `r number(n_records)` records; `r n_after` of them start in January `r last_dfo_year+1`, after the end of the years it covers, and are left out. `r number(n_multi)` records name more than one country, and a flood counts once for each country it hit. `r n_gone` records name only `r and_list(gone)`, which no longer exist as countries, and are left out of the figures by country.

**Tsunamis are not floods.** DFO's records have `r n_tsunami` tsunamis: `r n_tsunami-nrow(thai)` with the cause "tsunami", and the record of `r format(thai$start_date,"%d %B %Y")` in `r thai$country`, which DFO calls a "`r tolower(thai$cause)`" and gives `r number(thai$dead)` dead: the date of the earthquake and tsunami in the Indian Ocean, which left `r number(227898)` people dead or missing in all the countries on its shores ([NOAA](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazard/data/publications/2004_1226.pdf)). EM-DAT counts the Thai tsunami with the earthquakes, with `r number(as.numeric(emdat$dead[emdat$iso=="THA"&emdat$year==2004&emdat$subtype=="Tsunami"]))` dead and missing. These `r n_tsunami` are left out, which leaves `r number(n_floods)` floods from `r first_dfo_year` to `r last_dfo_year`.

**EM-DAT: the years, and the empty cells.** The yearly figures of EM-DAT that this page uses start in `r first_em_year`, and they are those of the version of `r format(emdat_date,"%d %B %Y")`. `r partial_year` is not over, and its `r n_partial` floods so far are left out: the figures run from `r first_em_year` to `r last_em_year`. In `r percent(share_no_dead)` of the rows of floods (a country, a year and a kind of flood) no number of dead is given, and in `r percent(share_no_affected)` no number of people affected. EM-DAT cannot tell an unreported number from none ([EM-DAT FAQ](https://doc.emdat.be/docs/additional-resources-and-tutorials/faq/)), so a total here is a minimum, and the rows without a figure are counted as zero. A flood that hit several countries is a record in each. `r nrow(em_lost)` rows for former countries are left out. EM-DAT includes `r n_glof` country-years of glacial lake outburst floods, which are kept.

## 5. Are floods more frequent or deadlier than they were?

```{r trend_numbers}

                           world_pop$population_historical[match(em_year$year,world_pop$year)]*1e6

low_first<-dfo_year[which.min(dfo_year$n),]
em_peak<-em_year[which.max(em_year$n),]
# what a rank test says of a trend: "no clear trend" unless the chance of the pattern without a
# trend is under 5 in 100
trend_text<-function(x,years) {
  p<-cor.test(years,x,method="kendall",exact=FALSE)$p.value
  if(p>=0.05)
    return("no clear trend")
  if(cor(x,years,method="kendall")>0)
    return("a rise")
  return("a fall")
}
em_dead_peak<-em_year[which.max(em_year$dead),]
```

**There is no clear trend in the floods since 2000, and the older years do not compare.** The count of large floods in DFO's record went from `r low_first$n` in `r low_first$year` to `r peak$n` in `r peak$year`, and down to `r low_after` in `r and_list(low_after_years)`: swings of that size over a few years say more about how floods were found and written down than about floods (from 2000 to 2023 a rank test finds no trend in this count: by chance, `r chance(dfo_late$n,dfo_late$year)` in 100). DFO warns that the quality and the quantity of the information about a flood is "not always in proportion to its actual magnitude", and that news from some countries arrives later and with less detail ([DFO notes on the archive](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://floodobservatory.colorado.edu/Archives/ArchiveNotes.html)). EM-DAT warns of the same, the more so for the years before 2000: the rise in the number of disasters recorded from the 1960s on "largely reflects improvements in reporting, communication technology, and the growth of the database itself", and it advises leaving the years before 2000 out of any study of trends ([EM-DAT FAQ](https://doc.emdat.be/docs/additional-resources-and-tutorials/faq/)). So the figures for EM-DAT keep to `r first_em_year` onwards.

From `r first_em_year` to `r last_em_year`, EM-DAT records `r number(min(em_year$n))` to `r number(max(em_year$n))` floods a year, with `r number(em_peak$n)` in `r and_list(em_year$year[em_year$n==em_peak$n])`, and a rank test finds `r trend_text(em_year$n,em_year$year)`: the chance, in 100, of a pattern as strong as this one without a trend is `r chance(em_year$n,em_year$year)`. For the dead it finds `r trend_text(em_year$dead,em_year$year)` (by chance, `r chance(em_year$dead,em_year$year)` in 100); the dead range from `r number(min(em_year$dead))` to `r number(max(em_year$dead))` a year, most in `r em_dead_peak$year`. For the dead of floods per million people in the world, from `r first_em_year` to `r last_pop_year`, the last year of the population data, it finds `r trend_text(rate_years$per_million_world,rate_years$year)` (`r one(rate_years$per_million_world[1])` in `r first_em_year`, `r one(rate_years$per_million_world[nrow(rate_years)])` in `r last_pop_year`; by chance, `r chance(rate_years$per_million_world,rate_years$year)` in 100). For the people affected it finds `r trend_text(em_year$affected,em_year$year)` (by chance, `r chance(em_year$affected,em_year$year)` in 100), but a few years dominate the total, such as `r em_year$year[which.max(em_year$affected)]`, with `r million(max(em_year$affected))` million.

## So, what does it mean?

1. **Asia has the most large floods.** `r percent(share_asia)` of the large floods of the record, and the countries with the most are `r and_list(with_the(top_count$name[1:3]))`. Per square kilometre, `r and_list(with_the(top_area$name[1:2]))` lead.
2. **A few large countries have most of the dead, and other countries have the most for each person.** The ten countries with the most dead have `r percent(100*sum(top_dead$dead)/total_dead)` of the deaths in EM-DAT since `r first_em_year`; per million people, `r with_the(top_rate$name[1])` leads, with `r one(top_rate$per_million[1])` a year.
3. **Every part of the world has its wet season.** Asia floods in June to September, South America and Oceania in the first months of the year.
4. **A few floods kill most of the people.** The `r nrow(top_worst)` deadliest floods have `r percent(top_share)` of the dead of DFO's floods, and `r sum(top_worst$cause_group=="Cyclone")` of them came with a cyclone.
5. **The records do not show a clear rise in floods or in flood deaths since `r first_em_year`.** Older counts rise and fall with the way floods were recorded.

**What this does not show.** Neither record is a count of all floods: DFO records the large floods that the news and government reports tell of, and EM-DAT those that meet its entry criteria, so a country with little news coverage or few reports may have more floods than it shows. A flood that kills nobody, in a country that does not ask for help, may appear in neither. The counts are of floods, not of how long they lasted or how much land they covered, and the estimates of the dead, the missing and the displaced differ between sources. Nothing here says what causes floods, or whether climate change is changing them: for that, a record must be built to measure it. And the table of the worst floods leaves out the records that did not agree with the other source, which may include a true record that EM-DAT lists differently.

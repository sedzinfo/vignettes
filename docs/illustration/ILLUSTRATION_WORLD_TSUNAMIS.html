Waves from the Sea: Where Tsunamis Start and Where They Strike Since 1900

Tsunamis since 1900 in NOAA’s global database: where they start, how far their waves travel, how tall they get, which were the deadliest, and whether there are more of them than there used to be.
Published

November 27, 2026

What is this?

A tsunami is a series of waves that starts when something moves a great deal of water at once, most often an earthquake under the sea. In the open ocean the waves are low and fast. Near a coast they grow, and they can flood it. This story looks at the tsunamis of the last 125 years and asks five questions:

  1. Where in the world do tsunamis start?
  2. Where do the waves strike, and how far do they travel?
  3. How big are they, and how often does one kill?
  4. Which were the deadliest?
  5. Are there more of them than there used to be?

For the earthquakes behind most tsunamis, see Earthquakes; for those that come from eruptions, Volcanoes; for all the hazards together, Natural Disasters.

The data come from the Global Historical Tsunami Database of the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), kept by its National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) (database). It lists tsunamis from 2000 BC to today, collected from reports, tide gauges and surveys. Its hazard-service interface gives it as two tables, downloaded here when the page is rendered, with no key:

  • The events: one row for each tsunami, with where its source was, what caused it, how reliable the report is, the highest water, and the deaths. We use the tsunamis since 1900, the first year of instrumentally located earthquakes (NOAA).
  • The runups: one row for each place where a tsunami’s waves were measured, with the height of the water, the distance from the source and the country. A runup is the maximum height of the water above the normal sea level (NOAA).

The meaning of every code below (validity, cause, region, kind of measurement) is from the help pages of NOAA’s own search tool (HazEL).

The record is of reports, not of every wave. A tsunami enters the database when someone saw it, measured it or wrote about it. Small waves far from people are missed, and how well they are recorded has changed over the years (section 5).

# the items of a page of NOAA's hazard service, or nothing when the request failed
noaa_items<-function(response) {
  if(is.null(response)||response$status_code!=200) {
    return(NULL)
  }
  result<-fromJSON(rawToChar(response$content))$items
  return(result)
}
# one table of NOAA's hazard service: the first page says how many pages there are, and the rest
# are asked for at the same time, five at once, because one page takes a few seconds
noaa<-function(table,filter="") {
  base<-paste0("https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazel/hazard-service/api/v1/tsunamis/",table,"?",filter,
               "&page=")
  first<-fromJSON(paste0(base,1))
  pages<-list(first$items)
  todo<-seq_len(first$totalPages)[-1]
  for(attempt in 1:3) {
    if(length(todo)==0) break
    got<-list()
    pool<-new_pool(total_con=5,host_con=5)
    for(p in todo) {
      local({
        page<-p
        curl_fetch_multi(paste0(base,page),
                         done=function(r) got[[as.character(page)]]<<-noaa_items(r),
                         fail=function(e) NULL,pool=pool)
      })
    }
    multi_run(pool=pool)
    pages<-c(pages,got)
    todo<-setdiff(todo,as.integer(names(got)[!sapply(got,is.null)]))
  }
  stopifnot(length(todo)==0)
  # the pages as one table: a column that a page lacks is empty there
  columns<-unique(unlist(lapply(pages,names)))
  result<-do.call(rbind,lapply(pages,function(x) {
    x[setdiff(columns,names(x))]<-NA
    x[columns]
  }))
  rownames(result)<-NULL
  return(result)
}

cause_group<-function(code) {
  result<-rep("Other or unknown",length(code))
  result[code%in%1:3]<-"Earthquake"
  result[code%in%4:7]<-"Volcano"
  result[code%in%8]<-"Landslide"

Only probable and definite tsunamis are kept. NOAA rates every report from -1 to 4. Since 1900 the database has 1,667 entries: 860 definite, 315 probable, 221 questionable, 188 very doubtful, 22 that only caused a seiche (a wave that sloshes in a lake or bay) and 61 erroneous. This page keeps the 1,175 definite and probable ones, 70% of the entries. The rest are too uncertain to count as tsunamis. Of those kept, 75 started in a lake, a reservoir or a river rather than the sea; they stay, and are named where they matter.

Deaths are those of the tsunami alone. The database gives two figures: the deaths of the tsunami, and a total for the whole event, which includes the earthquake or eruption that caused it. For the earthquake of Haiti in 2010 the total is 316,000 and the tsunami’s own figure 7. The two differ for 47 of the tsunamis that have both, so this page uses the tsunami’s own deaths, and only where a number is recorded (section 4).

The runups are those of the kept tsunamis, in places where the report was not doubtful. The runups table has 35,150 rows for all of history, and its filters do not cut it by year, so all of it is downloaded. 31,769 are of tsunamis since 1900. Of these, 561 belong to tsunamis that are not probable or definite, and 1,127 more are marked by NOAA as doubtful, or as waves that probably came from the weather or from an eruption’s air waves rather than from the sea. That leaves 30,081 places, for 1,027 tsunamis.

Places without a position are not on the map. 73 of the kept tsunamis have no latitude and longitude, so the map shows 1,102.

The latest year is incomplete. The database has tsunamis up to 2026, a year that is not over, so the counts over time (section 5) use the 126 full years, 1900 to 2025.

count_values<-function(x,name) {
  values<-sort(unique(x),method="radix",na.last=TRUE)
  result<-data.frame(values,vapply(values,function(v) sum(x%in%v),integer(1),USE.NAMES=FALSE))
  names(result)<-c(name,"n")
  return(result)
}

## What is this?

A tsunami is a series of waves that starts when something moves a great deal of water at once, most often an earthquake under the sea. In the open ocean the waves are low and fast. Near a coast they grow, and they can flood it. This story looks at the tsunamis of the last 125 years and asks five questions:

1. **Where in the world do tsunamis start?**
2. **Where do the waves strike, and how far do they travel?**
3. **How big are they, and how often does one kill?**
4. **Which were the deadliest?**
5. **Are there more of them than there used to be?**

For the earthquakes behind most tsunamis, see [Earthquakes](ILLUSTRATION_WORLD_EARTHQUAKES.html); for those that come from eruptions, [Volcanoes](ILLUSTRATION_WORLD_VOLCANOES.html); for all the hazards together, [Natural Disasters](ILLUSTRATION_NATURAL_DISASTERS.html).

The data come from the **Global Historical Tsunami Database** of the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), kept by its National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) ([database](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazard/tsu_db.shtml)). It lists tsunamis from 2000 BC to today, collected from reports, tide gauges and surveys. Its hazard-service interface gives it as two tables, downloaded here when the page is rendered, with no key:

- **The events**: one row for each tsunami, with where its source was, what caused it, how reliable the report is, the highest water, and the deaths. We use the tsunamis since 1900, the first year of instrumentally located earthquakes ([NOAA](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazard/tsunami-db-intro.html)).
- **The runups**: one row for each place where a tsunami's waves were measured, with the height of the water, the distance from the source and the country. A **runup** is the maximum height of the water above the normal sea level ([NOAA](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazard/tsunami-db-intro.html)).

The meaning of every code below (validity, cause, region, kind of measurement) is from the help pages of NOAA's own search tool ([HazEL](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazel/view/hazards/tsunami/event-search)).

**The record is of reports, not of every wave.** A tsunami enters the database when someone saw it, measured it or wrote about it. Small waves far from people are missed, and how well they are recorded has changed over the years (section 5).

noaa_items<-function(response) {
  if(is.null(response)||response$status_code!=200) {
    return(NULL)
  }
  result<-fromJSON(rawToChar(response$content))$items
  return(result)
}
# one table of NOAA's hazard service: the first page says how many pages there are, and the rest
# are asked for at the same time, five at once, because one page takes a few seconds
noaa<-function(table,filter="") {
  base<-paste0("https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/hazel/hazard-service/api/v1/tsunamis/",table,"?",filter,
               "&page=")
  first<-fromJSON(paste0(base,1))
  pages<-list("1"=first$items)
  todo<-seq_len(first$totalPages)[-1]
  for(attempt in 1:3) {
    if(length(todo)==0) break
    got<-list()
    pool<-new_pool(total_con=5,host_con=5)
    for(p in todo) {
      local({
        page<-p
        curl_fetch_multi(paste0(base,page),
                         done=function(r) got[[as.character(page)]]<<-noaa_items(r),
                         fail=function(e) NULL,pool=pool)
      })
    }
    multi_run(pool=pool)
    pages<-c(pages,got)
    todo<-setdiff(todo,as.integer(names(got)[!sapply(got,is.null)]))
  }
  stopifnot(length(todo)==0)
  # the pages in their order (they come in the order in which they are finished)
  pages<-pages[order(as.integer(names(pages)))]
  # the pages as one table: a column that a page lacks is empty there
  columns<-unique(unlist(lapply(pages,names)))
  result<-do.call(rbind,lapply(pages,function(x) {
    x[setdiff(columns,names(x))]<-NA
    x[columns]
  }))
  rownames(result)<-NULL
  return(result)
}

**Only probable and definite tsunamis are kept.** NOAA rates every report from -1 to 4. Since 1900 the database has `r number(n_all)` entries: `r number(count_status(4))` definite, `r number(count_status(3))` probable, `r number(count_status(2))` questionable, `r number(count_status(1))` very doubtful, `r number(count_status(0))` that only caused a seiche (a wave that sloshes in a lake or bay) and `r number(count_status(-1))` erroneous. This page keeps the `r number(n_kept)` definite and probable ones, `r percent(100*n_kept/n_all)` of the entries. The rest are too uncertain to count as tsunamis. Of those kept, `r number(n_lake)` started in a lake, a reservoir or a river rather than the sea; they stay, and are named where they matter.

**Deaths are those of the tsunami alone.** The database gives two figures: the deaths of the tsunami, and a total for the whole event, which includes the earthquake or eruption that caused it. For the earthquake of Haiti in 2010 the total is `r number(haiti$deathsTotal[1])` and the tsunami's own figure `r number(haiti$deaths[1])`. The two differ for `r number(nrow(total_ex))` of the tsunamis that have both, so this page uses the tsunami's own deaths, and only where a number is recorded (section 4).

**The runups are those of the kept tsunamis, in places where the report was not doubtful.** The runups table has `r number(n_runups_all)` rows for all of history, and its filters do not cut it by year, so all of it is downloaded. `r number(n_runups_since)` are of tsunamis since 1900. Of these, `r number(n_runups_dropped_events)` belong to tsunamis that are not probable or definite, and `r number(n_runups_doubtful)` more are marked by NOAA as doubtful, or as waves that probably came from the weather or from an eruption's air waves rather than from the sea. That leaves `r number(n_runups)` places, for `r number(n_runup_events)` tsunamis.

**Places without a position are not on the map.** `r number(n_kept-n_with_xy)` of the kept tsunamis have no latitude and longitude, so the map shows `r number(n_with_xy)`.

**The latest year is incomplete.** The database has tsunamis up to `r last_year`, a year that is not over, so the counts over time (section 5) use the `r n_full` full years, `r first_year` to `r last_year-1`.

## So, what does it mean?

1. **Tsunamis follow the earthquakes, and most of them are in the Pacific.** `r percent(ocean_in("Pacific Ocean"))` of the tsunamis with a region started in the Pacific; `r percent(cause_n("Earthquake","share"))` of all of them were caused by an earthquake.
2. **A few cross an ocean.** `r number(n_far)` tsunamis, `r percent(100*n_far/nrow(reach))` of those measured, struck `r number(far_km)` km or more from their source, and Hawaii received `r number(hawaii_far)` of them.
3. **The tallest waves come from landslides, the deadliest from earthquakes.** `r tall_by_cause[["Landslide"]]` of the `r nrow(tall)` tsunamis of 30 m or more were landslides alone, and the highest water was `r one(tallest$maxWaterHeight)` m.
4. **Deaths are concentrated in a few events.** Two tsunamis, off Sumatra in 2004 and off Japan in 2011, account for `r percent(top2_share)` of the deaths on record.
5. **More are recorded now, and the data cannot say whether more happen.** `r one(mean_years(yearly,early))` a year at first and `r one(mean_years(yearly,late))` lately, while the share with a measured height went from `r percent(measured[1])` to `r percent(measured[3])`.

**What this does not show.** The database lists the tsunamis that were reported, not all the waves of the sea, and it does not say how likely a tsunami is in any one place. The map shows the sources, not the coasts that are in danger. Doubtful reports are left out, and so is everything before `r first_year`, though the database goes back to 2000 BC. Deaths are a floor, and the heights mix the tide gauge, the eyewitness and the survey. The numbers of places for each country depend on how well each country measures its tsunamis.

For the earthquakes behind most tsunamis, see [Earthquakes](ILLUSTRATION_WORLD_EARTHQUAKES.html); for eruptions, [Volcanoes](ILLUSTRATION_WORLD_VOLCANOES.html); for all the hazards together, [Natural Disasters](ILLUSTRATION_NATURAL_DISASTERS.html).

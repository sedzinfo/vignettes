From Lignite to Sunshine: How Greece Makes Its Electricity

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Greek electricity in Eurostat data since 1990: how lignite gave way to gas, sun and wind, what changes with the seasons, how Greece compares with the EU, what households pay, and how Greece began to sell electricity abroad.

What is this?

For most of the last century Greece made its electricity by burning lignite, a soft brown coal dug from open-pit mines around Ptolemaida and Kozani in Western Macedonia and around Megalopoli in the Peloponnese. Today most of it comes from natural gas, the sun and the wind. This story follows the change with official data, in six questions:

  1. What does Greece make its electricity from?
  2. When did the sun and the wind overtake lignite?
  3. What changes with the seasons?
  4. How does Greece compare with the rest of the EU?
  5. What do households pay?
  6. Does Greece now sell electricity abroad?

All the data come from Eurostat, the statistical office of the EU. They are downloaded when the page is rendered, so a new render brings in the latest figures. Electricity is counted in terawatt hours (TWh), a billion kilowatt hours each, or in gigawatt hours (GWh), a million kilowatt hours each.

eurostat_values<-function(id,filters) {
  x<-get_eurostat(id,filters=filters,time_format="raw",cache=FALSE)
  result<-as.data.frame(x[!is.na(x$values),])
  result$time<-as.character(result$time)
  return(result)
}

# production by fuel, one column for each fuel and the rest (biomass, waste) as "Other", GWh
by_fuel<-function(x,codes) {
  times<-sort(unique(x$time))
  result<-data.frame(time=times)
  for(k in seq_along(codes)) {
    s<-x[x$siec==codes[k],]
    result[[fuels$fuel[k]]]<-s$values[match(times,s$time)]
  }
  total<-x[x$siec=="TOTAL",]
  result$total<-total$values[match(times,total$time)]
  result$Other<-result$total-rowSums(result[fuels$fuel],na.rm=TRUE)
  return(result)
}

# labels just right of the plot, at the given heights, moved apart where they would touch
spread<-function(y,gap) {
  o<-order(y)
  for(k in seq_along(o)[-1])
    y[o[k]]<-max(y[o[k]],y[o[k-1]]+gap)
  return(y)
}
right_labels<-function(y,text) {
  result<-lapply(seq_along(y),function(k) {
    list(x=1.01,y=y[k],text=text[k],xref="paper",yref="y",xanchor="left",showarrow=FALSE,
         font=list(size=12,color=dark_gray))
  })
  return(result)
}

1. What does Greece make its electricity from?

Mostly from natural gas, the sun and the wind; hardly any more from lignite. In 2024 Greece produced 57.9 TWh of electricity. Natural gas made 37% of it, the wind 21%, the sun 19% and lignite 6%. In 1990 lignite had made 72%, and the sun and the wind nothing at all.

2. When did the sun and the wind overtake lignite?

In 2020. That year the sun and the wind made 29% of the electricity of Greece, lignite 14%. Lignite had made 73% in 1994; by 2024 it was down to 6%. Natural gas, which hardly made any electricity in 1990, took over much of the rest: it now makes 37%, and almost all of that gas comes from abroad, by pipeline and by ship.

3. What changes with the seasons?

The sun and the wind make the largest share in spring, when there is plenty of both and little need for heating or cooling. In 2023–2025 they made on average 47% of the electricity of April, but 33% of December. Their best month so far was October 2022, with 53%.

The monthly figures are net production and Eurostat’s first estimates, so they do not add up exactly to the yearly ones above.

4. How does Greece compare with the rest of the EU?

The EU sets its targets with another measure: the share of renewable energy in the electricity a country consumes, counting hydro and biomass as well as the sun and the wind, and averaging hydro and wind over several years so that one wet or windy year does not count too much. On that measure Greece went from 7.8% in 2004 to 51.2% in 2024, 10th of the 27 countries of the EU and above its average of 47.5%. Eurostat’s first, provisional figure for 2025 is 60.9%.

5. What do households pay?

Less than the average household of the EU, in every half year since 2007. In the energy crisis of 2021–2023, sharpened by the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the price of a kilowatt hour for a Greek household rose to €0.244 in the second half of 2022 (with all taxes, in the middle band of 2,500 to 5,000 kWh a year), against €0.277 in the EU. In the first half of 2026 it was €0.240, against €0.283.

6. Does Greece now sell electricity abroad?

Yes, since 2024. For three decades Greece bought more electricity from its neighbours than it sold them: in 2019 it bought 9.9 TWh more than it sold. In 2024 it sold more than it bought, for the first time since 2000, and in 2025 it sold 3.0 TWh more: 6.4 TWh went abroad and 3.4 TWh came in.

# production by fuel, one column for each fuel and the rest (biomass, waste) as "Other", GWh
by_fuel<-function(x,codes) {
  times<-sort(unique(x$time))
  result<-data.frame(time=times)
  for(k in seq_along(codes)) {
    s<-x[x$siec==codes[k],]
    result[[fuels$fuel[k]]]<-s$values[match(times,s$time)]
  }
  total<-x[x$siec=="TOTAL",]
  result$total<-total$values[match(times,total$time)]
  result$Other<-result$total-rowSums(result[fuels$fuel],na.rm=TRUE)
  return(result)
}

## 1. What does Greece make its electricity from?

**Mostly from natural gas, the sun and the wind; hardly any more from lignite.** In `r last` Greece produced `r twh(yearly$total[yearly$year==last])` of electricity. Natural gas made `r percent(at("Natural gas",last))` of it, the wind `r percent(at("Wind",last))`, the sun `r percent(at("Solar",last))` and lignite `r percent(at("Lignite",last))`. In 1990 lignite had made `r percent(at("Lignite",1990))`, and the sun and the wind nothing at all.

## 2. When did the sun and the wind overtake lignite?

**In `r crossing`.** That year the sun and the wind made `r percent(at("sun_wind",crossing))` of the electricity of Greece, lignite `r percent(at("Lignite",crossing))`. Lignite had made `r percent(max(shares$Lignite))` in `r shares$year[which.max(shares$Lignite)]`; by `r last` it was down to `r percent(at("Lignite",last))`. Natural gas, which hardly made any electricity in 1990, took over much of the rest: it now makes `r percent(at("Natural gas",last))`, and almost all of that gas comes from abroad, by pipeline and by ship.

## 3. What changes with the seasons?

**The sun and the wind make the largest share in `r season[best]``r if(season[best]%in%c("spring","autumn")) ", when there is plenty of both and little need for heating or cooling"`.** In `r paste(range(recent_years),collapse="–")` they made on average `r percent(sun_wind_month[best])` of the electricity of `r month.name[best]`, but `r percent(sun_wind_month[worst])` of `r month.name[worst]`. Their best month so far was `r month_label(monthly$time[which.max(monthly$sun_wind)])`, with `r percent(max(monthly$sun_wind))`.

The monthly figures are net production and Eurostat's first estimates, so they do not add up exactly to the yearly ones above.

## 4. How does Greece compare with the rest of the EU?

The EU sets its targets with another measure: the share of **renewable energy in the electricity a country consumes**, counting hydro and biomass as well as the sun and the wind, and averaging hydro and wind over several years so that one wet or windy year does not count too much. On that measure Greece went from `r percent(ren_at("EL",2004),1)` in 2004 to **`r percent(ren_at("EL",ren_year),1)` in `r ren_year`**, `r ordinal(match("EL",eu_renewables$geo[eu_renewables$geo!="EU27_2020"]))` of the 27 countries of the EU and above its average of `r percent(ren_at("EU27_2020",ren_year),1)`.`r if(ren_latest>ren_year) paste0(" Eurostat's first, provisional figure for ",ren_latest," is ",percent(ren_at("EL",ren_latest),1),".")`

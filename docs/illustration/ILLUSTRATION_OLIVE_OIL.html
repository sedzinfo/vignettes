Why Did Olive Oil Get So Expensive? Prices, Harvests and the Drought in Spain

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
The price of extra virgin olive oil at the markets of Greece, Spain and Italy every week since 2010, the harvests of the four big producers of the EU, and what shoppers paid: why the price tripled in 2023 and 2024, and where it stands now.

What is this?

At the start of 2024 a kilo of extra virgin olive oil cost three times as much at the markets of Greece as it had a few years before. Greek families, who use more olive oil than almost anyone, felt it in the shops. This story follows the price and its causes with official data, in five questions:

  1. How much did olive oil cost?
  2. Why did the price triple?
  3. Why did Greek prices rise, when Greece had a good harvest?
  4. Did shoppers pay more?
  5. Where is the price now?

The prices and the harvests come from the agri-food data portal of the European Commission, which collects every week the prices that producers get at the main markets of each country. What shoppers paid comes from Eurostat, the statistical office of the EU. All are downloaded when the page is rendered.

# the lines of a file on the web, tried again a few times: the servers sometimes refuse a connection
# or take too long to answer
read_lines<-function(url,tries=4) {
  for(k in seq_len(tries)) {
    result<-tryCatch(readLines(url,warn=FALSE),error=function(e) NULL)
    if(!is.null(result))
      return(result)
    Sys.sleep(10)
  }
  stop("Could not download ",url)
}
portal<-"https://ec.europa.eu/agrifood/api/oliveOil/"

# the price of extra virgin olive oil every week since 2010, the average of the markets of Greece,
# Spain and Italy, euros a kilo (the portal gives euros for 100 kg)
weekly<-jsonlite::fromJSON(paste(read_lines(paste0(portal,"prices?memberStateCodes=EL,ES,IT",
                                                   "&beginDate=01/01/2010",
                                                   "&products=Extra%20virgin%20olive%20oil",
                                                   "%20(up%20to%200.8%25)",
                                                   "&markets=Average%20national%20price")),
                                 collapse=""))
weekly<-data.frame(country=weekly$memberStateCode,
                   date=as.Date(weekly$beginDate,format="%d/%m/%Y"),
                   price=as.numeric(gsub("[€,]","",weekly$price))/100)
weekly<-weekly[order(weekly$country,weekly$date),]
greece<-weekly[weekly$country=="EL",]
spain<-weekly[weekly$country=="ES",]
# the normal years before the crisis, and the dearest week
normal<-greece$price[format(greece$date,"%Y")>="2015"&format(greece$date,"%Y")<="2021"]
base<-mean(normal)
peak_of<-function(country) {
  x<-weekly[weekly$country==country,]
  result<-x[which.max(x$price),]
  return(result)
}
peak<-peak_of("EL")
latest<-greece[nrow(greece),]
# how closely the Greek and Spanish prices of the same weeks move together
same_weeks<-merge(greece,spain,by="date",suffixes=c("_greece","_spain"))
together<-cor(same_weeks$price_greece,same_weeks$price_spain)

# the harvests of the four big producers of the EU, thousand tonnes of olive oil, by marketing year
# (October to September); the last years are estimates
harvests<-jsonlite::fromJSON(paste(read_lines(paste0(portal,"production",
                                                     "?memberStateCodes=EL,ES,IT,PT",
                                                     "&granularity=annual")),
                                   collapse=""))
harvests<-data.frame(country=harvests$memberStateCode,season=harvests$marketingYear,
                     tonnes=harvests$yearProductionQuantity,
                     estimate=harvests$isEstimated=="Y")
harvests<-harvests[order(harvests$country,harvests$season),]
crop<-function(country,season) {
  result<-harvests$tonnes[harvests$country==country&harvests$season==season]
  return(result)
}
# Spain's share of the four in the seasons before the drought that all four reported
seasons<-names(which(table(harvests$season[!harvests$estimate])==4))
before_drought<-seasons[seasons<"2022/23"]
four<-harvests[harvests$season%in%before_drought,]
spain_share<-100*sum(four$tonnes[four$country=="ES"])/sum(four$tonnes)

# what shoppers paid: Eurostat's index of the consumer prices of olive oil and of all food,
# 2015 = 100, every month. It stops in December 2025, when Eurostat moved to a new classification
# in which olive oil is no longer a group of its own
shops<-get_eurostat("prc_hicp_midx",filters=list(geo=c("EL","EU27_2020"),unit="I15",
                                                 coicop=c("CP01153","CP011")),
                    time_format="raw",cache=FALSE)
shops<-as.data.frame(shops[!is.na(shops$values),])
shops$date<-as.Date(paste0(shops$time,"-01"))
shops<-shops[order(shops$date),]
index<-function(geo,coicop,month) {
  result<-shops$values[shops$geo==geo&shops$coicop==coicop&format(shops$date,"%Y-%m")==month]
  return(result)
}
greek_oil<-shops[shops$geo=="EL"&shops$coicop=="CP01153",]

1. How much did olive oil cost?

About €2.98 a kilo in 2015–2021, and €8.91 at the peak. These are the prices that producers got for extra virgin olive oil at the main markets of each country, in bulk, before bottling, transport and the shop’s margin. In the week of 8 January 2024 the average of the Greek markets reached €8.91 a kilo, 3.0 times the average of 2015–2021. Spain and Italy peaked in the same month, at €9.03 and €9.61.

2. Why did the price triple?

Because Spain, which makes most of Europe’s olive oil, lost more than half of its harvest to drought. In the seasons from 2013/14 to 2021/22, Spain made 67% of the olive oil of the four big producers of the EU. After a year of drought and record heat, its harvest fell from 1,491,000 tonnes in 2021/22 to 666,000 tonnes in 2022/23, 55% less, and stayed low in 2023/24 with 855,000 tonnes. Buyers bid up the price of what there was.

3. Why did Greek prices rise, when Greece had a good harvest?

Because olive oil is sold in one European market. Greece made 346,000 tonnes in 2022/23, more than in an average season; yet the Greek price rose with the Spanish one. Traders buy where the oil is cheapest, and much Greek oil is sold in bulk to Italian bottlers. So the prices of the three countries move together: the weekly prices of Greece and Spain since 2010 have a correlation of 0.99 (1 would mean that they always move together). Then, in 2023/24, the Greek harvest fell too, to 192,000 tonnes.

4. Did shoppers pay more?

Yes, a lot more, though less than the markets. Eurostat’s index of the price of olive oil in Greek shops rose by 139% from January 2021 to its peak in July 2024. The price at the markets is only part of what a shopper pays: bottling, transport, taxes and the margins of the shops are the rest, and they did not triple. All food in Greece rose by 35% from January 2021 to December 2025.

5. Where is the price now?

Back near where it was before the drought. Spain’s harvest recovered in 2024/25, to 1,421,000 tonnes, and the price fell with it. In the week of 9 November 2026 a kilo of extra virgin olive oil fetched €3.26 at the Greek markets, 1.1 times the average of 2015–2021 and 63% below the peak. In the shops, prices fall more slowly: in December 2025 the Greek index of olive oil was still 45% above January 2021.

The lesson of these years is that the price of olive oil depends less on any one country than on the weather of Spain, and as droughts and heatwaves become more common around the Mediterranean (see Athens Is Getting Hotter), years like 2023 may come again.

## 2. Why did the price triple?

**Because Spain, which makes most of Europe's olive oil, lost more than half of its harvest to drought.** In the seasons from `r before_drought[1]` to `r tail(before_drought,1)`, Spain made `r round(spain_share)`% of the olive oil of the four big producers of the EU. After a year of drought and record heat, its harvest fell from `r thousand(crop("ES","2021/22"))` in 2021/22 to `r thousand(crop("ES","2022/23"))` in 2022/23, `r round(100*(1-crop("ES","2022/23")/crop("ES","2021/22")))`% less, and stayed low in 2023/24 with `r thousand(crop("ES","2023/24"))`. Buyers bid up the price of what there was.

## 3. Why did Greek prices rise, when Greece had a good harvest?

**Because olive oil is sold in one European market.** Greece made `r thousand(crop("EL","2022/23"))` in 2022/23, `r if(crop("EL","2022/23")>mean(harvests$tonnes[harvests$country=="EL"&!harvests$estimate])) "more than in an average season" else "about an average harvest"`; yet the Greek price rose with the Spanish one. Traders buy where the oil is cheapest, and much Greek oil is sold in bulk to Italian bottlers. So the prices of the three countries move together: the weekly prices of Greece and Spain since 2010 have a correlation of `r format(round(together,2),nsmall=2)` (1 would mean that they always move together). Then, in 2023/24, the Greek harvest fell too, to `r thousand(crop("EL","2023/24"))`.

## 4. Did shoppers pay more?

**Yes, a lot more, though less than the markets.** Eurostat's index of the price of olive oil in Greek shops rose by `r round(100*(max(greek_oil$values)/index("EL","CP01153","2021-01")-1))`% from January 2021 to its peak in `r format(greek_oil$date[which.max(greek_oil$values)],"%B %Y")`. The price at the markets is only part of what a shopper pays: bottling, transport, taxes and the margins of the shops are the rest, and they did not triple. All food in Greece rose by `r round(100*(index("EL","CP011",format(max(shops$date),"%Y-%m"))/index("EL","CP011","2021-01")-1))`% from January 2021 to `r format(max(shops$date),"%B %Y")`.

## 5. Where is the price now?

**Back near where it was before the drought.** Spain's harvest recovered in 2024/25, to `r if(harvests$estimate[harvests$country=="ES"&harvests$season=="2024/25"]) "an estimated "``r thousand(crop("ES","2024/25"))`, and the price fell with it. In the week of `r day(latest$date)` a kilo of extra virgin olive oil fetched `r euro(latest$price)` at the Greek markets, `r format(round(latest$price/base,1),nsmall=1)` times the average of 2015–2021 and `r round(100*(1-latest$price/peak$price))`% below the peak. In the shops, prices fall more slowly: in `r format(max(greek_oil$date),"%B %Y")` the Greek index of olive oil was still `r round(100*(tail(greek_oil$values,1)/index("EL","CP01153","2021-01")-1))`% above January 2021.

The lesson of these years is that the price of olive oil depends less on any one country than on the weather of Spain, and as droughts and heatwaves become more common around the Mediterranean (see [Athens Is Getting Hotter](ILLUSTRATION_ATHENS_HEAT.Rmd)), years like 2023 may come again.

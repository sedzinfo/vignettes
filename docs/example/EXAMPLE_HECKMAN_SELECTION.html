Heckman Selection Model: What Would the Women Who Did Not Work Have Earned?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
The Heckman sample selection model in R, by hand and with sampleSelection, on Mroz’s classic data of 753 married women of whom 428 worked for pay: wages that are seen only for a selected group, the two-step estimator with the inverse Mills ratio, maximum likelihood, testing for selection, why exclusion restrictions decide everything, a simulation with a known truth, predicted wage offers, how it differs from Tobit and two-part models, and APA reporting.

The question: wages that nobody paid

How much more does a woman earn for each year of education? In 1975 a study asked 753 married American women. But only 428 of them worked for pay. For the other 325 there is no wage. Not because the question was left out: there was none to report.

A regression of the wage on education, for the women who worked, describes the women who worked. But the question is usually about all women: what would education be worth to a woman who is at home now? If those who work are a special group, if for example they are the ones who were offered a good wage, then the sample is selected by the very thing we want to explain, and the regression may be wrong.

James Heckman (1979) proposed a model for this situation, with two equations: one that decides who is in the sample, and one for the outcome. It brought him a Nobel Prize, and it is one of the most used, and most misused, models of social science. This page shows what it does, and what it can and cannot repair.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: two equations

The outcome equation, for every woman, whether she works or not. It is the wage that she is or would be offered:

\[y_i^* = x_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i\]

The selection equation. She works if the advantage of working, \(d_i^*\), is positive:

\[d_i^* = z_i^\top \gamma + u_i, \qquad \text{we see } y_i = y_i^* \text{ only if } d_i^* > 0\]

Step 2: the errors may be correlated

\[\begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_i \\ u_i \end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left(0, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2 & \rho\sigma \\ \rho\sigma & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right)\]

\(u_i\) is whatever makes her more likely to work than \(z_i\) says: ambition, health, a well-connected aunt. \(\varepsilon_i\) is whatever makes her wage higher than \(x_i\) says: ambition, health, a well-connected aunt. If the two are related (\(\rho \ne 0\)), then those who work have, on average, errors \(\varepsilon\) that are not zero. This is the whole problem. If \(\rho = 0\), selection may depend on everything in \(z\), and OLS on the workers is still fine.

Step 3: the bias of OLS is a missing predictor

For the women who work, the expected error is

\[E(\varepsilon_i \mid u_i > -z_i^\top\gamma) = \rho\sigma\,\lambda_i, \qquad \lambda_i = \frac{\phi(z_i^\top\gamma)}{\Phi(z_i^\top\gamma)}\]

\(\lambda_i\) is the inverse Mills ratio. It is large for women who work although the model says they hardly would (they must have a large \(u\)), and near 0 for those who would work anyway. So

\[E(y_i \mid \text{works}) = x_i^\top\beta + (\rho\sigma)\,\lambda_i\]

OLS leaves out \(\lambda_i\). In Heckman’s words, selection bias is a specification error: an omitted variable.

Step 4: the two-step estimator

  1. Fit a probit regression of working on \(z\), for all the women. Compute \(\hat\lambda_i\).
  2. Fit OLS of the wage on \(x\) and \(\hat\lambda\), for the women who work.

The coefficient of \(\hat\lambda\) estimates \(\rho\sigma\). Its t test is a test of whether there is selection at all. The ordinary standard errors of step 2 are wrong, because \(\hat\lambda\) is estimated and the errors have unequal variances.

Step 5: maximum likelihood

Both equations at once. A woman who does not work contributes \(P(d^* \le 0)\). A woman who works contributes the density of her wage, times the probability of working given that wage:

\[\log L = \sum_{d_i = 0} \log \Phi(-z_i^\top\gamma) + \sum_{d_i = 1} \left[ \log \frac{1}{\sigma}\phi\!\left(\frac{e_i}{\sigma}\right) + \log \Phi\!\left(\frac{z_i^\top\gamma + \rho\, e_i/\sigma}{\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\right) \right]\]

with \(e_i = y_i - x_i^\top\beta\).

It is more precise than the two steps if the bivariate normal model is right.

Step 6: exclusion restrictions

If \(x\) and \(z\) contain the same variables, the only thing that tells \(\lambda_i\) from the predictors in \(x\) is its curvature, and over most of its range it is nearly a straight line. The model is then identified by the assumption of normality alone, which is not much. To identify it properly there must be at least one variable in \(z\) that is not in \(x\): something that makes a woman work, but does not change the wage that she would be offered. Here: the income of the rest of the family, her age, and her children. Whether employers really did not care about a woman’s children in 1975 is a question that the model cannot answer.

Z <- model.matrix(probit); X <- model.matrix(~ educ + exper + I(exper^2), data = women)
y <- ifelse(works, women$logwage, 0)
minus_loglik <- function(par) {
  g <- par[1:ncol(Z)]
  b <- par[ncol(Z) + 1:ncol(X)]
  s <- exp(par[length(par) - 1])
  r <- tanh(par[length(par)])
  a <- drop(Z %*% g)
  e <- (y - drop(X %*% b)) / s
  -sum(ifelse(
    works,
    dnorm(e, log = TRUE) - log(s) + pnorm((a + r * e) / sqrt(1 - r^2), log.p = TRUE),
    pnorm(-a, log.p = TRUE)
  ))
}

If \(\rho = 0\), the likelihood of the model is that of the probit times that of the OLS regression. So the likelihood-ratio test of selection compares the two: \(\chi^2\)(1) = 0.03, p = .858. We find no sign of selection bias. The return to a year of education is 0.107 with OLS, 0.109 with the two steps and 0.108 with maximum likelihood.

That the correction changes nothing is a common result, and a legitimate one. It does not mean that the workers are like the others: they differ in education, experience and children. It means that what is left, after these, seems not to be related to the wage. Or that our restrictions cannot show it.

A known truth

A simulation with the real predictors shows both sides. The true return to education is 0.10. We set the correlation \(\rho\) of the errors to 0, 0.5 and 0.8, and estimate with OLS, with the two steps, and with the two steps without exclusion restrictions (the data are generated with them):

set.seed(41)
beta  <- c(-0.5, 0.10, 0.04, -0.0008); s0 <- 0.66; gamma0 <- coef(probit)
reps  <- 500
heck  <- function(yy, d, Zm) {                                         # two-step, by hand, fast
  p  <- suppressWarnings(glm.fit(Zm, d, family = binomial("probit")))
  a  <- drop(Zm %*% p$coefficients); m <- dnorm(a) / pnorm(a)
  unname(lm.fit(cbind(X[d == 1, ], m[d == 1]), yy[d == 1])$coefficients[2])
}
one <- function(rho) {
  u <- rnorm(nrow(X))
  e <- s0 * (rho * u + sqrt(1 - rho^2) * rnorm(nrow(X)))
  d <- as.integer(drop(Z %*% gamma0) + u > 0)
  yy <- drop(X %*% beta) + e
  c(
    OLS = unname(lm.fit(X[d == 1, ], yy[d == 1])$coefficients[2]),
    two_step = heck(yy, d, Z),
    no_exclusions = heck(yy, d, X)
  )
}
sims <- do.call(

      method = colnames(r),
      mean = colMeans(r),
      SD = apply(r, 2, sd),
      RMSE = sqrt(colMeans((r - 0.10)^2)),
      row.names = NULL
    )
  })
)

8. Things to keep in mind

  • Find an exclusion restriction before you run the model, and defend it. Without one, do not use the Heckman correction (Puhani, 2000; Bushway et al., 2007).
  • The predictors of the outcome belong in the selection equation too. Leaving one out biases everything.
  • It is not a cure for every missing outcome. If the outcome is missing for reasons that are in your predictors, OLS or multiple imputation is better. Heckman’s model is for missingness that depends on the unseen part of the outcome.
  • Normality carries a lot of weight. There are versions with other distributions, and semiparametric ones that need stronger exclusion restrictions.
  • A non-significant \(\hat\lambda\) is not proof that there is no selection. With weak exclusions the test has little power.
  • The two steps need special standard errors. The package has them; OLS with a column \(\hat\lambda\) has not.
  • Bounds. Without any model one can still say something. If the missing outcomes lie within known limits, the answer lies within limits (Manski, 1989). They are often wide, and that is the honest measure of what the data say.

## The question: wages that nobody paid

How much more does a woman earn for each year of education? In 1975 a study asked 753 married American women. But only 428 of them worked for pay. For the other 325 there is **no wage**. Not because the question was left out: there was none to report.

A regression of the wage on education, for the women who worked, describes the women who worked. But the question is usually about **all** women: what would education be worth to a woman who is at home now? If those who work are a special group, if for example they are the ones who were **offered a good wage**, then the sample is selected by the very thing we want to explain, and the regression may be wrong.

James Heckman (1979) proposed a model for this situation, with two equations: one that decides **who is in the sample**, and one for the **outcome**. It brought him a Nobel Prize, and it is one of the most used, and most misused, models of social science. This page shows what it does, and what it can and cannot repair.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: two equations

The **outcome** equation, for every woman, whether she works or not. It is the wage that she is or would be offered:

$$y_i^* = x_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i$$

The **selection** equation. She works if the advantage of working, $d_i^*$, is positive:

$$d_i^* = z_i^\top \gamma + u_i, \qquad \text{we see } y_i = y_i^* \text{ only if } d_i^* > 0$$

### Step 2: the errors may be correlated

$$\begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_i \\ u_i \end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left(0, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2 & \rho\sigma \\ \rho\sigma & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right)$$

$u_i$ is whatever makes her more likely to work than $z_i$ says: ambition, health, a well-connected aunt. $\varepsilon_i$ is whatever makes her wage higher than $x_i$ says: ambition, health, a well-connected aunt. If the two are related ($\rho \ne 0$), then those who work have, on average, errors $\varepsilon$ that are not zero. **This is the whole problem.** If $\rho = 0$, selection may depend on everything in $z$, and OLS on the workers is still fine.

### Step 3: the bias of OLS is a missing predictor

For the women who work, the expected error is

$$E(\varepsilon_i \mid u_i > -z_i^\top\gamma) = \rho\sigma\,\lambda_i, \qquad \lambda_i = \frac{\phi(z_i^\top\gamma)}{\Phi(z_i^\top\gamma)}$$

$\lambda_i$ is the **inverse Mills ratio**. It is large for women who work although the model says they hardly would (they must have a large $u$), and near 0 for those who would work anyway. So

$$E(y_i \mid \text{works}) = x_i^\top\beta + (\rho\sigma)\,\lambda_i$$

OLS leaves out $\lambda_i$. In Heckman's words, selection bias is a **specification error**: an omitted variable.

### Step 4: the two-step estimator

1. Fit a **probit** regression of working on $z$, for all the women. Compute $\hat\lambda_i$.
2. Fit OLS of the wage on $x$ **and** $\hat\lambda$, for the women who work.

The coefficient of $\hat\lambda$ estimates $\rho\sigma$. Its *t* test is a test of whether there is selection at all. The ordinary standard errors of step 2 are wrong, because $\hat\lambda$ is estimated and the errors have unequal variances.

### Step 5: maximum likelihood

Both equations at once. A woman who does not work contributes $P(d^* \le 0)$. A woman who works contributes the density of her wage, times the probability of working **given that wage**:

$$\log L = \sum_{d_i = 0} \log \Phi(-z_i^\top\gamma) + \sum_{d_i = 1} \left[ \log \frac{1}{\sigma}\phi\!\left(\frac{e_i}{\sigma}\right) + \log \Phi\!\left(\frac{z_i^\top\gamma + \rho\, e_i/\sigma}{\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\right) \right]$$

with $e_i = y_i - x_i^\top\beta$.

It is more precise than the two steps if the bivariate normal model is right.

### Step 6: exclusion restrictions

If $x$ and $z$ contain the same variables, the only thing that tells $\lambda_i$ from the predictors in $x$ is its **curvature**, and over most of its range it is nearly a straight line. The model is then identified by the assumption of normality alone, which is not much. To identify it properly there must be at least one variable in $z$ that is **not** in $x$: something that makes a woman work, but does not change the wage that she would be offered. Here: the income of the rest of the family, her age, and her children. Whether employers really did not care about a woman's children in 1975 is a question that the model cannot answer.

### A known truth

A simulation with the real predictors shows both sides. The true return to education is 0.10. We set the correlation $\rho$ of the errors to 0, 0.5 and 0.8, and estimate with OLS, with the two steps, and with the two steps **without** exclusion restrictions (the data are generated with them):

```{r simulation}
set.seed(41)

reps  <- 500
heck  <- function(yy, d, Zm) {                                         # two-step, by hand, fast
  p  <- suppressWarnings(glm.fit(Zm, d, family = binomial("probit")))
  a  <- drop(Zm %*% p$coefficients); m <- dnorm(a) / pnorm(a)
  unname(lm.fit(cbind(X[d == 1, ], m[d == 1]), yy[d == 1])$coefficients[2])
}
one <- function(rho) {
  u <- rnorm(nrow(X))
  e <- s0 * (rho * u + sqrt(1 - rho^2) * rnorm(nrow(X)))
  d <- as.integer(drop(Z %*% gamma0) + u > 0)
  yy <- drop(X %*% beta) + e
  c(
    OLS = unname(lm.fit(X[d == 1, ], yy[d == 1])$coefficients[2]),
    two_step = heck(yy, d, Z),
    no_exclusions = heck(yy, d, X)
  )
}
sims <- do.call(
  rbind,
  lapply(c(0, 0.5, 0.8), function(rho) {

      rho,
      method = colnames(r),

      RMSE = sqrt(colMeans((r - 0.10)^2)),

    )

)
print(
```

## 7. What would the others have earned?

The outcome equation gives a wage offer to every woman. For the women at home there are two predictions: the **unconditional** one, $x^\top\beta$, for any woman with such education and experience, and the one **given that she does not work**, which uses the selection term with the opposite sign:

$$E(y_i^* \mid \text{does not work}) = x_i^\top\beta - \rho\sigma\,\frac{\phi(z_i^\top\gamma)}{1 - \Phi(z_i^\top\gamma)}$$

```{r predictions}
xb      <- drop(X %*% coef(ml)[9:12])
rs      <- coef(ml)[["rho"]] * coef(ml)[["sigma"]]
a       <- drop(Z %*% coef(ml)[1:8])
offer   <- ifelse(works, xb + rs * dnorm(a) / pnorm(a), xb - rs * dnorm(a) / pnorm(-a))
ols_hat <- drop(X %*% coef(ols))
wages <- data.frame(group = c("working", "not working"),
                    observed      = c(mean(women$logwage[works]), NA),
                    unconditional = c(mean(xb[works]), mean(xb[!works])),
                    conditional   = c(mean(offer[works]), mean(offer[!works])),
                    OLS           = c(mean(ols_hat[works]), mean(ols_hat[!works])))
print(cbind(group = wages$group, round(wages[, -1], 3)), row.names = FALSE)
gap <- 1 - exp(mean(offer[!works]) - mean(offer[works]))
gap
```

On this model, the women who did not work would have been offered wages `r pct(gap)` lower than those of the women who worked, nearly all of it because of less experience and education. With $\rho$ so close to zero, the selection term adds almost nothing, and OLS says the same. The log wage is predicted; for dollars one also needs $e^{\sigma^2/2}$.

## 8. Things to keep in mind

- **Find an exclusion restriction before you run the model**, and defend it. Without one, do not use the Heckman correction (Puhani, 2000; Bushway et al., 2007).
- **The predictors of the outcome belong in the selection equation too.** Leaving one out biases everything.
- **It is not a cure for every missing outcome.** If the outcome is missing for reasons that are in your predictors, OLS or [multiple imputation](EXAMPLE_MISSING_DATA.Rmd) is better. Heckman's model is for missingness that depends on the **unseen** part of the outcome.
- **Normality** carries a lot of weight. There are versions with other distributions, and semiparametric ones that need stronger exclusion restrictions.
- **A non-significant $\hat\lambda$** is not proof that there is no selection. With weak exclusions the test has little power.
- **The two steps need special standard errors.** The package has them; OLS with a column $\hat\lambda$ has not.
- **Bounds.** Without any model one can still say something. If the missing outcomes lie within known limits, the answer lies within limits (Manski, 1989). They are often wide, and that is the honest measure of what the data say.

| Situation | Model |
|---|---|
| Values pile up at a limit because they cannot be recorded beyond it | [Tobit](EXAMPLE_TOBIT.Rmd) |
| Cases beyond a limit of the outcome are missing | [Truncated regression](EXAMPLE_TRUNCATED_REGRESSION.Rmd) |
| Outcome seen only for a group selected by **another** decision, related to the outcome's error; interest in everybody | **Heckman** |
| A real zero for some (no purchase), and an amount for the others; interest in the observed outcome | Two-part (hurdle) model |

**Method** (data analysis):

> Wages were observed only for the `r sum(works)` of `r nrow(women)` married women who worked for pay in 1975 (Mroz, 1987). We estimated the determinants of the log hourly wage with Heckman's (1979) sample selection model by maximum likelihood. The wage equation contained education, experience and its square; the selection equation contained these and the income of the other family members, age, and the numbers of children under 6 and between 6 and 18, which we assumed to affect the decision to work but not the wage offered. Selection was tested with a likelihood-ratio test of ρ = 0, and the results were compared with ordinary least squares on the working women and with the two-step estimator. Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`) with the package sampleSelection (Toomet & Henningsen, 2008).

## 10. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | The outcome is seen only for a group selected by a decision that is related to the outcome's error |
| **The model** | A probit for selection and a regression for the outcome, with correlated normal errors |
| **The bias** | A missing predictor: $\rho\sigma\lambda$, with $\lambda$ the inverse Mills ratio |
| **How (R)** | `sampleSelection::selection(d ~ z1 + z2 + x, y ~ x, data, method = "ml")` or `"2step"` |
| **Needs** | At least one variable that moves selection but not the outcome |
| **Test** | $\rho = 0$: likelihood ratio, or the *t* test of $\hat\lambda$ |
| **If $\rho = 0$** | OLS on those who are seen is right, and more precise |
| **Without exclusions** | Identified by normality alone: do not |
| **Related pages** | [Truncated regression](EXAMPLE_TRUNCATED_REGRESSION.Rmd), [Tobit](EXAMPLE_TOBIT.Rmd), [missing data](EXAMPLE_MISSING_DATA.Rmd) |

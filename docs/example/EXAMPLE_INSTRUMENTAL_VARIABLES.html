Instrumental Variables: What Is a Year of School Worth?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Instrumental variables and two-stage least squares in R with ivreg, on Card’s 3,010 young men and the college near home: why least squares is wrong when something unseen matters, the conditions for an instrument, the Wald estimator and two-stage least squares by hand, why the second stage gives wrong standard errors, the first-stage F, Wu-Hausman and Sargan tests by hand, weak instruments in a simulation and the Anderson-Rubin interval, whose effect it is, how small a violation would change the answer, a control that is not innocent, and APA reporting.

The question: school, or what comes with it?

People with more schooling earn more. Is it the school that makes the difference? Or would the same persons have earned more anyway, because they are able, hard-working, or from families that help? Nobody can measure all of that. So adding control variables, the remedy of ordinary regression, does not finish the job.

An instrumental variable (an instrument) takes another way. Find something that

  • pushes people towards more schooling,
  • has nothing to do with their ability or family,
  • and touches their wages in no other way than through schooling.

Then compare the wages of those who were pushed and those who were not. Whatever difference there is, must have come through schooling.

David Card (1993) proposed the distance to a college. A boy who grew up near a four-year college could live at home while studying. For some boys that decided the matter. This page repeats his analysis, with every step by hand, and then asks how far one can trust it. The second part is the more important one: the conditions above cannot be proved from the data.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: why least squares is wrong

The true equation has a variable \(u\) that we cannot see, which is also related to schooling \(s\):

\[\log w = \beta\, s + u, \qquad \text{Cov}(s, u) \ne 0\]

Least squares attributes to \(s\) everything that comes along with it, \(\hat\beta_{OLS} \to \beta + \text{Cov}(s, u)/\text{Var}(s)\). The sample size does not help.

Step 2: the conditions

  1. Relevance: the instrument changes schooling. This can be checked.
  2. Independence: the instrument has nothing to do with \(u\) (after the controls).
  3. Exclusion: the instrument changes wages only through schooling.
  4. Monotonicity (for the interpretation, section 6): the instrument pushes nobody in the opposite direction.

Step 3: the Wald estimator

With one instrument that is 0 or 1 and no controls, the estimator is the ratio of two differences:

\[\hat\beta_{IV} = \frac{\bar y_{\text{near}} - \bar y_{\text{far}}}{\bar s_{\text{near}} - \bar s_{\text{far}}}\]

The numerator is the effect of the instrument on the outcome (the reduced form), the denominator its effect on schooling (the first stage).

Step 4: two-stage least squares

With controls \(X\) (and perhaps several instruments \(Z\)) the same idea is done with two regressions, two-stage least squares (2SLS):

  1. First stage: regress schooling on the instrument and the controls, and keep the fitted values \(\hat s\). They contain only the part of schooling that the instrument and the controls can explain.
  2. Second stage: regress the log wage on \(\hat s\) and the controls.

\[\hat\beta = (W^\top P_Z W)^{-1} W^\top P_Z\, y, \qquad P_Z = Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top\]

(\(W\): schooling and the controls; \(Z\): instrument and controls.)

Step 5: the standard error

The second regression gives the right coefficient and a wrong standard error: its residuals are measured from \(\hat s\) instead of \(s\). The right ones use the real schooling:

\[e = y - W\hat\beta, \qquad \text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \frac{e^\top e}{n - p}\,(W^\top P_Z W)^{-1}\]

5. Weak instruments

If the instrument hardly moves schooling, the estimator divides by a number near zero. The estimates jump around, and the usual interval (estimate ± 1.96 SE) can be wrong. A simulation where we know the truth (β = 0.08). pi is the effect of the instrument on schooling, rho the endogeneity: the correlation between what we do not see in schooling and what we do not see in wages.

Code 
set.seed(211)
one <- function(pi, rho, n = 1000) {
  z <- rep(0:1, length.out = n)
  u <- rnorm(n)
  s <- 12 + pi * z + 2 * (rho * u + sqrt(1 - rho^2) * rnorm(n))
  y <- 0.08 * s + 0.4 * u
  zc <- z - mean(z)
  fs <- sum(zc * s) / sum(zc^2)
  rf <- sum(zc * y) / sum(zc^2)
  b <- rf / fs
  e <- (y - mean(y)) - b * (s - mean(s))
  se <- sqrt(sum(e^2) / (n - 2) / sum(zc^2)) / abs(fs)
  F <- fs^2 * sum(zc^2) / (sum(((s - mean(s)) - fs * zc)^2) / (n - 2))
  d <- (y - mean(y)) - 0.08 * (s - mean(s))
  g <- sum(zc * d) / sum(zc^2)
  ar <- g / sqrt(sum((d - g * zc)^2) / (n - 2) / sum(zc^2)) # Anderson-Rubin at the truth
  c(
    iv = b,
    ols = cov(s, y) / var(s),
    F = F,
    covered = abs(b - 0.08) < 1.96 * se,
    ar_covered = abs(ar) < 1.96
  )
}
settings <- expand.grid(pi = c(0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1), rho = c(0.6, 0.95))

  • Least squares gives about 0.20 with an endogeneity of 0.6 and 0.27 with 0.95, instead of 0.08, whatever the instrument.
  • With a strong instrument (mean F = 63.8) the IV estimates lie around 0.08.
  • With the weakest one (mean F = 1.8) the median of the IV estimates is 0.15 or 0.16: it has moved towards least squares. And 90% of the estimates lie between -0.82 and 0.91 (endogeneity 0.6).
  • The usual interval contains the truth in 94% to 95% of the samples with an endogeneity of 0.6, even with weak instruments. It is right there, and very wide. With 0.95 its coverage falls to 79% for the weakest instrument.

This is why the advice in the literature looks contradictory. An old rule asks for F > 10 (Staiger & Stock, 1997). Lee et al. (2022) show that a t test with the usual 1.96 needs an F above 104.7 to have an error of at most 5% in the worst case. Angrist and Kolesár (2024) show that with one instrument the usual interval is nearly right unless the endogeneity is extreme. Card’s instrument has an F of 13.3, and the estimated endogeneity is -0.29. It is best to use an interval that does not depend on all this:

The Anderson–Rubin interval

A test that works with any strength (Anderson & Rubin, 1949): if the true effect were \(\beta_0\), then \(y - \beta_0 s\) would contain nothing of schooling, and the instrument should not predict it. Test that for many \(\beta_0\) and keep those that are not rejected. In the simulation it has the right coverage in all settings (from 94.3% to 95.4%).

6. Whose effect is it?

Card’s result (IV higher than OLS) is typical for this literature. One explanation is that the effect is not the same for everybody. Boys who would go to college anyway, and boys who would never go, are not moved by the instrument. IV is about the others, the compliers. Imbens and Angrist (1994) called it the local average treatment effect (LATE). If those who are held back by cost gain more from school than the average, the IV estimate is higher, and it is right, for them.

Where on the ladder did the instrument push? Schooling is the sum of steps, \(s = \sum_k I(s \ge k)\), so the first stage can be split into steps:

8. Things to keep in mind

  • The instrument is an argument, not a statistic. Write down why it should be independent, and why there is no other way to the outcome.
  • Always show the first stage, the reduced form and the OLS result next to the IV result. The reduced form is an ordinary regression: if you cannot see the effect there, it is probably not there (Angrist & Krueger, 2001).
  • Weak instruments: report the (robust) first-stage F and an Anderson–Rubin interval. Many weak instruments are worse than one good one: 2SLS then moves towards OLS. (LIML is better.)
  • Do not put fitted values from a logistic first stage into a second regression. 2SLS wants linear first stages, also for a 0/1 treatment.
  • All controls belong in both stages.
  • Standard errors: use ivreg(), not the second lm(); robust or clustered by the unit to which the instrument belongs (here the county, which is not in the file).
  • LATE: the estimate is for those whom the instrument moved.
  • Measurement error in the predictor is another reason to use IV: a second, independent measurement is an instrument for the first. (With known error variances, see Deming regression.)
  • Relatives: regression with selection uses the same kind of exclusion; panel data removes what is constant over time without any instrument.

Method (data analysis):

The return to schooling was estimated for 3,010 men of the National Longitudinal Survey of Young Men (log hourly wage in 1976; Card, 1993). Because unobserved ability and family background may influence both schooling and wages, schooling was instrumented with the presence of a four-year college in the local labour market of residence in 1966, which lowered the cost of college for boys who could live at home. All models contained experience, experience squared, and indicators for black, residence in the south, metropolitan residence in 1966 and 1976, and region in 1966. Two-stage least squares was used (R package ivreg); the strength of the instrument was judged with the first-stage F, and a 95% Anderson–Rubin interval (Anderson & Rubin, 1949) was computed because it is valid with weak instruments. Endogeneity was tested with the Wu–Hausman test, and the robustness of the result to a direct effect of the instrument was examined (Conley et al., 2012). Analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.6.2; R Core Team, 2026).

## The question: school, or what comes with it?

People with more schooling earn more. Is it the **school** that makes the difference? Or would the same persons have earned more anyway, because they are able, hard-working, or from families that help? Nobody can measure all of that. So adding control variables, the remedy of ordinary regression, does not finish the job.

An **instrumental variable** (an *instrument*) takes another way. Find something that

- pushes people towards more schooling,
- has nothing to do with their ability or family,
- and touches their wages in **no other way** than through schooling.

Then compare the wages of those who were pushed and those who were not. Whatever difference there is, must have come through schooling.

David Card (1993) proposed the **distance to a college**. A boy who grew up near a four-year college could live at home while studying. For some boys that decided the matter. This page repeats his analysis, with every step by hand, and then asks how far one can trust it. The second part is the more important one: the conditions above cannot be proved from the data.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: why least squares is wrong

The true equation has a variable $u$ that we cannot see, which is also related to schooling $s$:

$$\log w = \beta\, s + u, \qquad \text{Cov}(s, u) \ne 0$$

Least squares attributes to $s$ everything that comes along with it, $\hat\beta_{OLS} \to \beta + \text{Cov}(s, u)/\text{Var}(s)$. The sample size does not help.

### Step 2: the conditions

1. **Relevance**: the instrument changes schooling. This can be checked.
2. **Independence**: the instrument has nothing to do with $u$ (after the controls).
3. **Exclusion**: the instrument changes wages **only** through schooling.
4. **Monotonicity** (for the interpretation, section 6): the instrument pushes nobody in the opposite direction.

### Step 3: the Wald estimator

With one instrument that is 0 or 1 and no controls, the estimator is the ratio of two differences:

$$\hat\beta_{IV} = \frac{\bar y_{\text{near}} - \bar y_{\text{far}}}{\bar s_{\text{near}} - \bar s_{\text{far}}}$$

The numerator is the effect of the instrument on the outcome (the **reduced form**), the denominator its effect on schooling (the **first stage**).

```{r wald}
c(
  reduced_form = diff(by_near$lwage),
  first_stage = diff(by_near$educ),
  Wald = diff(by_near$lwage) / diff(by_near$educ)
)
```

Without controls we know that condition 2 fails, so `r f3(diff(by_near$lwage) / diff(by_near$educ))` is not our answer.

### Step 4: two-stage least squares

With controls $X$ (and perhaps several instruments $Z$) the same idea is done with two regressions, **two-stage least squares** (2SLS):

1. First stage: regress schooling on the instrument and the controls, and keep the fitted values $\hat s$. They contain only the part of schooling that the instrument and the controls can explain.
2. Second stage: regress the log wage on $\hat s$ and the controls.

$$\hat\beta = (W^\top P_Z W)^{-1} W^\top P_Z\, y, \qquad P_Z = Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top$$

($W$: schooling and the controls; $Z$: instrument and controls.)

```{r two_stages}
fml <- function(lhs, rhs) as.formula(paste(lhs, "~", rhs))
ols          <- lm(fml("lwage", paste("educ +", controls)), card)
first_stage  <- lm(fml("educ", paste("nearc4 +", controls)), card)
reduced_form <- lm(fml("lwage", paste("nearc4 +", controls)), card)
card$educ_hat <- fitted(first_stage)
second_stage <- lm(fml("lwage", paste("educ_hat +", controls)), card)

W <- model.matrix(ols); Z <- model.matrix(first_stage); y <- card$lwage
PW   <- Z %*% solve(crossprod(Z), crossprod(Z, W))                        # P_Z W
beta <- drop(solve(crossprod(PW, W), crossprod(PW, y)))
cbind(
  value = c(
    OLS = coef(ols)[["educ"]],
    reduced_form_over_first_stage = coef(reduced_form)[["nearc4"]] / coef(first_stage)[["nearc4"]],
    second_stage = coef(second_stage)[["educ_hat"]],
    matrix_formula = beta[["educ"]]
  )
)
```

The three ways give the same `r f3(beta[["educ"]])`. Least squares gave `r f3(coef(ols)[["educ"]])`.

### Step 5: the standard error

The second regression gives the right coefficient and a **wrong standard error**: its residuals are measured from $\hat s$ instead of $s$. The right ones use the real schooling:

$$e = y - W\hat\beta, \qquad \text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \frac{e^\top e}{n - p}\,(W^\top P_Z W)^{-1}$$

```{r standard_error}
e  <- drop(y - W %*% beta); s2 <- sum(e^2) / (nrow(W) - ncol(W))
se <- sqrt(diag(s2 * solve(crossprod(PW))))
iv <- ivreg(fml("lwage", paste("educ +", controls, "| nearc4 +", controls)), data = card)
rbind(
  by_hand = c(estimate = beta[["educ"]], SE = se[["educ"]]),
  ivreg = coef(summary(iv))["educ", 1:2],
  second_stage_lm = coef(summary(second_stage))["educ_hat", 1:2]
)
robust_se <- sqrt(sandwich::vcovHC(iv, type = "HC1")["educ", "educ"]); c(robust_SE = robust_se)
```

Here the wrong standard error happens to be close to the right one. It can be far off, in both directions. In `ivreg()`, the part after `|` lists the instruments **and** all controls again.

- **Wu–Hausman**: is the IV estimate different from least squares? Put the first-stage residual into the wage equation; if it matters, schooling is *endogenous*. But two instruments of the same kind can be wrong in the same way, and then the test does not notice. (The *F* for both together is `r f1(weak2[["statistic"]])`: the second one adds almost nothing.)

## 5. Weak instruments

If the instrument hardly moves schooling, the estimator divides by a number near zero. The estimates jump around, and the usual interval (estimate ± 1.96 *SE*) can be wrong. A simulation where we know the truth (β = 0.08). `pi` is the effect of the instrument on schooling, `rho` the endogeneity: the correlation between what we do not see in schooling and what we do not see in wages.

```{r weak_simulation}
set.seed(211)
one <- function(pi, rho, n = 1000) {
  z <- rep(0:1, length.out = n)
  u <- rnorm(n)
  s <- 12 + pi * z + 2 * (rho * u + sqrt(1 - rho^2) * rnorm(n))
  y <- 0.08 * s + 0.4 * u
  zc <- z - mean(z)
  fs <- sum(zc * s) / sum(zc^2)
  rf <- sum(zc * y) / sum(zc^2)
  b <- rf / fs
  e <- (y - mean(y)) - b * (s - mean(s))
  se <- sqrt(sum(e^2) / (n - 2) / sum(zc^2)) / abs(fs)
  F <- fs^2 * sum(zc^2) / (sum(((s - mean(s)) - fs * zc)^2) / (n - 2))
  d <- (y - mean(y)) - 0.08 * (s - mean(s))
  g <- sum(zc * d) / sum(zc^2)
  ar <- g / sqrt(sum((d - g * zc)^2) / (n - 2) / sum(zc^2)) # Anderson-Rubin at the truth
  c(
    iv = b,
    ols = cov(s, y) / var(s),
    F = F,
    covered = abs(b - 0.08) < 1.96 * se,
    ar_covered = abs(ar) < 1.96
  )
}
settings <- expand.grid(pi = c(0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1), rho = c(0.6, 0.95))
sim <- do.call(
  rbind,
  lapply(seq_len(nrow(settings)), function(i) {
    r <- t(replicate(2000, one(settings$pi[i], settings$rho[i])))
    data.frame(
      settings[i, ],
      mean_F = mean(r[, "F"]),
      OLS = mean(r[, "ols"]),
      IV_median = median(r[, "iv"]),
      IV_low = quantile(r[, "iv"], 0.05),
      IV_high = quantile(r[, "iv"], 0.95),
      coverage = mean(r[, "covered"]),
      coverage_AR = mean(r[, "ar_covered"])
    )
  })
)
print(round(sim, 3), row.names = FALSE)
si <- function(p, r) sim[sim$pi == p & sim$rho == r, ]
c(endogeneity_in_the_data_of_Card = cor(residuals(iv), residuals(first_stage)))
```

- **Least squares** gives about `r f2(si(0.1, 0.6)$OLS)` with an endogeneity of 0.6 and `r f2(si(0.1, 0.95)$OLS)` with 0.95, instead of 0.08, whatever the instrument.
- With a **strong** instrument (mean *F* = `r f1(si(1, 0.6)$mean_F)`) the IV estimates lie around 0.08.
- With the **weakest** one (mean *F* = `r f1(si(0.1, 0.6)$mean_F)`) the median of the IV estimates is `r f2(si(0.1, 0.6)$IV_median)` or `r f2(si(0.1, 0.95)$IV_median)`: it has moved towards least squares. And 90% of the estimates lie between `r f2(si(0.1, 0.6)$IV_low)` and `r f2(si(0.1, 0.6)$IV_high)` (endogeneity 0.6).
- The **usual interval** contains the truth in `r pct(min(sim$coverage[sim$rho == 0.6]))` to `r pct(max(sim$coverage[sim$rho == 0.6]))` of the samples with an endogeneity of 0.6, even with weak instruments. It is right there, and very wide. With 0.95 its coverage falls to `r pct(si(0.1, 0.95)$coverage)` for the weakest instrument.

This is why the advice in the literature looks contradictory. An old rule asks for *F* > 10 (Staiger & Stock, 1997). Lee et al. (2022) show that a *t* test with the usual 1.96 needs an *F* above 104.7 to have an error of at most 5% in the **worst case**. Angrist and Kolesár (2024) show that with **one** instrument the usual interval is nearly right unless the endogeneity is extreme. Card's instrument has an *F* of `r f1(F_first)`, and the estimated endogeneity is `r f2(cor(residuals(iv), residuals(first_stage)))`. It is best to use an interval that does not depend on all this:

### The Anderson–Rubin interval

A test that works with **any** strength (Anderson & Rubin, 1949): if the true effect were $\beta_0$, then $y - \beta_0 s$ would contain nothing of schooling, and the instrument should not predict it. Test that for many $\beta_0$ and keep those that are not rejected. In the simulation it has the right coverage in all settings (from `r pct(min(sim$coverage_AR), 1)` to `r pct(max(sim$coverage_AR), 1)`).

The interval goes from `r f3(ci_ar[1])` to `r f3(ci_ar[2])`. It is longer than the usual one on the upper side, and it contains the result of least squares.

## 6. Whose effect is it?

Card's result (IV higher than OLS) is typical for this literature. One explanation is that the effect is not the same for everybody. Boys who would go to college anyway, and boys who would never go, are not moved by the instrument. IV is about the others, the **compliers**. Imbens and Angrist (1994) called it the *local average treatment effect* (LATE). If those who are held back by cost gain more from school than the average, the IV estimate is higher, and it is right, **for them**.

Where on the ladder did the instrument push? Schooling is the sum of steps, $s = \sum_k I(s \ge k)$, so the first stage can be split into steps:

```{r steps_chart, fig.height = 4.5}

So the estimate is about boys who were near the decision for or against some college, and not about the value of the tenth year of school.

Other explanations are less flattering: measurement error in schooling pulls least squares down, the instrument may be invalid, and studies with a "significant" IV result are more easily published.

### Card's own test

Card argued like this. A college nearby should matter most to boys from **poor** families, for whom the cost decides. So the **product** of "college nearby" and "parents without high school" can be the instrument, and then "college nearby" itself may go into the wage equation. If it has a direct effect on wages, it will show there. In his Table 5 the IV estimate is 0.093 (0.065) and the direct effect 0.015 (0.029). His classes of parental education are not in our file, but we can build something like them. As he did in this table, we treat experience as endogenous, with age as its instrument (the last part of this section says why):

### How large a violation would change the answer?

Suppose that growing up near a college raises wages **directly** by $\gamma$ (better labour market, better schools). Then the right estimate would be

$$\hat\beta(\gamma) = \frac{\text{reduced form} - \gamma}{\text{first stage}}$$

(Conley et al., 2012, develop this idea.)

A direct effect of only `r pct(g_ols, 1)` on wages would bring the estimate down to the one of least squares, and one of `r pct(rf, 1)` to zero. Because IV **divides** by a small first stage, small violations become large errors. And nobody can rule out a direct effect of 2%.

### A control that is not innocent

`exper` is age − schooling − 6. It contains schooling, the very variable that we consider endogenous. Two remedies: use age instead of experience, or treat experience as endogenous too and use age as its instrument. Card did the second in his panel B of Table 3 and found 0.122 (0.046):

```{r experience}
iv_age <- ivreg(
  fml("lwage", paste("educ +", controls_age, "| nearc4 +", controls_age)),
  data = card
)
iv_exper <- ivreg(fml("lwage", paste("educ +", controls, "| nearc4 +", controls_age)), data = card)
ols_age  <- lm(fml("lwage", paste("educ +", controls_age)), card)
alt <- rbind(
  `OLS, experience` = coef(summary(ols))["educ", 1:2],
  `IV, experience as in Card` = coef(summary(iv))["educ", 1:2],
  `OLS, age` = coef(summary(ols_age))["educ", 1:2],
  `IV, age` = coef(summary(iv_age))["educ", 1:2],
  `IV, experience instrumented by age` = coef(summary(iv_exper))["educ", 1:2]
)
F_age <- summary(iv_age)$diagnostics["Weak instruments", "statistic"]
round(alt, 3); c(first_stage_F_with_age = F_age)
```

The last row gives his number. With age, the first stage is `r if (F_age < F_first) "weaker" else "stronger"` (*F* = `r f1(F_age)`), and the IV estimate is `r f3(alt["IV, age", 1])` (*SE* = `r f3(alt["IV, age", 2])`). At a given age, a man with one more year of school has one year **less** experience, so the two versions do not measure the same thing: with experience held constant the coefficient is larger by the value of that year of work. The choice must be made by the question, and reported.

## 8. Things to keep in mind

- **The instrument is an argument, not a statistic.** Write down why it should be independent, and why there is no other way to the outcome.
- **Always show** the first stage, the reduced form and the OLS result next to the IV result. The reduced form is an ordinary regression: if you cannot see the effect there, it is probably not there (Angrist & Krueger, 2001).
- **Weak instruments**: report the (robust) first-stage *F* and an Anderson–Rubin interval. Many weak instruments are worse than one good one: 2SLS then moves towards OLS. (LIML is better.)
- **Do not put fitted values from a logistic first stage into a second regression.** 2SLS wants linear first stages, also for a 0/1 treatment.
- **All controls belong in both stages.**
- **Standard errors**: use `ivreg()`, not the second `lm()`; robust or clustered by the unit to which the instrument belongs (here the county, which is not in the file).
- **LATE**: the estimate is for those whom the instrument moved.
- **Measurement error** in the predictor is another reason to use IV: a second, independent measurement is an instrument for the first. (With known error variances, see [Deming regression](EXAMPLE_DEMING_REGRESSION.Rmd).)
- **Relatives**: [regression with selection](EXAMPLE_HECKMAN_SELECTION.Rmd) uses the same kind of exclusion; [panel data](EXAMPLE_PANEL_DATA.Rmd) removes what is constant over time without any instrument.

## 10. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | A predictor is related to something unseen that also affects the outcome, and there is a source of variation that is not |
| **Conditions** | Relevance (testable), independence, exclusion (not testable), monotonicity |
| **One 0/1 instrument** | Difference in the outcome, divided by the difference in the predictor |
| **In general** | Two-stage least squares, $\hat\beta = (W^\top P_Z W)^{-1}W^\top P_Z y$ |
| **How (R)** | `ivreg(y ~ x + controls | z + controls)`; also `fixest::feols()` |
| **Check** | First-stage *F*, Wu–Hausman, Sargan (with extra instruments), balance, sensitivity |
| **Interval** | Anderson–Rubin when the instrument is not very strong |
| **Meaning** | The effect for those whom the instrument moved (LATE) |

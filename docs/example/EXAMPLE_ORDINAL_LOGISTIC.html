Ordinal Logistic Regression: What Makes People Satisfied With Their Job?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Ordinal logistic regression (the proportional odds model) in R with MASS::polr(), on the 2022 General Social Survey: how autonomy, respect, job security, stress and working from home go with the job satisfaction of about 2,200 US workers, with odds ratios, thresholds, predicted probabilities, the proportional odds assumption and APA reporting.

The question: four answers in order

Since 1972 the General Social Survey (GSS) has asked Americans: “On the whole, how satisfied are you with the work you do — would you say you are very satisfied, moderately satisfied, a little dissatisfied, or very dissatisfied?” In 2022 it also asked them about the job itself: are they free to decide how to do their work, are they treated with respect, is the job secure, how often is it stressful, how often do they work from home?

This page asks: which of these go with being more satisfied with one’s job? Does working from home make people happier with their work, or is it something else?

The answer has four values in order: very dissatisfied, a little dissatisfied, moderately satisfied, very satisfied. But nobody knows how far apart they are: the step from “a little dissatisfied” to “moderately satisfied” may be bigger than the step from “moderately” to “very”. The type of outcome decides the type of regression:

Outcome Regression Example
Two categories logistic hired or not
Ordered categories ordinal logistic job satisfaction: very dissatisfied … very satisfied
Unordered categories multinomial logistic how someone left a job: quit, retired, laid off…

Three other ways to analyse the answers, and what each gets wrong:

  • A linear regression on the scores 1 to 4 assumes the four answers are equally far apart, and predicts answers such as 3.4 that nobody can give.
  • A logistic regression of satisfied against dissatisfied throws away the difference between “moderately” and “very” satisfied, where most people are.
  • A multinomial logistic regression ignores the order, and needs a whole set of coefficients for every answer: 27 parameters here instead of 11.

The ordinal logistic regression uses the order and needs one coefficient per predictor.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: cut the scale in three places

Four ordered answers can be split into “this answer or lower” against “higher” in three places:

Split \(j\) Lower: \(Y \le j\) Higher: \(Y > j\)
1 very dissatisfied a little dissatisfied or more
2 dissatisfied (very or a little) satisfied (moderately or very)
3 moderately satisfied or less very satisfied

\(P(Y_i \le j)\) is a cumulative probability: the probability that person \(i\) gives answer \(j\) or a lower one.

Step 2: one logistic regression per split, with the same slopes

\[\text{logit}\,P(Y_i \le j) = \ln\frac{P(Y_i \le j)}{P(Y_i > j)} = \theta_j - (\beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_p x_{pi}), \qquad j = 1, 2, 3\]

Each split has its own intercept, \(\theta_j\), called a threshold or cut-point. The slopes \(\beta\) are the same at all three splits: a predictor changes the odds of a higher answer by the same factor wherever the scale is cut. This is the proportional odds assumption, and the model is also called the proportional odds model.

The thresholds increase, \(\theta_1 < \theta_2 < \theta_3\), because \(P(Y \le j)\) can only grow with \(j\). The minus sign makes a positive \(\beta\) mean higher answers: here, more satisfied. R’s polr() and clm() and SPSS’s PLUM write the model this way; some programs, for example SAS, write it with a plus sign, and their coefficients have the opposite sign.

Step 3: from the splits to the four answers

Write \(\eta_i = \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_p x_{pi}\). Then

\[P(Y_i \le j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\theta_j - \eta_i)}}\]

and the probability of each answer is the difference between two cumulative probabilities:

\[P(Y_i = 1) = P(Y_i \le 1) \qquad P(Y_i = j) = P(Y_i \le j) - P(Y_i \le j - 1) \qquad P(Y_i = 4) = 1 - P(Y_i \le 3)\]

The four probabilities are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1.

Step 4: what a coefficient means

\(e^{\beta}\) is a cumulative odds ratio: the number by which the odds of a higher answer rather than a lower one are multiplied for each step up the predictor, with the other predictors held constant. With proportional odds the same number holds at every split. An odds ratio of 2 for respect means that one step up the respect scale doubles the odds of being very satisfied rather than less satisfied, and doubles the odds of being satisfied rather than dissatisfied, and doubles the odds of being anything but very dissatisfied.

Step 5: the picture behind the model, a hidden satisfaction scale

Imagine that each person has a hidden, continuous satisfaction, \(Y^*\), which depends on the predictors plus a random part:

\[Y^*_i = \eta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \text{logistic}(0, 1)\]

The person answers by where \(Y^*\) falls among the thresholds: “very dissatisfied” below \(\theta_1\), “a little dissatisfied” between \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\), “moderately satisfied” between \(\theta_2\) and \(\theta_3\), “very satisfied” above \(\theta_3\). The predictors move the whole distribution of \(Y^*\) to the right or to the left; the thresholds stay where they are. This gives exactly the formulas of steps 2 and 3. With a normal \(\varepsilon\) instead of a logistic one, it is the ordinal probit model (polr(..., method = "probit")). Section 5 draws the picture with the fitted numbers.

Step 6: how R finds the numbers

By maximum likelihood: R looks for the thresholds and the \(\beta\)’s that make the answers people actually gave as probable as possible,

\[\ln L = \sum_i \ln P(Y_i = \text{the answer that person } i \text{ gave})\]

Step 7: count the parameters

Three thresholds, plus one slope for each of autonomy, respect, security, stress, self-employment and age, plus two for working from home (three groups): 11 parameters. A multinomial model with the same predictors needs 9 coefficients for each of the three answers other than the reference: 27.

How to read the output

Code 
b     <- coef(fit)                          # the slopes
theta <- fit$zeta                           # the thresholds
se    <- sqrt(diag(vcov(fit)))[names(b)]
z     <- b / se
p     <- 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))                 # summary() prints no p values: compute them
or    <- exp(b)                             # odds ratios
ci    <- exp(confint(fit))                  # their 95% confidence intervals (profile likelihood)
Part Meaning Here
Coefficients One slope \(\beta\) per predictor term, with its standard error and \(t = \beta / SE\). Positive: more satisfied respect: \(\beta\) = 0.73, SE = 0.07
Intercepts The thresholds \(\theta_j\), named after the two answers they separate Moderately satisfied|Very satisfied: 4.87
Residual Deviance \(-2 \ln L\): how badly the model fits; smaller is better. Used for the tests in section 6 4,065
AIC The deviance plus 2 for every parameter, for comparing models fitted to the same data 4,087
  • There are no p values. The t value is really a \(z\): compare it with the normal distribution. For respect, z = 10.03, p < .001.
  • The thresholds are the cut-points on the hidden scale for someone with every predictor at 0. Here that person does not exist (autonomy and respect start at 1, age at 18), so the thresholds mean little on their own; they are needed for the predicted probabilities.
  • One coefficient per predictor, not one per answer: that is the gain over the multinomial model.

## The question: four answers in order

Since 1972 the General Social Survey (GSS) has asked Americans: *"On the whole, how satisfied are you with the work you do — would you say you are very satisfied, moderately satisfied, a little dissatisfied, or very dissatisfied?"* In 2022 it also asked them about the job itself: are they free to decide how to do their work, are they treated with respect, is the job secure, how often is it stressful, how often do they work from home?

This page asks: **which of these go with being more satisfied with one's job?** Does working from home make people happier with their work, or is it something else?

The answer has four values **in order**: very dissatisfied, a little dissatisfied, moderately satisfied, very satisfied. But nobody knows how far apart they are: the step from "a little dissatisfied" to "moderately satisfied" may be bigger than the step from "moderately" to "very". The type of outcome decides the type of regression:

| Outcome | Regression | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two categories | [logistic](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED.rmd) | hired or not |
| **Ordered categories** | **ordinal logistic** | **job satisfaction: very dissatisfied ... very satisfied** |
| Unordered categories | [multinomial logistic](EXAMPLE_MULTINOMIAL_LOGISTIC.Rmd) | how someone left a job: quit, retired, laid off... |

Three other ways to analyse the answers, and what each gets wrong:

- **A linear regression on the scores 1 to 4** assumes the four answers are equally far apart, and predicts answers such as 3.4 that nobody can give.
- **A logistic regression of satisfied against dissatisfied** throws away the difference between "moderately" and "very" satisfied, where most people are.
- **A multinomial logistic regression** ignores the order, and needs a whole set of coefficients for every answer: 27 parameters here instead of 11.

The ordinal logistic regression uses the order and needs **one coefficient per predictor**.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: cut the scale in three places

Four ordered answers can be split into "this answer or lower" against "higher" in three places:

| Split $j$ | Lower: $Y \le j$ | Higher: $Y > j$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | very dissatisfied | a little dissatisfied or more |
| 2 | dissatisfied (very or a little) | satisfied (moderately or very) |
| 3 | moderately satisfied or less | very satisfied |

$P(Y_i \le j)$ is a **cumulative probability**: the probability that person $i$ gives answer $j$ or a lower one.

### Step 2: one logistic regression per split, with the same slopes

$$\text{logit}\,P(Y_i \le j) = \ln\frac{P(Y_i \le j)}{P(Y_i > j)} = \theta_j - (\beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_p x_{pi}), \qquad j = 1, 2, 3$$

Each split has its own intercept, $\theta_j$, called a **threshold** or **cut-point**. The slopes $\beta$ are **the same at all three splits**: a predictor changes the odds of a higher answer by the same factor wherever the scale is cut. This is the **proportional odds** assumption, and the model is also called the proportional odds model.

The thresholds increase, $\theta_1 < \theta_2 < \theta_3$, because $P(Y \le j)$ can only grow with $j$. The minus sign makes a positive $\beta$ mean **higher** answers: here, more satisfied. R's `polr()` and `clm()` and SPSS's PLUM write the model this way; some programs, for example SAS, write it with a plus sign, and their coefficients have the opposite sign.

### Step 3: from the splits to the four answers

Write $\eta_i = \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_p x_{pi}$. Then

$$P(Y_i \le j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\theta_j - \eta_i)}}$$

and the probability of each answer is the difference between two cumulative probabilities:

$$P(Y_i = 1) = P(Y_i \le 1) \qquad P(Y_i = j) = P(Y_i \le j) - P(Y_i \le j - 1) \qquad P(Y_i = 4) = 1 - P(Y_i \le 3)$$

The four probabilities are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1.

### Step 4: what a coefficient means

$e^{\beta}$ is a **cumulative odds ratio**: the number by which the odds of a higher answer rather than a lower one are multiplied for each step up the predictor, with the other predictors held constant. With proportional odds the same number holds at every split. An odds ratio of 2 for respect means that one step up the respect scale doubles the odds of being very satisfied rather than less satisfied, **and** doubles the odds of being satisfied rather than dissatisfied, **and** doubles the odds of being anything but very dissatisfied.

### Step 5: the picture behind the model, a hidden satisfaction scale

Imagine that each person has a hidden, continuous satisfaction, $Y^*$, which depends on the predictors plus a random part:

$$Y^*_i = \eta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \text{logistic}(0, 1)$$

The person answers by where $Y^*$ falls among the thresholds: "very dissatisfied" below $\theta_1$, "a little dissatisfied" between $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, "moderately satisfied" between $\theta_2$ and $\theta_3$, "very satisfied" above $\theta_3$. The predictors move the whole distribution of $Y^*$ to the right or to the left; the thresholds stay where they are. This gives exactly the formulas of steps 2 and 3. With a normal $\varepsilon$ instead of a logistic one, it is the **ordinal probit** model (`polr(..., method = "probit")`). Section 5 draws the picture with the fitted numbers.

### Step 6: how R finds the numbers

By maximum likelihood: R looks for the thresholds and the $\beta$'s that make the answers people actually gave as probable as possible,

$$\ln L = \sum_i \ln P(Y_i = \text{the answer that person } i \text{ gave})$$

### Step 7: count the parameters

Three thresholds, plus one slope for each of autonomy, respect, security, stress, self-employment and age, plus two for working from home (three groups): **11 parameters**. A multinomial model with the same predictors needs 9 coefficients for each of the three answers other than the reference: 27.

## 4. Fitting the model

```{r fit}
fit <- polr(satisfaction ~ autonomy + respect + security + stress + home + self_employed + age10,
            data = d, Hess = TRUE)
summary(fit)
```

`Hess = TRUE` keeps the matrix that `summary()` needs for the standard errors; without it, `summary()` fits the model again.

### How to read the output

```{r output_parts}
b     <- coef(fit)                          # the slopes
theta <- fit$zeta                           # the thresholds
se    <- sqrt(diag(vcov(fit)))[names(b)]
z     <- b / se
p     <- 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))                 # summary() prints no p values: compute them
or    <- exp(b)                             # odds ratios
ci    <- exp(confint(fit))                  # their 95% confidence intervals (profile likelihood)
```

| Part | Meaning | Here |
|---|---|---|
| `Coefficients` | One slope $\beta$ per predictor term, with its standard error and $t = \beta / SE$. Positive: more satisfied | `respect`: $\beta$ = `r f2(b[["respect"]])`, *SE* = `r f2(se[["respect"]])` |
| `Intercepts` | The thresholds $\theta_j$, named after the two answers they separate | `Moderately satisfied|Very satisfied`: `r f2(theta[[3]])` |
| `Residual Deviance` | $-2 \ln L$: how badly the model fits; smaller is better. Used for the tests in section 6 | `r num(round(deviance(fit)))` |
| `AIC` | The deviance plus 2 for every parameter, for comparing models fitted to the same data | `r num(round(AIC(fit)))` |

- **There are no *p* values.** The `t value` is really a $z$: compare it with the normal distribution. For `respect`, *z* = `r f2(z[["respect"]])`, *p* `r pval(p[["respect"]])`.
- **The thresholds** are the cut-points on the hidden scale for someone with every predictor at 0. Here that person does not exist (autonomy and respect start at 1, age at 18), so the thresholds mean little on their own; they are needed for the predicted probabilities.
- **One coefficient per predictor**, not one per answer: that is the gain over the multinomial model.

## 6. Testing the predictors and the model

The **likelihood-ratio test** compares the deviances of two nested models. First the whole model against a model with thresholds only, then each predictor: drop it and see how much worse the model gets.

```{r tests}
null <- polr(satisfaction ~ 1, data = d)
model_test <- anova(null, fit)
model_test

tests <- drop1(fit, test = "Chisq")
tests

mcfadden <- 1 - deviance(fit) / deviance(null)   # McFadden's pseudo-R squared
mcfadden
```

- **Every predictor except working from home is significant.** The $\chi^2$ values rank them: respect (`r f2(tests["respect", "LRT"])`), stress (`r f2(tests["stress", "LRT"])`), autonomy (`r f2(tests["autonomy", "LRT"])`) and job security (`r f2(tests["security", "LRT"])`), then age and self-employment. Working from home: $\chi^2$(2) = `r f2(tests["home", "LRT"])`, *p* `r pval(tests["home", "Pr(>Chi)"])`.
- **McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$** = `r f2(mcfadden)`: one minus the deviance of the model divided by the deviance of the model with thresholds only. It runs much lower than the $R^2$ of a linear regression; use it to compare models fitted to the same data.

Is the order worth using? The same predictors in a multinomial model, which ignores the order:

```{r versus_multinomial}
multi <- nnet::multinom(formula(fit), data = d, trace = FALSE)
data.frame(model      = c("ordinal (polr)", "multinomial (multinom)"),
           parameters = c(length(b) + length(theta), length(coef(multi))),
           AIC        = round(c(AIC(fit), AIC(multi)), 1))
```

The ordinal model fits better by AIC, with `r length(b) + length(theta)` parameters instead of `r length(coef(multi))`: the multinomial model spends `r length(coef(multi)) - length(b) - length(theta)` more parameters and gains less than they cost.

How often does the model pick the right answer, if it picks the most likely one for each person?

```{r classification}
guess <- predict(fit, type = "class")
table(observed = d$satisfaction, predicted = guess)
accuracy  <- mean(as.character(guess) == as.character(d$satisfaction))
base_rate <- max(overall)   # always guessing the most common answer
c(model = accuracy, most_common_answer = base_rate)
```

`r pct(accuracy)` right, against `r pct(base_rate)` for always guessing the most common answer. The model almost never predicts the two dissatisfied answers: they are rare, so for almost nobody are they the **most** likely answer. As in the [multinomial example](EXAMPLE_MULTINOMIAL_LOGISTIC.Rmd), the predicted probabilities carry the information, not the single best guess.

The formal test: fit the same model with `clm()` from the package `ordinal`, which can relax the assumption one predictor at a time. `nominal_test()` does that for each predictor and compares the two models with a likelihood-ratio test:

```{r nominal_test}

```

No predictor needs its own slopes: the smallest *p* is `r nz(min(nominal$"Pr(>Chi)", na.rm = TRUE), 3)`, for job security. The differences that the chart shows could be chance. The **Brant test** (Brant, 1990), in the package `brant`, is a common alternative that answers the same question.

### If the assumption fails: a partial proportional odds model

If a predictor did fail the test, it could get one slope per split while the others keep one slope: a **partial proportional odds** model (Peterson & Harrell, 1990). In `clm()`, the predictors in `nominal` get their own slopes:

```{r partial}
partial <- clm(satisfaction ~ autonomy + respect + stress + home + self_employed + age10,
               nominal = ~ security, data = d)
coef(partial)[grep("security", names(coef(partial)))]
anova(clm_fit, partial)
```

`clm()` writes these slopes as shifts of the thresholds, so the effect of security on a **higher** answer is the number shown with its sign changed: `r paste(f2(-coef(partial)[grep("security", names(coef(partial)))]), collapse = ", ")` at the three splits. The likelihood-ratio test is the same as the `security` row of `nominal_test()`, and the AIC is a little worse: two more parameters that do not pay for themselves. We keep the simple model. If several predictors failed, the alternatives are a model with every slope free (`nominal = ~ .`, the generalized ordered logit) or the [multinomial model](EXAMPLE_MULTINOMIAL_LOGISTIC.Rmd).

### Four more things to keep in mind

```{r robustness}
# answers given as categories instead of numbers: one coefficient per answer
fit_steps <- update(fit, . ~ . - autonomy - respect - security - stress +
                      factor(autonomy) + factor(respect) + factor(security) + factor(stress))
steps_test <- anova(fit, fit_steps)
stress_steps <- coef(fit_steps)[paste0("factor(stress)", 2:5)]   # hardly ever ... always, against never

# the people who answered on the web against those interviewed in person or by phone
very_by_mode <- prop.table(table(d$web, d$satisfaction), 1)[, "Very satisfied"]
fit_mode <- update(fit, . ~ . + web)
round(cbind(without_mode = coef(fit), with_mode = coef(fit_mode)[names(coef(fit))]), 2)
```

- **Equal steps.** The four scored questions count each step up the scale the same. Treating the answers as categories instead, with a coefficient for each answer, fits somewhat better, $\chi^2$(`r steps_test$"   Df"[2]`) = `r f2(steps_test$"LR stat."[2])`, *p* `r pval(steps_test$"Pr(Chi)"[2])`: the steps are not all equal. For stress, for example, the coefficients of "hardly ever", "sometimes", "often" and "always" against "never" are `r paste(f2(stress_steps), collapse = ", ")`, not evenly spaced. The scored model is easier to read and tells the same story.
- **Web or interview.** In 2022 most people answered the GSS on the web. `r pct(very_by_mode[["Yes"]])` of them said "very satisfied", against `r pct(very_by_mode[["No"]])` of those interviewed in person or by phone, as people often give more positive answers when an interviewer is listening. Adding the mode to the model leaves the other coefficients almost unchanged (above).
- **The sample, not the population.** These are unweighted estimates for the people in the survey. To describe all US workers, use the GSS weights (`wtssps`) and the design of the survey, for example with `svyolr()` in the package `survey` (Lumley, 2010).
- **Association, not cause.** People who like their work may also see their workplace in a better light, and judge it more respectful and more secure. One survey at one point in time cannot tell which comes first.

## 9. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | The outcome is a category with three or more values in a natural order |
| **Why not linear regression** | It assumes equal steps between the answers and predicts values nobody can give |
| **Why not multinomial logistic regression** | It ignores the order and needs a set of coefficients for every answer |
| **How (R)** | `MASS::polr(y ~ x, data, Hess = TRUE)`, with `y` an ordered factor; or `ordinal::clm()` |
| **Output** | Thresholds (one per split) and one slope per predictor; `exp()` of a slope is a cumulative odds ratio |
| **Easiest to read** | Predicted probabilities: `predict(fit, newdata, type = "probs")` |
| **Tests** | Likelihood-ratio tests with `anova()` and `drop1()`; McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$ |
| **Assumptions** | Independent observations; proportional odds (the same slopes at every split) |
| **If proportional odds fails** | A partial proportional odds model, `clm(..., nominal = ~ x)`; or a multinomial model |

Seemingly Unrelated Regression: Do General Electric and Westinghouse Invest Alike?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) in R, by hand and with systemfit, on Grunfeld’s investment data of US firms (1935-1954), the example of Zellner’s original paper: why equations with correlated errors should be estimated together, feasible GLS with the Kronecker product, when SUR gives the same as OLS, the Breusch-Pagan test, tests between equations, iterated SUR and maximum likelihood, a simulation of the gain and of standard errors that are too small, and APA reporting.

The question: two firms, two equations, one economy

General Electric and Westinghouse were the two giants of electrical engineering in the United States. How much did each of them invest in new equipment every year, and what did it depend on? Grunfeld’s theory says: on the value of the firm (what the market expects of it) and on the capital that it already has (which must be replaced). So there is a regression for each firm:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{invest}_{GE,t} &= \beta_{1} + \beta_{2}\,\text{value}_{GE,t} + \beta_{3}\,\text{capital}_{GE,t} + \varepsilon_{GE,t} \\ \text{invest}_{W,t} &= \gamma_{1} + \gamma_{2}\,\text{value}_{W,t} + \gamma_{3}\,\text{capital}_{W,t} + \varepsilon_{W,t} \end{aligned}\]

The two equations have nothing in common. Different firms, different outcomes, different predictors, different coefficients. They seem unrelated. But both firms lived through the same years. A good year for the whole industry, which neither value nor capital explains, makes both errors positive. Then the error of one equation contains information about the error of the other.

Arnold Zellner (1962) showed how to use this information. His method, seemingly unrelated regressions (SUR), estimates the equations together and gets more precise coefficients than separate regressions. His example was this one, with these two firms.

SUR is the neighbour of multivariate regression. There, all equations have the same predictors, and joint estimation changes nothing. Here the predictors differ, and it does.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: the errors of one year are correlated

Let \(m\) be the number of equations (firms) and \(T\) the number of years. Within a year, the errors of the equations have a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). Between years, the errors are independent:

\[\text{Cov}(\varepsilon_{it}, \varepsilon_{jt}) = \sigma_{ij}, \qquad \text{Cov}(\varepsilon_{it}, \varepsilon_{js}) = 0 \;\text{ for } t \ne s\]

Step 2: stack the equations

Write the two outcomes one below the other, and the predictors in blocks:

\[\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} y_{GE} \\ y_W \end{pmatrix}}_{y} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} X_{GE} & 0 \\ 0 & X_W \end{pmatrix}}_{X} \begin{pmatrix} \beta \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_{GE} \\ \varepsilon_W \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{Cov}(\varepsilon) = \Sigma \otimes I_T\]

The Kronecker product \(\Sigma \otimes I_T\) is a matrix of \(2 \times 2\) blocks: in each block, \(\sigma_{ij}\) on the diagonal, because only errors of the same year are related. It is now one regression with correlated errors, which is generalized least squares (see this page):

\[\hat\beta_{SUR} = \big(X^\top (\Sigma^{-1} \otimes I)\, X\big)^{-1} X^\top (\Sigma^{-1} \otimes I)\, y, \qquad \text{Var}(\hat\beta_{SUR}) = \big(X^\top (\Sigma^{-1} \otimes I)\, X\big)^{-1}\]

Step 3: \(\Sigma\) is unknown, so estimate it

  1. Estimate each equation by OLS, and keep the residuals.
  2. \(\hat\sigma_{ij} = \sum_t e_{it} e_{jt} / T\). (Dividing by \(T - k\) instead, with \(k\) coefficients per equation, is the other common choice.)
  3. Put \(\hat\Sigma\) into the formula: feasible GLS.

The step from 1 to 3 can be repeated with the new residuals until nothing changes. The end is the maximum likelihood estimate for normal errors.

Step 4: where the gain comes from

Suppose the residual of General Electric in 1946 is large and positive. Because the errors correlate, we expect a positive error for Westinghouse in 1946, too. SUR takes that expected error out of Westinghouse’s investment before it looks for the slope. It is like a covariate that OLS does not have.

Step 5: when there is nothing to gain

Two cases give exactly the OLS estimates:

  • The errors are uncorrelated (\(\sigma_{ij} = 0\)): no information to pass on.
  • All equations have the same predictors: the covariate of step 4 would be orthogonal to the predictors by construction.

The gain is largest when the errors are highly correlated and the predictors are not.

How to read the output

  • Covariance of the residuals used for estimation is \(\hat\Sigma\) from the OLS residuals, and ... correlations of the residuals shows the link between the equations, after the final fit.
  • The coefficients of each equation are read as in any regression. One million dollars more in the value of General Electric goes with 0.038 million more investment in the year, and for Westinghouse with 0.058.
  • McElroy R² is a single \(R^2\) for the whole system. The \(R^2\) of an equation can be lower than with OLS, because OLS makes each \(R^2\) as large as possible, and SUR does not aim for that.

5. Tests

Are the equations related at all? The Breusch–Pagan test

The null hypothesis is that \(\Sigma\) is diagonal. Under it, \(T\) times the sum of the squared correlations of the OLS residuals follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(m(m-1)/2\) degrees of freedom (Breusch & Pagan, 1980). (This is another test than the Breusch–Pagan test for unequal variances.)

Code 
lm_stat <- Tn * r_errors^2
c(LM = lm_stat, df = 1, p = pchisq(lm_stat, 1, lower.tail = FALSE))

10.627798572  1.000000000  0.001114003

The errors are related, \(\chi^2\)(1) = 10.63, p = .001.

Do the two firms invest alike?

The question of the title compares coefficients between equations. Separate regressions cannot do it properly: the two estimates are correlated. In the system it is an ordinary linear hypothesis, with the covariance matrix of all six coefficients:

\[H_0:\; \beta_2 = \gamma_2 \;\text{ and }\; \beta_3 = \gamma_3\]

6. When does SUR help? Experiments

The same predictors: nothing happens

If each firm had all four predictors (its own and the other’s), the equations would have identical right-hand sides:

Code 
same_rhs <- list(GE = invest_ge ~ value_ge + capital_ge + value_wh + capital_wh,
                 W  = invest_wh ~ value_ge + capital_ge + value_wh + capital_wh)
max(abs(
  coef(systemfit(same_rhs, method = "OLS", data = wide)) -
    coef(systemfit(same_rhs, method = "SUR", data = wide))
))
[1] 1.193534e-11

The largest difference between OLS and SUR is zero, up to rounding, although the errors are correlated. This is the case of multivariate regression.

A known truth: how large is the gain, and are the standard errors right?

We take the predictors of the real data, a truth equal to our SUR estimates, and errors with a chosen correlation. For each correlation we simulate 1,000 histories of 20 years, and compare the true spread of the estimates of one slope with the standard errors that the programs report:

Code 
set.seed(12)
truth <- b_sur
sds   <- sqrt(diag(Sigma))
fit_both <- function(yy) {
  b_o <- c(qr.coef(qr(X_ge), yy[1:Tn]), qr.coef(qr(X_wh), yy[Tn + 1:Tn]))
  e <- matrix(yy - X %*% b_o, Tn)
  S <- crossprod(e) / (Tn - k) # the cautious version, with degrees of freedom
  Si <- solve(S)
  A <- rbind(
    cbind(Si[1, 1] * crossprod(X_ge), Si[1, 2] * crossprod(X_ge, X_wh)),
    cbind(Si[2, 1] * crossprod(X_wh, X_ge), Si[2, 2] * crossprod(X_wh))
  )
  y1 <- yy[1:Tn]
  y2 <- yy[Tn + 1:Tn]
  V <- solve(A)
  b_s <- V %*%
    c(
      Si[1, 1] * crossprod(X_ge, y1) + Si[1, 2] * crossprod(X_ge, y2),
      Si[2, 1] * crossprod(X_wh, y1) + Si[2, 2] * crossprod(X_wh, y2)
    )
  c(
    ols = b_o[[2]],
    sur = b_s[[2]],
    se_ols = sqrt(S[1, 1] * solve(crossprod(X_ge))[2, 2]),
    se_sur = sqrt(V[2, 2])
  )
}

7. Things to keep in mind

  • Many equations, few years. \(\Sigma\) has \(m(m+1)/2\) elements. If \(m\) comes close to \(T\), the reported standard errors become far too small, as in the third experiment; with \(m > T\) the estimate of \(\Sigma\) cannot even be inverted. Beck and Katz (1995) showed this for panels of countries, and proposed to keep OLS and correct its standard errors instead (panel-corrected standard errors).
  • Autocorrelation. SUR allows errors to correlate between equations in the same year, not between years. Our residuals fail here:

  • The Durbin–Watson statistics (1.07 and 1.41, far below 2) show positive autocorrelation, so all the standard errors of this page, OLS and SUR, are too small. A serious analysis would add autoregressive errors to each equation (Parks’ method), or dynamics.
  • One wrong equation spoils the others. If the equation of one firm lacks an important predictor, its error goes through \(\Sigma\) into the estimates of the other firms. OLS keeps such damage inside its own equation. SUR is more precise when everything is right, and less robust when something is wrong.
  • Endogeneity. SUR does not help if a predictor depends on the errors, for example if investment raises the value of the firm in the same year. (Here the value is that of the beginning of the year.) Three-stage least squares combines SUR with instrumental variables: method = "3SLS".
  • Pooling. If the coefficients are the same in all equations, a panel model is simpler and more precise. SUR is for few units, many times and different coefficients.
  • In psychology and sociology the same model appears as a path model with correlated residuals. lavaan fits it with y1 ~ x1; y2 ~ x2; y1 ~~ y2, and with maximum likelihood it gives the iterated estimates. For categorical outcomes, the bivariate probit model is the relative of SUR.

## The question: two firms, two equations, one economy

General Electric and Westinghouse were the two giants of electrical engineering in the United States. How much did each of them invest in new equipment every year, and what did it depend on? Grunfeld's theory says: on the **value** of the firm (what the market expects of it) and on the **capital** that it already has (which must be replaced). So there is a regression for each firm:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{invest}_{GE,t} &= \beta_{1} + \beta_{2}\,\text{value}_{GE,t} + \beta_{3}\,\text{capital}_{GE,t} + \varepsilon_{GE,t} \\
\text{invest}_{W,t}  &= \gamma_{1} + \gamma_{2}\,\text{value}_{W,t} + \gamma_{3}\,\text{capital}_{W,t} + \varepsilon_{W,t}
\end{aligned}$$

The two equations have nothing in common. Different firms, different outcomes, different predictors, different coefficients. They **seem unrelated**. But both firms lived through the same years. A good year for the whole industry, which neither value nor capital explains, makes **both** errors positive. Then the error of one equation contains information about the error of the other.

Arnold Zellner (1962) showed how to use this information. His method, **seemingly unrelated regressions** (SUR), estimates the equations together and gets more precise coefficients than separate regressions. His example was this one, with these two firms.

SUR is the neighbour of [multivariate regression](EXAMPLE_MULTIVARIATE_REGRESSION.Rmd). There, all equations have the same predictors, and joint estimation changes nothing. Here the predictors differ, and it does.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: the errors of one year are correlated

Let $m$ be the number of equations (firms) and $T$ the number of years. Within a year, the errors of the equations have a covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Between years, the errors are independent:

$$\text{Cov}(\varepsilon_{it}, \varepsilon_{jt}) = \sigma_{ij}, \qquad \text{Cov}(\varepsilon_{it}, \varepsilon_{js}) = 0 \;\text{ for } t \ne s$$

### Step 2: stack the equations

Write the two outcomes one below the other, and the predictors in blocks:

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} y_{GE} \\ y_W \end{pmatrix}}_{y} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} X_{GE} & 0 \\ 0 & X_W \end{pmatrix}}_{X} \begin{pmatrix} \beta \\ \gamma \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_{GE} \\ \varepsilon_W \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{Cov}(\varepsilon) = \Sigma \otimes I_T$$

The Kronecker product $\Sigma \otimes I_T$ is a matrix of $2 \times 2$ blocks: in each block, $\sigma_{ij}$ on the diagonal, because only errors of the same year are related. It is now one regression with correlated errors, which is **generalized least squares** (see [this page](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd)):

$$\hat\beta_{SUR} = \big(X^\top (\Sigma^{-1} \otimes I)\, X\big)^{-1} X^\top (\Sigma^{-1} \otimes I)\, y, \qquad \text{Var}(\hat\beta_{SUR}) = \big(X^\top (\Sigma^{-1} \otimes I)\, X\big)^{-1}$$

### Step 3: $\Sigma$ is unknown, so estimate it

1. Estimate each equation by OLS, and keep the residuals.
2. $\hat\sigma_{ij} = \sum_t e_{it} e_{jt} / T$. (Dividing by $T - k$ instead, with $k$ coefficients per equation, is the other common choice.)
3. Put $\hat\Sigma$ into the formula: **feasible** GLS.

The step from 1 to 3 can be repeated with the new residuals until nothing changes. The end is the maximum likelihood estimate for normal errors.

### Step 4: where the gain comes from

Suppose the residual of General Electric in 1946 is large and positive. Because the errors correlate, we expect a positive error for Westinghouse in 1946, too. SUR takes that expected error **out** of Westinghouse's investment before it looks for the slope. It is like a covariate that OLS does not have.

### Step 5: when there is nothing to gain

Two cases give exactly the OLS estimates:

- **The errors are uncorrelated** ($\sigma_{ij} = 0$): no information to pass on.
- **All equations have the same predictors**: the covariate of step 4 would be orthogonal to the predictors by construction.

The gain is largest when the errors are highly correlated and the predictors are not.

### How to read the output

- **`Covariance of the residuals used for estimation`** is $\hat\Sigma$ from the OLS residuals, and **`... correlations of the residuals`** shows the link between the equations, after the final fit.
- The **coefficients of each equation** are read as in any regression. One million dollars more in the value of General Electric goes with `r f3(b[["GE_value_ge"]])` million more investment in the year, and for Westinghouse with `r f3(b[["W_value_wh"]])`.
- **`McElroy R²`** is a single $R^2$ for the whole system. The $R^2$ of an equation can be lower than with OLS, because OLS makes each $R^2$ as large as possible, and SUR does not aim for that.

## 5. Tests

### Are the equations related at all? The Breusch–Pagan test

The null hypothesis is that $\Sigma$ is diagonal. Under it, $T$ times the sum of the squared correlations of the OLS residuals follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $m(m-1)/2$ degrees of freedom (Breusch & Pagan, 1980). (This is another test than the Breusch–Pagan test for unequal variances.)

```{r breusch_pagan}
lm_stat <- Tn * r_errors^2
c(LM = lm_stat, df = 1, p = pchisq(lm_stat, 1, lower.tail = FALSE))
```

The errors are related, $\chi^2$(1) = `r f2(lm_stat)`, *p* `r pval(pchisq(lm_stat, 1, lower.tail = FALSE))`.

### Do the two firms invest alike?

The question of the title compares coefficients **between** equations. Separate regressions cannot do it properly: the two estimates are correlated. In the system it is an ordinary linear hypothesis, with the covariance matrix of all six coefficients:

$$H_0:\; \beta_2 = \gamma_2 \;\text{ and }\; \beta_3 = \gamma_3$$

```{r equal}
equal_slopes <- linearHypothesis(
  sur,
  c("GE_value_ge - W_value_wh = 0", "GE_capital_ge - W_capital_wh = 0"),
  test = "F"
)
equal_slopes

difference <- function(term_a, term_b) {
  d <- b[[term_a]] - b[[term_b]]
  s <- sqrt(vcov(sur)[term_a, term_a] + vcov(sur)[term_b, term_b] - 2 * vcov(sur)[term_a, term_b])
  c(difference = d, SE = s, t = d / s)
}
diffs <- rbind(
  value = difference("GE_value_ge", "W_value_wh"),
  capital = difference("GE_capital_ge", "W_capital_wh")
)
diffs
```

We cannot say that the slopes differ, *F*(`r equal_slopes$Df[2]`, `r equal_slopes$Res.Df[2]`) = `r f2(equal_slopes$F[2])`, *p* `r pval(equal_slopes$"Pr(>F)"[2])`. That is not evidence that they are the same: with `r Tn` years the standard errors of the differences are large. If theory says that the slopes are equal, we can impose it, and estimate them with both firms' data:

```{r restricted}
restricted <- systemfit(

  restrict.matrix = c("GE_value_ge - W_value_wh = 0", "GE_capital_ge - W_capital_wh = 0")

```

## 6. When does SUR help? Experiments

### The same predictors: nothing happens

If each firm had **all four** predictors (its own and the other's), the equations would have identical right-hand sides:

```{r identical}
same_rhs <- list(GE = invest_ge ~ value_ge + capital_ge + value_wh + capital_wh,
                 W  = invest_wh ~ value_ge + capital_ge + value_wh + capital_wh)
max(abs(
  coef(systemfit(same_rhs, method = "OLS", data = wide)) -
    coef(systemfit(same_rhs, method = "SUR", data = wide))
))
```

The largest difference between OLS and SUR is zero, up to rounding, although the errors are correlated. This is the case of [multivariate regression](EXAMPLE_MULTIVARIATE_REGRESSION.Rmd).

### A known truth: how large is the gain, and are the standard errors right?

We take the predictors of the real data, a truth equal to our SUR estimates, and errors with a chosen correlation. For each correlation we simulate 1,000 histories of 20 years, and compare the **true** spread of the estimates of one slope with the standard errors that the programs report:

```{r simulation}
set.seed(12)

sds   <- sqrt(diag(Sigma))

  b_o <- c(qr.coef(qr(X_ge), yy[1:Tn]), qr.coef(qr(X_wh), yy[Tn + 1:Tn]))
  e <- matrix(yy - X %*% b_o, Tn)

  Si <- solve(S)
  A <- rbind(
    cbind(Si[1, 1] * crossprod(X_ge), Si[1, 2] * crossprod(X_ge, X_wh)),
    cbind(Si[2, 1] * crossprod(X_wh, X_ge), Si[2, 2] * crossprod(X_wh))

  y2 <- yy[Tn + 1:Tn]

      Si[1, 1] * crossprod(X_ge, y1) + Si[1, 2] * crossprod(X_ge, y2),
      Si[2, 1] * crossprod(X_wh, y1) + Si[2, 2] * crossprod(X_wh, y2)

    ols = b_o[[2]],
    sur = b_s[[2]],

rhos <- c(0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.73, 0.9)
```

### Many equations: the standard errors stop being true

Again a known truth: the predictors of 2, 5 and 10 of the firms, their OLS coefficients, and errors that correlate at 0.4 between all the firms. How does the reported standard error of the slope of value (first firm) compare with the true spread of the estimates?

```{r many_equations}
set.seed(13)
firms <- setdiff(unique(grunfeld$firm), "American Steel")             # ten firms
many <- function(m, reps = 500) {
  d <- lapply(c("General Electric", setdiff(firms, "General Electric"))[1:m], function(f) {
    subset(grunfeld, firm == f)
  })
  Xs <- lapply(d, function(z) cbind(1, z$value, z$capital))
  fits <- lapply(seq_len(m), function(j) lm.fit(Xs[[j]], d[[j]]$invest))
  mu <- sapply(fits, function(f) f$fitted.values)
  s0 <- sapply(fits, function(f) sqrt(sum(f$residuals^2) / (Tn - k)))
  R <- matrix(0.4, m, m)
  diag(R) <- 1
  L <- t(chol(diag(s0) %*% R %*% diag(s0)))
  r <- replicate(reps, {
    Ys <- mu + t(L %*% matrix(rnorm(m * Tn), m))
    Es <- sapply(seq_len(m), function(j) lm.fit(Xs[[j]], Ys[, j])$residuals)
    Si <- solve(crossprod(Es) / (Tn - k))
    A <- do.call(
      rbind,
      lapply(1:m, function(i) {
        do.call(cbind, lapply(1:m, function(j) Si[i, j] * crossprod(Xs[[i]], Xs[[j]])))
      })
    )
    v <- do.call(
      c,
      lapply(1:m, function(i) {
        rowSums(sapply(1:m, function(j) Si[i, j] * crossprod(Xs[[i]], Ys[, j])))
      })
    )
    V <- solve(A)
    c(est = sum(V[2, ] * v), se = sqrt(V[2, 2]))
  })
  data.frame(
    equations = m,
    covariances = m * (m + 1) / 2,
    true_spread = sd(r["est", ]),
    reported_SE = mean(r["se", ]),
    ratio = mean(r["se", ]) / sd(r["est", ])
  )
}
trap <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c(2, 5, 10), many))
round(trap, 4)
```

With two equations the reported standard error is `r f2(trap$ratio[1])` times the true one, with five `r f2(trap$ratio[2])` and with ten `r f2(trap$ratio[3])`. With ten firms and 20 years there are `r trap$covariances[3]` variances and covariances to estimate, and the formula does not know how badly.

## 7. Things to keep in mind

- **Many equations, few years.** $\Sigma$ has $m(m+1)/2$ elements. If $m$ comes close to $T$, the reported standard errors become far too small, as in the third experiment; with $m > T$ the estimate of $\Sigma$ cannot even be inverted. Beck and Katz (1995) showed this for panels of countries, and proposed to keep OLS and correct its standard errors instead (panel-corrected standard errors).
- **Autocorrelation.** SUR allows errors to correlate between equations in the same year, not between years. Our residuals fail here:

```{r durbin_watson}
dw <- rbind(
  GE = unlist(dwtest(ols_ge)[c("statistic", "p.value")]),
  Westinghouse = unlist(dwtest(ols_wh)[c("statistic", "p.value")])
)
round(dw, 3)
```

- The Durbin–Watson statistics (`r f2(dw[1, 1])` and `r f2(dw[2, 1])`, far below 2) show positive autocorrelation, so **all** the standard errors of this page, OLS and SUR, are too small. A serious analysis would add autoregressive errors to each equation (Parks' method), or dynamics.
- **One wrong equation spoils the others.** If the equation of one firm lacks an important predictor, its error goes through $\Sigma$ into the estimates of the other firms. OLS keeps such damage inside its own equation. SUR is more precise when everything is right, and less robust when something is wrong.
- **Endogeneity.** SUR does not help if a predictor depends on the errors, for example if investment raises the value of the firm in the same year. (Here the value is that of the beginning of the year.) Three-stage least squares combines SUR with [instrumental variables](EXAMPLE_INSTRUMENTAL_VARIABLES.Rmd): `method = "3SLS"`.
- **Pooling.** If the coefficients are the same in all equations, a panel model is simpler and more precise. SUR is for few units, many times and different coefficients.
- **In psychology and sociology** the same model appears as a *path model* with correlated residuals. `lavaan` fits it with `y1 ~ x1; y2 ~ x2; y1 ~~ y2`, and with maximum likelihood it gives the iterated estimates. For categorical outcomes, the bivariate probit model is the relative of SUR.

**Method** (data analysis):

> We modelled the annual gross investment of General Electric and Westinghouse from `r min(ge$year)` to `r max(ge$year)` (*T* = `r Tn` years, million dollars; Grunfeld's data as corrected by Kleiber & Zeileis, 2010) with one equation per firm, with the market value of the firm and its stock of capital as predictors. Because both firms were exposed to the same economic conditions, the equations were estimated jointly by seemingly unrelated regressions (Zellner, 1962), using the residual covariance matrix of the OLS equations with a correction for degrees of freedom. Independence of the equations was tested with the Breusch–Pagan (1980) test, and equality of the slopes with an *F* test on the joint estimates. Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`) with the package systemfit (Henningsen & Hamann, 2007).

**Results:**

> The residuals of the two equations were correlated, *r* = `r nz(r_errors)`, χ²(1) = `r f2(lm_stat)`, *p* `r pval(pchisq(lm_stat, 1, lower.tail = FALSE))`. In the joint estimates (Table 1), investment increased with the value of the firm both for General Electric, *b* = `r f3(b[["GE_value_ge"]])`, *SE* = `r f3(se[["GE_value_ge"]])`, *t*(`r Tn - k`) = `r f2(b[["GE_value_ge"]] / se[["GE_value_ge"]])`, and for Westinghouse, *b* = `r f3(b[["W_value_wh"]])`, *SE* = `r f3(se[["W_value_wh"]])`, *t*(`r Tn - k`) = `r f2(b[["W_value_wh"]] / se[["W_value_wh"]])`. The slopes of the two firms did not differ significantly, *F*(`r equal_slopes$Df[2]`, `r equal_slopes$Res.Df[2]`) = `r f2(equal_slopes$F[2])`, *p* `r pval(equal_slopes$"Pr(>F)"[2])`. The residuals were autocorrelated (Durbin–Watson = `r f2(dw[1, 1])` and `r f2(dw[2, 1])`), so the standard errors are probably too small.

## 9. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | Several regressions with different predictors whose errors are correlated, such as firms in the same years or outcomes of the same people |
| **The model** | Stacked equations, errors with covariance $\Sigma \otimes I$ |
| **How (R)** | `systemfit(list(eq1 = y1 ~ x1, eq2 = y2 ~ x2), method = "SUR", data = wide)` |
| **The estimator** | Feasible GLS: OLS, then $\hat\Sigma$ from its residuals, then GLS; iterated = maximum likelihood |
| **Gain** | Large with correlated errors and different predictors |
| **No gain** | Uncorrelated errors, or the same predictors everywhere |
| **Tests** | Breusch–Pagan for $\Sigma$ diagonal; `linearHypothesis()` between equations |
| **Dangers** | Many equations and few times; autocorrelation; one misspecified equation |
| **Related pages** | [Multivariate regression](EXAMPLE_MULTIVARIATE_REGRESSION.Rmd), [generalized least squares](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd) |

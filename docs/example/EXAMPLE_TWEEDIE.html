Tweedie Regression: How Much Rain Falls on Corfu?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Tweedie regression in R with mgcv and statmod, on 70 years of daily rain on the Greek island of Corfu: one model for many zeros and a skewed amount, the compound Poisson-gamma distribution (showers and their size), estimating the power, rain ratios by season and after a wet day, the probability of a dry day, where a single dispersion fails, a dispersion that changes with the season, comparison with a two-part model, a trend that hides two opposite trends, and APA reporting.

The question: zeros and amounts in one model

Corfu is one of the rainiest places in Greece. But on most days it does not rain at all. On the others there may fall half a millimetre or a hundred. How does the rain of a day depend on the season, and on whether it rained the day before? Has it changed in 70 years?

The outcome is an amount that is exactly zero most of the time and otherwise positive and skewed. Insurance claims look the same (most policies have no claim in a year), and so do catches of fish, spending on a product, and hours of unpaid work. Two-part models treat the zeros and the amounts separately, with two sets of coefficients. Tweedie regression treats them together, with one distribution and one set of coefficients for the mean.

There is a physical picture behind it: a day’s rain is the sum of the rain of several showers. The number of showers is random, and may be zero. Then the rain is exactly zero.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: showers

The number of showers on a day is Poisson with mean \(\lambda\). Each shower brings a gamma-distributed amount, independently of the others, with mean \(\alpha\theta\). The rain of the day is their sum. If the Poisson number is 0, the sum is 0:

\[y = \sum_{k=1}^{N} g_k, \qquad N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad P(y = 0) = e^{-\lambda}\]

This is the compound Poisson–gamma distribution. Dunn (2004) used it for rain.

Step 2: the same distribution, written as a Tweedie

Tweedie distributions are those whose variance is a power of the mean:

\[\text{Var}(y) = \phi\,\mu^p\]

\(p = 0\) is the normal, \(p = 1\) the Poisson, \(p = 2\) the gamma, \(p = 3\) the inverse Gaussian (Jørgensen, 1987). For \(1 < p < 2\) it is the compound Poisson–gamma, with a heap at exactly zero and a continuous part above. The two ways of writing it are connected:

\[\lambda = \frac{\mu^{2-p}}{\phi\,(2 - p)}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{2 - p}{p - 1}, \qquad \theta = \phi\,(p - 1)\,\mu^{p-1}\]

Code 
set.seed(111)
mu <- 3; p <- 1.6; phi <- 8
lambda <- mu^(2 - p) / (phi * (2 - p))
alpha <- (2 - p) / (p - 1)
theta <- phi * (p - 1) * mu^(p - 1)
showers <- rpois(200000, lambda)
# the sum of n gammas is a gamma (0 showers: 0)
y_sim <- rgamma(200000, shape = alpha * showers, scale = theta)
c(
  mean = mean(y_sim),
  variance = var(y_sim),
  theory = phi * mu^p,
  dry = mean(y_sim == 0),
  theory_dry = exp(-lambda)
)
      mean   variance     theory        dry theory_dry 
 3.0195968 46.7935322 46.3963691  0.6151650  0.6157269

Step 3: the regression

\[\log\mu_i = x_i^\top\beta\]

With the log link, \(e^\beta\) is a ratio of mean amounts, “rain ratios” here. One coefficient per predictor, for everything: if the mean goes up, the formulas of step 2 make showers more frequent and larger at the same time.

Step 4: estimating \(p\)

Given \(p\), the \(\beta\)’s are estimated like those of any generalized linear model, from the mean and the variance function alone. \(p\) itself is estimated by maximum likelihood. The density has no simple formula (it is an infinite series), but mgcv and the package tweedie compute it (Dunn & Smyth, 2005).

Overall, the model expects 72.6% dry days, and there are 72.6%. Excellent. Month by month it fails. In July it expects 86% dry days, and 95% are dry; in December 63%, and 53%. With one \(\phi\) for the whole year, the probability of a dry day follows from the mean, and on Corfu it varies more than that. In summer, wet days are rarer than the mean suggests, and bring more rain than the model expects of them; in winter they are more frequent and lighter.

A dispersion that changes with the season

Let \(\phi\) depend on the season too. mgcv has a family for it, twlss(), with three formulas: for the mean, for the power and for the dispersion.

  list(
    mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
    ~1,
    ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade

6. Against a two-part model

A two-part model has separate coefficients for whether and how much: a logistic regression and a gamma regression.

Code 
whether <- glm(
  I(mm > 0) ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
  family = binomial,
  data = rain
)
amount <- glm(
  mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
  family = Gamma("log"),
  data = subset(rain, mm > 0)
)
mean_two <- predict(whether, rain, type = "response") * predict(amount, rain, type = "response")
shape <- 1 / MASS::gamma.dispersion(amount) # ML estimate of the dispersion of the gamma
ll_two <- as.numeric(logLik(whether)) +
  sum(dgamma(amount$y, shape = shape, scale = fitted(amount) / shape, log = TRUE))
aic_two <- -2 * ll_two + 2 * (length(coef(whether)) + length(coef(amount)) + 1)
ll_tw <- c(
  `Tweedie` = AIC(fit),
  `Tweedie, varying dispersion` = AIC(fit2),
  `two-part (logistic + gamma)` = aic_two
)
round(ll_tw)
                    Tweedie Tweedie, varying dispersion two-part (logistic + gamma)

compare <- cbind(

By the AIC the order is: two-part (logistic + gamma), then Tweedie, varying dispersion, then Tweedie. The row of wet_before shows what the Tweedie model adds up: after a wet day the odds of rain are 5.1 times as high, and the amount, if it rains, 1.26 times. The Tweedie ratio of 3.4 is the total of both.

Tweedie Two-part
Coefficients per predictor one: the effect on the mean two: whether, and how much
Zeros follow from the mean and \(\phi\) a model of their own
Best for totals (rain per month, cost per policy) and simple reporting understanding the process
Sum of several days or policies stays Tweedie with the same \(p\) leaves the model

7. The trend that is not one

Has the rain of Corfu changed? Now we leave wet_before out, because a change of it would be part of the answer, and we let the days of a year be related (cluster-robust standard errors). The total with a Tweedie model, and the two parts:

Code 
by_year <- function(m, d) coeftest(m, vcov = vcovCL(m, cluster = d$year))["decade", ]
wet_days <- subset(rain, mm > 0)
tw0 <- glm(
  mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade,
  family = tweedie(var.power = p_hat, link.power = 0),
  data = rain
)
st <- by_year(tw0, rain)
sw <- by_year(
  glm(I(mm > 0) ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade, family = binomial, data = rain),
  rain
)
sa <- by_year(
  glm(mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade, family = Gamma("log"), data = wet_days),
  wet_days
)
s1 <- by_year(
  glm(I(mm >= 1) ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade, family = binomial, data = rain),
  rain
)
signif(
  rbind(
    `total (Tweedie)` = st,
    `whether (logistic)` = sw,
    `how much (gamma)` = sa,
    `at least 1 mm (logistic)` = s1
  ),
  3
)

The total changed by -2.0% per decade, which is not distinguishable from zero (p = .127). But over the decades the odds of a wet day fell, by 7.7% per decade (p < .001), and the rain on a wet day rose, by 2.3% per decade (p = .013). In the mean, the two partly cancel. Fewer wet days with more rain each would be a real change of the climate. But it could also be a change of the measurement: if small amounts of a few tenths of a millimetre were recorded less often, there would be fewer wet days with a larger average. Counting only days with at least 1 mm, the odds change by -5.5% per decade (p < .001). So the fall is not only a matter of the smallest amounts. One station with changed instruments cannot settle it.

8. Things to keep in mind

  • \(1 < p < 2\) only for data with zeros. If the estimate runs to one of the limits, think again about the model.
  • One coefficient for everything is the strength and the weakness. If a predictor acts differently on whether and on how much, a Tweedie model gives the sum of the two and hides the parts.
  • Check the zeros in groups, not only overall.
  • The standard errors depend on how \(\phi\) is estimated. Report which. Robust (sandwich) errors are a good alternative.
  • Comparing AIC works only if \(p\) and \(\phi\) are estimated by maximum likelihood. A glm() with the family of statmod has no AIC of its own (tweedie::AICtweedie() computes it).
  • Exposure: for claims per policy and year, use offset(log(exposure)), or weights. This is the standard model for the pure premium in car insurance (Smyth & Jørgensen, 2002).
  • Dependence. Days are not independent. We let the day before into the model; the correlation of neighbouring residuals is then 0.03.
  • Extremes. A Tweedie model is for means. For the size of a flood, use extreme-value models.

Daily rain at Corfu airport, 1955–2024 (N = 25,075 days; Menne et al., 2012), was zero on 72.6% of the days and right-skewed on the others. We used a generalized linear model with a Tweedie (compound Poisson–gamma) distribution and a log link, with two harmonics of the season, an indicator of rain on the day before, and a linear trend in decades. ## The question: zeros and amounts in one model

Corfu is one of the rainiest places in Greece. But on most days it does not rain at all. On the others there may fall half a millimetre or a hundred. How does the rain of a day depend on the season, and on whether it rained the day before? Has it changed in 70 years?

The outcome is an **amount** that is exactly zero most of the time and otherwise positive and skewed. Insurance claims look the same (most policies have no claim in a year), and so do catches of fish, spending on a product, and hours of unpaid work. [Two-part models](EXAMPLE_HURDLE_TWO_PART.Rmd) treat the zeros and the amounts separately, with two sets of coefficients. **Tweedie regression** treats them **together**, with one distribution and one set of coefficients for the mean.

There is a physical picture behind it: a day's rain is the sum of the rain of several **showers**. The number of showers is random, and may be zero. Then the rain is exactly zero.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: showers

The number of showers on a day is Poisson with mean $\lambda$. Each shower brings a gamma-distributed amount, independently of the others, with mean $\alpha\theta$. The rain of the day is their sum. If the Poisson number is 0, the sum is 0:

$$y = \sum_{k=1}^{N} g_k, \qquad N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad P(y = 0) = e^{-\lambda}$$

This is the **compound Poisson–gamma** distribution. Dunn (2004) used it for rain.

### Step 2: the same distribution, written as a Tweedie

Tweedie distributions are those whose variance is a power of the mean:

$$\text{Var}(y) = \phi\,\mu^p$$

$p = 0$ is the normal, $p = 1$ the Poisson, $p = 2$ the [gamma](EXAMPLE_GAMMA.Rmd), $p = 3$ the [inverse Gaussian](EXAMPLE_INVERSE_GAUSSIAN.Rmd) (Jørgensen, 1987). For $1 < p < 2$ it **is** the compound Poisson–gamma, with a heap at exactly zero and a continuous part above. The two ways of writing it are connected:

$$\lambda = \frac{\mu^{2-p}}{\phi\,(2 - p)}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{2 - p}{p - 1}, \qquad \theta = \phi\,(p - 1)\,\mu^{p-1}$$

```{r simulate}
set.seed(111)
mu <- 3; p <- 1.6; phi <- 8
lambda <- mu^(2 - p) / (phi * (2 - p))
alpha <- (2 - p) / (p - 1)
theta <- phi * (p - 1) * mu^(p - 1)
showers <- rpois(200000, lambda)
# the sum of n gammas is a gamma (0 showers: 0)
y_sim <- rgamma(200000, shape = alpha * showers, scale = theta)
c(
  mean = mean(y_sim),
  variance = var(y_sim),
  theory = phi * mu^p,
  dry = mean(y_sim == 0),
  theory_dry = exp(-lambda)
)
```

### Step 3: the regression

$$\log\mu_i = x_i^\top\beta$$

With the log link, $e^\beta$ is a ratio of mean amounts, "rain ratios" here. One coefficient per predictor, for everything: if the mean goes up, the formulas of step 2 make showers more frequent **and** larger at the same time.

### Step 4: estimating $p$

Given $p$, the $\beta$'s are estimated like those of any generalized linear model, from the mean and the variance function alone. $p$ itself is estimated by maximum likelihood. The density has no simple formula (it is an infinite series), but `mgcv` and the package `tweedie` compute it (Dunn & Smyth, 2005).

## 4. Fitting the model and reading the output

```{r fit}
fit <- gam(
  mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
  family = tw(),
  data = rain,
  method = "ML"
)
summary(fit)
p_hat   <- fit$family$getTheta(TRUE)
phi_hat <- fit$scale
b <- coef(fit); se <- sqrt(diag(vcov(fit)))
```

- **`Tweedie(p = ...)`**: the estimated power, `r f2(p_hat)`, between Poisson and gamma as it should be.
- **`wet_before`**: $e^{`r f2(b[["wet_before"]])`}$ = `r f2(exp(b[["wet_before"]]))`: after a wet day, the mean rain is **`r f1(exp(b[["wet_before"]]))` times** that after a dry day, in the same season.
- **`sin1` ... `cos2`**: the wave of the season, to be read as a picture.
- **`decade`**: $e^{`r f3(b[["decade"]])`}$ = `r f3(exp(b[["decade"]]))`: the mean rain changed by `r pct(exp(b[["decade"]]) - 1)` per decade, *p* `r pval(summary(fit)$p.table["decade", 4])`, **given** the season and whether the day before was wet. If wet days became rarer, part of that is already in `wet_before`. Section 7 asks about the trend without this condition.
- **`Scale est.`** = `r f1(phi_hat)`: the dispersion $\phi$.

The same as a plain GLM, with the power fixed at its estimate:

```{r glm}
fit_glm <- glm(
  mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
  family = tweedie(var.power = p_hat, link.power = 0),
  data = rain
)
round(rbind(gam = b, glm = coef(fit_glm)), 4)
round(rbind(gam_ML = se, glm = coef(summary(fit_glm))[, 2]), 4)
c(dispersion_ML = phi_hat, dispersion_Pearson = summary(fit_glm)$dispersion)
```

The coefficients agree. The standard errors do not, because the two estimate $\phi$ differently. `gam()` uses maximum likelihood, which trusts the Tweedie distribution, zeros and all; `glm()` uses the Pearson statistic, which looks only at the variance. If the distribution were right, the two $\phi$ would agree. They are `r f1(phi_hat)` and `r f1(summary(fit_glm)$dispersion)`: the largest rains are larger than the model expects. For honest intervals we use the larger ones.

## 5. Does it get the zeros right?

A Tweedie model has an opinion about the probability of a dry day, $e^{-\lambda}$, without a parameter of its own for it. Over the whole year:

```{r zeros}
lambda_i <- fitted(fit)^(2 - p_hat) / (phi_hat * (2 - p_hat))
c(observed_dry = mean(rain$mm == 0), expected_dry = mean(exp(-lambda_i)))
check <- data.frame(
  month = 1:12,
  mean_obs = tapply(rain$mm, rain$month, mean),
  mean_fit = tapply(fitted(fit), rain$month, mean),
  dry_obs = tapply(rain$mm == 0, rain$month, mean),
  dry_fit = tapply(exp(-lambda_i), rain$month, mean)
)
round(check, 2)
```

Overall, the model expects `r pct(mean(exp(-lambda_i)))` dry days, and there are `r pct(mean(rain$mm == 0))`. Excellent. **Month by month it fails.** In July it expects `r pct(check$dry_fit[7], 0)` dry days, and `r pct(check$dry_obs[7], 0)` are dry; in December `r pct(check$dry_fit[12], 0)`, and `r pct(check$dry_obs[12], 0)`. With one $\phi$ for the whole year, the probability of a dry day follows from the mean, and on Corfu it varies **more** than that. In summer, wet days are rarer than the mean suggests, and bring more rain than the model expects of them; in winter they are more frequent and lighter.

### A dispersion that changes with the season

Let $\phi$ depend on the season too. `mgcv` has a family for it, `twlss()`, with three formulas: for the mean, for the power and for the dispersion.

fit2 <- gam(

## 6. Against a two-part model

A [two-part model](EXAMPLE_HURDLE_TWO_PART.Rmd) has separate coefficients for **whether** and **how much**: a logistic regression and a gamma regression.

```{r two_part}
whether <- glm(
  I(mm > 0) ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
  family = binomial,
  data = rain
)
amount <- glm(
  mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + wet_before + decade,
  family = Gamma("log"),
  data = subset(rain, mm > 0)
)
mean_two <- predict(whether, rain, type = "response") * predict(amount, rain, type = "response")
shape <- 1 / MASS::gamma.dispersion(amount) # ML estimate of the dispersion of the gamma
ll_two <- as.numeric(logLik(whether)) +
  sum(dgamma(amount$y, shape = shape, scale = fitted(amount) / shape, log = TRUE))
aic_two <- -2 * ll_two + 2 * (length(coef(whether)) + length(coef(amount)) + 1)
ll_tw <- c(
  `Tweedie` = AIC(fit),
  `Tweedie, varying dispersion` = AIC(fit2),
  `two-part (logistic + gamma)` = aic_two
)
round(ll_tw)
compare <- cbind(
  Tweedie_ratio = exp(coef(fit)[c("wet_before", "decade")]),
  odds_of_rain = exp(coef(whether)[c("wet_before", "decade")]),
  amount_ratio = exp(coef(amount)[c("wet_before", "decade")])
)
round(compare, 3)
```

By the AIC the order is: `r paste(names(sort(ll_tw)), collapse = ", then ")`. The row of `wet_before` shows what the Tweedie model adds up: after a wet day the **odds of rain** are `r f1(compare["wet_before", "odds_of_rain"])` times as high, and the **amount**, if it rains, `r f2(compare["wet_before", "amount_ratio"])` times. The Tweedie ratio of `r f1(compare["wet_before", "Tweedie_ratio"])` is the total of both.

| | Tweedie | Two-part |
|---|---|---|
| Coefficients per predictor | one: the effect on the mean | two: whether, and how much |
| Zeros | follow from the mean and $\phi$ | a model of their own |
| Best for | totals (rain per month, cost per policy) and simple reporting | understanding the process |
| Sum of several days or policies | stays Tweedie with the same $p$ | leaves the model |

## 7. The trend that is not one

Has the rain of Corfu changed? Now we leave `wet_before` out, because a change of it would be part of the answer, and we let the days of a year be related (cluster-robust standard errors). The total with a Tweedie model, and the two parts:

```{r trend}
by_year <- function(m, d) coeftest(m, vcov = vcovCL(m, cluster = d$year))["decade", ]
wet_days <- subset(rain, mm > 0)
tw0 <- glm(
  mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade,
  family = tweedie(var.power = p_hat, link.power = 0),
  data = rain
)
st <- by_year(tw0, rain)
sw <- by_year(
  glm(I(mm > 0) ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade, family = binomial, data = rain),
  rain
)
sa <- by_year(
  glm(mm ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade, family = Gamma("log"), data = wet_days),
  wet_days
)
s1 <- by_year(
  glm(I(mm >= 1) ~ sin1 + cos1 + sin2 + cos2 + decade, family = binomial, data = rain),
  rain
)
signif(
  rbind(
    `total (Tweedie)` = st,
    `whether (logistic)` = sw,
    `how much (gamma)` = sa,
    `at least 1 mm (logistic)` = s1
  ),
  3
)
yearly <- do.call(
  rbind,
  lapply(split(rain, rain$year), function(d) {
    data.frame(
      year = d$year[1],
      days = nrow(d),
      wet = mean(d$mm > 0),
      wet1 = mean(d$mm >= 1),
      per_wet = mean(d$mm[d$mm > 0]),
      mean = mean(d$mm)
    )
  })
)
yearly <- subset(yearly, days >= 350)
```

```{r trend_chart}
yl <- rbind(
  data.frame(year = yearly$year, value = 100 * yearly$wet, what = "wet days (%)"),
  data.frame(year = yearly$year, value = yearly$per_wet, what = "rain on a wet day (mm)"),
  data.frame(year = yearly$year, value = yearly$mean, what = "rain per day (mm)")
)
yl$what <- factor(
  yl$what,
  levels = c("wet days (%)", "rain on a wet day (mm)", "rain per day (mm)")

ggplot(yl, aes(year, value)) +
  geom_point(size = 1.2, colour = "grey40") +

  labs(title = "Corfu, year by year", x = NULL, y = NULL) +
  theme_bw()
```

The **total** changed by `r pct(exp(st[[1]]) - 1)` per decade, which is not distinguishable from zero (*p* `r pval(st[[4]])`). But over the decades the **odds of a wet day fell**, by `r pct(1 - exp(sw[[1]]))` per decade (*p* `r pval(sw[[4]])`), and the **rain on a wet day rose**, by `r pct(exp(sa[[1]]) - 1)` per decade (*p* `r pval(sa[[4]])`). In the mean, the two partly cancel. Fewer wet days with more rain each would be a real change of the climate. But it could also be a change of the **measurement**: if small amounts of a few tenths of a millimetre were recorded less often, there would be fewer wet days with a larger average. Counting only days with at least 1 mm, the odds change by `r pct(exp(s1[[1]]) - 1)` per decade (*p* `r pval(s1[[4]])`). `r if (s1[[4]] > .05) "So most of the trend is in the smallest amounts, and the suspicion of the measurement is justified." else "So the fall is not only a matter of the smallest amounts."` One station with changed instruments cannot settle it.

## 8. Things to keep in mind

- **$1 < p < 2$ only** for data with zeros. If the estimate runs to one of the limits, think again about the model.
- **One coefficient for everything** is the strength and the weakness. If a predictor acts differently on whether and on how much, a Tweedie model gives the sum of the two and hides the parts.
- **Check the zeros in groups**, not only overall.
- **The standard errors depend on how $\phi$ is estimated.** Report which. Robust (sandwich) errors are a good alternative.
- **Comparing AIC** works only if $p$ and $\phi$ are estimated by maximum likelihood. A `glm()` with the family of `statmod` has no AIC of its own (`tweedie::AICtweedie()` computes it).
- **Exposure**: for claims per policy and year, use `offset(log(exposure))`, or weights. This is the standard model for the *pure premium* in car insurance (Smyth & Jørgensen, 2002).
- **Dependence.** Days are not independent. We let the day before into the model; the correlation of neighbouring residuals is then `r f2(acf(residuals(fit, type = "pearson"), plot = FALSE)$acf[2])`.
- **Extremes.** A Tweedie model is for means. For the size of a flood, use extreme-value models.

## 9. Reporting the results in APA style

A report (APA 7th edition) needs the share of zeros, the power and how it was estimated, the dispersion and what it depends on, the ratios with intervals, and the check of the zeros. The numbers come from the R objects:

```{r apa_numbers}
s_glm <- coef(summary(fit_glm))
low <- exp(s_glm[, 1] - 1.96 * s_glm[, 2]); high <- exp(s_glm[, 1] + 1.96 * s_glm[, 2])
keep <- c("wet_before", "decade")
apa_table <- data.frame(
  Predictor = c("Wet day before", "Decade"),
  tw = paste0(f2(exp(s_glm[keep, 1])), " [", f2(low[keep]), ", ", f2(high[keep]), "]"),
  or = paste0(
    f2(exp(coef(whether)[keep])),
    " [",
    f2(exp(confint.default(whether)[keep, 1])),
    ", ",
    f2(exp(confint.default(whether)[keep, 2])),
    "]"
  ),
  am = paste0(
    f2(exp(coef(amount)[keep])),
    " [",
    f2(exp(confint.default(amount)[keep, 1])),
    ", ",
    f2(exp(confint.default(amount)[keep, 2])),
    "]"
  )

```

**Method** (data analysis):

> Daily rain at Corfu airport, 1955–2024 (*N* = `r num(nrow(rain))` days; Menne et al., 2012), was zero on `r pct(mean(rain$mm == 0))` of the days and right-skewed on the others. We used a generalized linear model with a Tweedie (compound Poisson–gamma) distribution and a log link, with two harmonics of the season, an indicator of rain on the day before, and a linear trend in decades. The power parameter was estimated by maximum likelihood; standard errors use the Pearson estimate of the dispersion. A second model let the dispersion depend on the same predictors (Wood et al., 2016). Results were compared with a two-part model (logistic and gamma regression). Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`) with the packages mgcv and statmod.

**Results:**

> The power was `r f2(p_hat)`. After a wet day the mean rain was `r f2(exp(s_glm["wet_before", 1]))` times that after a dry day, 95% CI [`r f2(low[["wet_before"]])`, `r f2(high[["wet_before"]])`]. Given the season and the day before, there was no trend in the mean, ratio per decade = `r f2(exp(s_glm["decade", 1]))`, 95% CI [`r f2(low[["decade"]])`, `r f2(high[["decade"]])`] (Table 1); without the day before, ratio = `r f2(exp(st[[1]]))`, *p* `r pval(st[[4]])`. The model expected `r pct(mean(exp(-lambda_i)))` dry days overall (observed `r pct(mean(rain$mm == 0))`), but missed the share of single months by up to `r f1(100 * dry_error[["one"]])` percentage points; with a dispersion that depended on the season and the day before, the largest error was `r f1(100 * dry_error[["seasonal"]])` points. In a two-part model without the day before, the odds of a wet day decreased over the decades, *OR* = `r f2(exp(sw[[1]]))` per decade, *p* `r pval(sw[[4]])`, while the rain on wet days increased, ratio = `r f2(exp(sa[[1]]))`, *p* `r pval(sa[[4]])`.

## 10. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | Exact zeros and positive skewed amounts, and the interest is in the mean or the total |
| **The distribution** | A Poisson number of gamma amounts; $\text{Var} = \phi\mu^p$ with $1 < p < 2$ |
| **Dry probability** | $\exp\{-\mu^{2-p} / [\phi(2 - p)]\}$ |
| **How (R)** | `mgcv::gam(y ~ x, family = tw())` estimates $p$; `glm(y ~ x, family = statmod::tweedie(var.power = p, link.power = 0))` |
| **Reading it** | $e^\beta$: ratio of means, zeros included |
| **Varying dispersion** | `mgcv::gam(list(y ~ x, ~ 1, ~ z), family = twlss())` |
| **Alternative** | Two-part model, when whether and how much are separate questions |
| **Related pages** | [Two-part models](EXAMPLE_HURDLE_TWO_PART.Rmd), [gamma](EXAMPLE_GAMMA.Rmd), [splines and GAMs](EXAMPLE_SPLINES_GAM.Rmd) |

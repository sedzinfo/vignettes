Difference-in-Differences: Does a Higher Minimum Wage Cost Jobs?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Difference-in-differences in R on the 410 fast-food restaurants of Card and Krueger: the four means, the same number from a regression, from changes and from two-way fixed effects, clustered standard errors by hand, the parallel trends assumption, comparisons within New Jersey, other outcomes and definitions, how small a difference in trends or a shock to a state changes the conclusion, why controlling for the first value gives another answer, what goes wrong when a treatment comes in waves (two-way fixed effects against group-time effects by hand and with did), and APA reporting.

The question: two states and a law

On 1 April 1992 the minimum wage in New Jersey rose from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour. In Pennsylvania, on the other side of the river, it stayed at $4.25. What happened to the jobs in fast-food restaurants, where many people earn the minimum?

Two simple comparisons come to mind, and both are wrong:

  • New Jersey after against before. But 1992 was a year of recession. Jobs may have changed anyway.
  • New Jersey against Pennsylvania, after. But the restaurants in the two states may have been different from the start.

Difference-in-differences (DiD) combines them. The change in Pennsylvania shows what happened anyway. The change in New Jersey, minus the change in Pennsylvania, is the effect of the law. It is simple enough for a pocket calculator. What it needs is one assumption, and most of this page is about that one.

The study by Card and Krueger (1993, published in the American Economic Review in 1994) is one of the best-known in economics, and one of the most disputed. This page uses it to teach the method. It does not settle the question.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: four means

\[\widehat{DiD} = (\bar y_{NJ,\text{after}} - \bar y_{NJ,\text{before}}) - (\bar y_{PA,\text{after}} - \bar y_{PA,\text{before}})\]

In New Jersey a restaurant had 20.44 full-time equivalents before and 21.03 after the law: a small rise. In Pennsylvania employment fell, from 23.33 to 21.17. The difference in differences is +2.75 FTE per restaurant. The textbook theory of the labour market expects a minus.

Step 2: a regression

The same number is the coefficient of the product term in a regression with two indicator variables:

\[y_{it} = \alpha + \beta\,NJ_i + \gamma\,\text{After}_t + \delta\,(NJ_i \times \text{After}_t) + \varepsilon_{it}\]

\(\alpha\) is Pennsylvania before, \(\beta\) the difference between the states before, \(\gamma\) the change in Pennsylvania, and \(\delta\) the DiD. The regression also gives a standard error, but the two interviews of the same restaurant are not independent. So the errors are clustered by restaurant, here by hand:

\[V = \frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{N-1}{N-K}\,(X^\top X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_g X_g^\top e_g e_g^\top X_g\Big)(X^\top X)^{-1}\]

Code 
long <- rbind(
  with(stores, data.frame(id, nj, after = 0, fte = fte1)),
  with(stores, data.frame(id, nj, after = 1, fte = fte2))
)
long <- subset(long, !is.na(fte))
fit <- lm(fte ~ nj * after, long)
X <- model.matrix(fit)
scores <- rowsum(X * residuals(fit), long$id)
G <- nrow(scores)
N <- nrow(X)
K <- ncol(X)
V <- G /
  (G - 1) *
  (N - 1) /
  (N - K) *
  solve(crossprod(X)) %*% crossprod(scores) %*% solve(crossprod(X))
clustered <- feols(fte ~ nj * after, long, cluster = ~ id)
rbind(
  estimate = c(
    lm = coef(fit)[["nj:after"]],
    by_hand = coef(fit)[["nj:after"]],
    feols = coef(clustered)[["nj:after"]]
  ),
  SE = c(
    sqrt(vcov(fit)["nj:after", "nj:after"]),
    sqrt(V["nj:after", "nj:after"]),
    se(clustered)[["nj:after"]]
  )
)

Step 3: changes, or two-way fixed effects

With two periods one can also take the change of each restaurant and compare the two groups with an ordinary regression. Or give every restaurant and every period its own constant (two-way fixed effects, TWFE, see panel data). For restaurants with both interviews that is the same:

Step 4: the assumption

What is the causal effect? The outcome of New Jersey after the law, minus what it would have been without it. The second part nobody has seen. DiD fills it in: New Jersey would have changed like Pennsylvania. That is the parallel trends assumption:

\[E[y_{\text{after}}(0) - y_{\text{before}}(0) \mid NJ] = E[y_{\text{after}}(0) - y_{\text{before}}(0) \mid PA]\]

(\(y(0)\): employment without the law.)

5. Other ways of looking

6. How far can we trust it?

The trends

With several periods before the law, one could look whether the two states moved together. Here there are only two interviews, so parallel trends cannot be checked at all. What one can do is to ask how wrong the assumption must be (the idea is developed by Rambachan & Roth, 2023). If New Jersey would have changed by \(d\) FTE more than Pennsylvania without any law, the effect is \(\widehat{DiD} - d\):

Code 
est <- coef(clustered)[["nj:after"]]; se_cl <- se(clustered)[["nj:after"]]
cbind(
  value = c(
    d_that_removes_the_significance = est - 1.96 * se_cl,
    d_that_removes_the_effect = est,
    change_in_Pennsylvania = diff(m["Pennsylvania", ])[[1]]
  )
)
                                    value
d_that_removes_the_significance  0.192656
d_that_removes_the_effect        2.753606
change_in_Pennsylvania          -2.165584

A difference in trends of only 0.19 FTE takes the interval to zero, and one of 2.75 takes the whole effect away. The change in Pennsylvania was -2.17.

Two states are two observations

The standard error counted 410 restaurants. But the law was given to states, and there are two. Suppose each state had a shock of its own in 1992 (weather, the local economy), with a standard deviation \(\sigma\) in the change per restaurant. Then the variance of the DiD is the one from the restaurants plus \(2\sigma^2\) (Donald & Lang, 2007):

Shocks with an SD of 0.37 FTE (1.8% of a restaurant’s staff) are enough to take the significance away. And nothing in the data can tell us how large they are, because each state was seen once. With an SD of 1 the interval is [-1.0, 6.5]. A large loss of jobs is still hard to reconcile with the data; a small one is not. This is the general lesson of Bertrand et al. (2004) as well: cluster at the level at which the treatment was given, and have many such clusters.

What others found

Neumark and Wascher (2000) collected payroll records of restaurants in the same two states and found a loss of jobs in New Jersey. Card and Krueger (2000) answered with official payroll records: employment grew as fast in New Jersey as in Pennsylvania, or slightly faster. How good the interview data are was a part of that debate.

7. When the treatment comes in waves

Laws rarely come in one place and on one day. Some states raise the wage in 1990, some in 1994, some never. For twenty years people ran one regression with unit and time effects and a treatment indicator (TWFE) on such data. If the effect grows with time or differs between places, that is wrong (Goodman-Bacon, 2021): the regression also uses places that were treated early as controls for those treated late. A simulation where we know the truth:

Code 
set.seed(231); n_unit <- 300; n_time <- 10
panel <- expand.grid(t = 1:n_time, id = 1:n_unit)
# treated from period 4, from period 8, never
panel$first <- rep(c(4, 8, 0), each = n_unit / 3)[panel$id]
panel$treated <- as.numeric(panel$first > 0 & panel$t >= panel$first)
# grows each year; faster in the early group

# parallel trends hold exactly

The true mean effect is 2.98. Every single effect is positive, and the trends are exactly parallel. TWFE says 1.51, and without the group that was never treated even -0.07.

The remedy (Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021) is to go back to the simple idea of step 1. For every group \(g\) and every period \(t\), do one DiD: with the period before the group’s treatment as “before”, and the never-treated as controls. Then average:

\[ATT(g, t) = (\bar y_{g,t} - \bar y_{g,g-1}) - (\bar y_{\text{never},t} - \bar y_{\text{never},g-1})\]

8. Things to keep in mind

  • The assumption is about changes, not levels. The groups may differ, but they must not be on different tracks.
  • Draw the periods before the treatment, as many as there are. Parallel trends before do not prove parallel trends after, but a difference is a warning.
  • The scale (levels, logs, percentages) is a part of the assumption.
  • Cluster at the level at which the treatment was given. With few clusters the usual formula is too optimistic. With two, there is no honest standard error at all.
  • Anticipation: if people react before the law starts, the period before is already treated.
  • Spillovers: if the customers of New Jersey drive to Pennsylvania, the control group is affected as well.
  • Composition: in a repeated cross-section the groups may change who is in them.
  • Control variables must not be changed by the treatment.
  • Staggered treatment: do not use the plain two-way regression. Use did, fixest::sunab(), or one DiD at a time.
  • Relatives: panel data, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and interrupted time series when there is no control group.

Method (data analysis):

The effect of New Jersey’s increase in the minimum wage (from $4.25 to $5.05 on 1 April 1992) on employment in fast-food restaurants was estimated with a difference-in-differences design. 410 restaurants in New Jersey (n = 331) and eastern Pennsylvania (n = 79), where the minimum wage did not change, were interviewed before and after the increase (Card & Krueger, 1993). The outcome was the number of full-time equivalents (full-time employees and managers plus half of the part-time employees); restaurants that had closed permanently were counted as zero. We regressed the outcome on state, period and their product, with standard errors clustered by restaurant. The analysis assumes that employment in New Jersey would have changed as in Pennsylvania without the law; with two interviews this cannot be examined, and we report how large a violation would change the conclusion. Comparisons within New Jersey used the increase that each restaurant needed to reach the new minimum. Analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.6.2; R Core Team, 2026).

Results:

Mean employment rose from 20.44 to 21.03 full-time equivalents in New Jersey and fell from 23.33 to 21.17 in Pennsylvania (Table 1). The difference in differences was 2.75, SE = 1.31, 95% CI [0.19, 5.32], p = .036. Across the restaurants of both states, a wage gap of 10% was associated with a change of 1.64 full-time equivalents (SE = 0.60). On the log scale the difference was 0.08 (p = .111), and adjusted for employment at the first interview it was 1.34 (SE = 0.99). A difference in trends of 0.19 between the states, or random shocks to the states with a standard deviation of 0.37, would suffice to make the result compatible with no effect. The data give no evidence for a loss of jobs, but the evidence for a gain is weak.

## The question: two states and a law

On 1 April 1992 the minimum wage in **New Jersey** rose from \$4.25 to \$5.05 an hour. In **Pennsylvania**, on the other side of the river, it stayed at \$4.25. What happened to the jobs in fast-food restaurants, where many people earn the minimum?

Two simple comparisons come to mind, and both are wrong:

- **New Jersey after against before.** But 1992 was a year of recession. Jobs may have changed anyway.
- **New Jersey against Pennsylvania, after.** But the restaurants in the two states may have been different from the start.

**Difference-in-differences** (DiD) combines them. The change in Pennsylvania shows what happened *anyway*. The change in New Jersey, **minus** the change in Pennsylvania, is the effect of the law. It is simple enough for a pocket calculator. What it needs is one assumption, and most of this page is about that one.

The study by Card and Krueger (1993, published in the *American Economic Review* in 1994) is one of the best-known in economics, and one of the most disputed. This page uses it to teach the method. It does not settle the question.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: four means

$$\widehat{DiD} = (\bar y_{NJ,\text{after}} - \bar y_{NJ,\text{before}}) - (\bar y_{PA,\text{after}} - \bar y_{PA,\text{before}})$$

```{r four_means}

```

In New Jersey a restaurant had `r f2(m["New Jersey", "before"])` full-time equivalents before and `r f2(m["New Jersey", "after"])` after the law: a small **rise**. In Pennsylvania employment **fell**, from `r f2(m["Pennsylvania", "before"])` to `r f2(m["Pennsylvania", "after"])`. The difference in differences is **+`r f2(did_hand)`** FTE per restaurant. The textbook theory of the labour market expects a minus.

### Step 2: a regression

The same number is the coefficient of the product term in a regression with two indicator variables:

$$y_{it} = \alpha + \beta\,NJ_i + \gamma\,\text{After}_t + \delta\,(NJ_i \times \text{After}_t) + \varepsilon_{it}$$

$\alpha$ is Pennsylvania before, $\beta$ the difference between the states before, $\gamma$ the change in Pennsylvania, and $\delta$ the DiD. The regression also gives a standard error, but the two interviews of the **same** restaurant are not independent. So the errors are *clustered* by restaurant, here by hand:

$$V = \frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{N-1}{N-K}\,(X^\top X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_g X_g^\top e_g e_g^\top X_g\Big)(X^\top X)^{-1}$$

```{r regression}
long <- rbind(
  with(stores, data.frame(id, nj, after = 0, fte = fte1)),
  with(stores, data.frame(id, nj, after = 1, fte = fte2))
)
long <- subset(long, !is.na(fte))
fit <- lm(fte ~ nj * after, long)
X <- model.matrix(fit)
scores <- rowsum(X * residuals(fit), long$id)
G <- nrow(scores)
N <- nrow(X)
K <- ncol(X)
V <- G /
  (G - 1) *
  (N - 1) /
  (N - K) *
  solve(crossprod(X)) %*% crossprod(scores) %*% solve(crossprod(X))
clustered <- feols(fte ~ nj * after, long, cluster = ~ id)
rbind(
  estimate = c(
    lm = coef(fit)[["nj:after"]],
    by_hand = coef(fit)[["nj:after"]],
    feols = coef(clustered)[["nj:after"]]
  ),
  SE = c(
    sqrt(vcov(fit)["nj:after", "nj:after"]),
    sqrt(V["nj:after", "nj:after"]),
    se(clustered)[["nj:after"]]
  )
)
```

The usual SE (`r f2(sqrt(vcov(fit)["nj:after", "nj:after"]))`) is too large here. It does not know that a restaurant is compared with itself.

### Step 3: changes, or two-way fixed effects

With two periods one can also take the **change** of each restaurant and compare the two groups with an ordinary regression. Or give every restaurant and every period its own constant (*two-way fixed effects*, TWFE, see [panel data](EXAMPLE_PANEL_DATA.Rmd)). For restaurants with both interviews that is the same:

```{r changes}

  both,
  sqrt(var(change[nj == 1]) / sum(nj == 1) + var(change[nj == 0]) / sum(nj == 0))

  `regression of the changes` = c(estimate = coef(change_fit)[["nj"]], SE = se(change_fit)[["nj"]]),
```

`r nrow(both)` of the `r nrow(stores)` restaurants have both values, and the result is `r f2(coef(change_fit)[["nj"]])` (*SE* `r f2(se(change_fit)[["nj"]])`).

### Step 4: the assumption

What is the causal effect? The outcome of New Jersey after the law, minus what it **would have been** without it. The second part nobody has seen. DiD fills it in: New Jersey would have changed like Pennsylvania. That is the **parallel trends** assumption:

$$E[y_{\text{after}}(0) - y_{\text{before}}(0) \mid NJ] = E[y_{\text{after}}(0) - y_{\text{before}}(0) \mid PA]$$

($y(0)$: employment without the law.)

## 5. Other ways of looking

### More than one comparison

Pennsylvania need not be the only comparison. In New Jersey some restaurants already paid \$5 or more. The law meant nothing to them. Card and Krueger used the **gap**: by how much a restaurant had to raise its starting wage to reach the new minimum.

```{r within_nj}
stores$gap <- with(stores, ifelse(nj == 0, 0, pmax(0, (5.05 - wage_st) / wage_st)))
both$gap <- stores$gap[match(both$id, stores$id)]
gap_all <- feols(change ~ gap, both, vcov = "hetero")
gap_nj <- feols(change ~ gap, subset(both, nj == 1), vcov = "hetero")
both$wage_group <- cut(
  both$wage_st,
  c(0, 4.251, 4.999, 10),
  labels = c("4.25", "4.26 to 4.99", "5.00 or more")
)
within <- with(
  subset(both, nj == 1 & !is.na(wage_group)),
  data.frame(
    starting_wage_before = levels(wage_group),
    restaurants = as.numeric(table(wage_group)),
    mean_before = as.numeric(tapply(fte1, wage_group, mean)),
    mean_change = as.numeric(tapply(change, wage_group, mean)),
    se = as.numeric(tapply(change, wage_group, function(v) sd(v) / sqrt(length(v))))
  )
)
print(data.frame(within[1:2], round(within[3:5], 2)), row.names = FALSE)
rbind(
  all_restaurants = c(slope = coef(gap_all)[["gap"]], SE = se(gap_all)[["gap"]]),
  new_jersey_only = c(coef(gap_nj)[["gap"]], se(gap_nj)[["gap"]])
)
mean_gap <- mean(stores$gap[stores$nj == 1], na.rm = TRUE)
```

In New Jersey, employment changed by `r f2(within$mean_change[1])` in the restaurants that paid \$4.25, and by `r f2(within$mean_change[3])` in those that already paid \$5. The slope on the gap (with both states) times the mean gap in New Jersey (`r f2(mean_gap)`) is `r f2(coef(gap_all)[["gap"]] * mean_gap)` FTE: the same sign as the DiD, and smaller. Two different control groups tell the same story. That is better evidence than one. (But the restaurants that already paid well were also **larger** before the law, `r f1(within$mean_before[3])` FTE against `r f1(within$mean_before[1])`, like those in Pennsylvania. Section 6 comes back to this.)

### Other outcomes, other definitions

```{r variants}
  d <- data.frame(nj = stores$nj, change = b - a)
  f <- feols(change ~ nj, d, vcov = "hetero")

}

    with(stores, empft + nmgrs + weight * emppt),

  )
}

open_again <- ifelse(stores$status2 == 3, NA, stores$fte2)
variants <- rbind(
  `FTE, part-time counts 0.5` = fte_w(0.5),
  `FTE, part-time counts 0.25` = fte_w(0.25),
  `FTE, part-time counts 1 (heads)` = fte_w(1),
  `FTE, closed restaurants left out` = change_in(stores$fte1, open_again),
  `log FTE` = change_in(pos(stores$fte1), pos(stores$fte2)),
  `full-time employees` = change_in(stores$empft, stores$empft2),
  `part-time employees` = change_in(stores$emppt, stores$emppt2),
  `hours open per day` = change_in(stores$hrsopen, stores$hrsopen2),
  `starting wage (dollars)` = change_in(stores$wage_st, stores$wage_st2),
```

- The result does not depend much on how a part-timer is counted (`r f2(v("FTE, part-time counts 1 (heads)", "estimate"))` to `r f2(v("FTE, part-time counts 0.25", "estimate"))`), nor on the closed restaurants.

- **Prices** of a meal rose by `r pct(exp(v("log price of a full meal", "estimate")) - 1, 1)` more in New Jersey (*p* `r pval(v("log price of a full meal", "p"))`). The customers paid a part of the law.

## 6. How far can we trust it?

### The trends

With several periods before the law, one could look whether the two states moved together. Here there are only **two** interviews, so parallel trends cannot be checked at all. What one can do is to ask how wrong the assumption must be (the idea is developed by Rambachan & Roth, 2023). If New Jersey would have changed by $d$ FTE more than Pennsylvania without any law, the effect is $\widehat{DiD} - d$:

```{r trend_sensitivity}
est <- coef(clustered)[["nj:after"]]; se_cl <- se(clustered)[["nj:after"]]
cbind(
  value = c(
    d_that_removes_the_significance = est - 1.96 * se_cl,
    d_that_removes_the_effect = est,
    change_in_Pennsylvania = diff(m["Pennsylvania", ])[[1]]
  )
)
```

A difference in trends of only `r f2(est - 1.96 * se_cl)` FTE takes the interval to zero, and one of `r f2(est)` takes the whole effect away. The change in Pennsylvania was `r f2(diff(m["Pennsylvania", ]))`.

### Two states are two observations

The standard error counted `r num(G)` restaurants. But the law was given to **states**, and there are two. Suppose each state had a shock of its own in 1992 (weather, the local economy), with a standard deviation $\sigma$ in the change per restaurant. Then the variance of the DiD is the one from the restaurants **plus** $2\sigma^2$ (Donald & Lang, 2007):

```{r state_shock, fig.height = 4}
sig <- seq(0, 2, length.out = 100); sdw <- sqrt(se_cl^2 + 2 * sig^2)
ggplot(data.frame(sig, lo = est - 1.96 * sdw, hi = est + 1.96 * sdw), aes(sig)) +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = lo, ymax = hi), fill = "#2a78d6", alpha = 0.25) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = est, colour = "#2a78d6") +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(
    title = "95% interval of the DiD, if states have shocks of their own",
    x = "standard deviation of the shock of a state (FTE per restaurant)",
    y = "DiD"
  ) +
  theme_bw()
sigma_limit <- sqrt(((est / 1.96)^2 - se_cl^2) / 2)
interval1 <- est + c(-1.96, 1.96) * sqrt(se_cl^2 + 2)
cbind(
  value = c(
    sigma_at_which_the_interval_reaches_zero = sigma_limit,
    low_if_sigma_1 = interval1[1],
    high_if_sigma_1 = interval1[2]
  )
)
```

Shocks with an SD of `r f2(sigma_limit)` FTE (`r pct(sigma_limit / m["New Jersey", "before"], 1)` of a restaurant's staff) are enough to take the significance away. And nothing in the data can tell us how large they are, because each state was seen once. With an SD of 1 the interval is [`r f1(interval1[1])`, `r f1(interval1[2])`]. A large loss of jobs is still hard to reconcile with the data; a small one is not. This is the general lesson of Bertrand et al. (2004) as well: cluster at the level at which the treatment was given, and have **many** such clusters.

### Were the groups alike?

The restaurants in Pennsylvania were **larger** at the first interview (`r f1(m["Pennsylvania", "before"])` against `r f1(m["New Jersey", "before"])`). Large ones tend to shrink, and small ones to grow, if only because an interview catches a busy or a quiet week (*regression to the mean*). One could adjust for the first value:

```{r ancova}
ancova <- feols(fte2 ~ nj + fte1, both, vcov = "hetero")
rbind(
  `difference in differences` = c(estimate = coef(change_fit)[["nj"]], SE = se(change_fit)[["nj"]]),
  `adjusted for the first value` = c(coef(ancova)[["nj"]], se(ancova)[["nj"]])
)
c(
  slope_of_the_first_value = coef(ancova)[["fte1"]],
  correlation_of_the_two_interviews = with(both, cor(fte1, fte2))
)
```

With the first value as a control, the effect is `r f2(coef(ancova)[["nj"]])` (*SE* `r f2(se(ancova)[["nj"]])`). The two methods answer with different assumptions: DiD says that the states differ **permanently**, and that the difference would have stayed the same; the other says that a restaurant with 20 FTE in New Jersey and one with 20 in Pennsylvania would have had the same future. DiD forces the slope of the first value to be 1; the data say `r f2(coef(ancova)[["fte1"]])`. Neither is right by itself. (The conflict is known as Lord's paradox.)

### What others found

Neumark and Wascher (2000) collected payroll records of restaurants in the same two states and found a **loss** of jobs in New Jersey. Card and Krueger (2000) answered with official payroll records: employment grew as fast in New Jersey as in Pennsylvania, or slightly faster. How good the interview data are was a part of that debate.

## 7. When the treatment comes in waves

Laws rarely come in one place and on one day. Some states raise the wage in 1990, some in 1994, some never. For twenty years people ran one regression with unit and time effects and a treatment indicator (TWFE) on such data. If the effect **grows with time** or differs between places, that is wrong (Goodman-Bacon, 2021): the regression also uses places that were treated **early** as controls for those treated **late**. A simulation where we know the truth:

```{r staggered}
set.seed(231); n_unit <- 300; n_time <- 10
panel <- expand.grid(t = 1:n_time, id = 1:n_unit)
# treated from period 4, from period 8, never
panel$first <- rep(c(4, 8, 0), each = n_unit / 3)[panel$id]
panel$treated <- as.numeric(panel$first > 0 & panel$t >= panel$first)
# grows each year; faster in the early group
panel$effect <- panel$treated * ifelse(panel$first == 4, 1, 0.3) * (panel$t - panel$first + 1)
unit_level <- rnorm(n_unit, 0, 2) + ifelse(rep(c(4, 8, 0), each = n_unit / 3) == 4, 2, 0)
time_level <- cumsum(rnorm(n_time, 0.2, 0.5))
# parallel trends hold exactly
panel$y <- unit_level[panel$id] + time_level[panel$t] + panel$effect + rnorm(nrow(panel))
truth      <- mean(panel$effect[panel$treated == 1])
twfe       <- feols(y ~ treated | id + t, panel, cluster = ~ id)
twfe_no_never <- feols(y ~ treated | id + t, subset(panel, first > 0), cluster = ~ id)
c(
  true_mean_effect = truth,
  TWFE = coef(twfe)[["treated"]],
  TWFE_without_the_never_treated = coef(twfe_no_never)[["treated"]]
)
```

The true mean effect is `r f2(truth)`. Every single effect is positive, and the trends are exactly parallel. TWFE says `r f2(coef(twfe)[["treated"]])`, and without the group that was never treated even `r f2(coef(twfe_no_never)[["treated"]])`.

The remedy (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021) is to go back to the simple idea of step 1. For every group $g$ and every period $t$, do **one** DiD: with the period before the group's treatment as "before", and the never-treated as controls. Then average:

$$ATT(g, t) = (\bar y_{g,t} - \bar y_{g,g-1}) - (\bar y_{\text{never},t} - \bar y_{\text{never},g-1})$$

```{r group_time}
ym <- function(g, t) mean(panel$y[panel$first == g & panel$t == t])
att <- do.call(
  rbind,
  lapply(c(4, 8), function(g) {
    data.frame(
      group = g,
      t = g:n_time,
      by_hand = sapply(g:n_time, function(t) (ym(g, t) - ym(g, g - 1)) - (ym(0, t) - ym(0, g - 1))),
      truth = sapply(g:n_time, function(t) mean(panel$effect[panel$first == g & panel$t == t]))
    )
  })
)
cs <- did::att_gt(
  yname = "y",
  tname = "t",
  idname = "id",
  gname = "first",
  data = panel,
  control_group = "nevertreated",
  bstrap = FALSE,
  cband = FALSE,
  base_period = "universal"
)
att$did_package <- cs$att[match(paste(att$group, att$t), paste(cs$group, cs$t))]
print(round(att, 2), row.names = FALSE)
overall <- did::aggte(cs, type = "simple", bstrap = FALSE, cband = FALSE)
c(
  mean_by_hand = mean(att$by_hand),
  did_package = overall$overall.att,
  SE = overall$overall.se,
  truth = truth
)
```

```{r group_time_chart, fig.height = 4}

es$rel <- es$t - es$group
```

The group-time effects agree with the truth, and their mean is `r f2(mean(att$by_hand))`. The points to the left of the line are the check that we could not make with two interviews: before the treatment there should be nothing. Of the `r nrow(pre)` intervals, `r sum(abs(pre$est) > 1.96 * pre$se)` `r if (sum(abs(pre$est) > 1.96 * pre$se) == 1) "misses" else "miss"` zero. Here we know that this is chance. With real data one would not, and a test of the trends before the treatment can fail in both directions.

## 8. Things to keep in mind

- **The assumption is about changes**, not levels. The groups may differ, but they must not be on different tracks.
- **Draw the periods before** the treatment, as many as there are. Parallel trends before do not prove parallel trends after, but a difference is a warning.
- **The scale** (levels, logs, percentages) is a part of the assumption.
- **Cluster** at the level at which the treatment was given. With few clusters the usual formula is too optimistic. With two, there is no honest standard error at all.
- **Anticipation**: if people react before the law starts, the period before is already treated.
- **Spillovers**: if the customers of New Jersey drive to Pennsylvania, the control group is affected as well.
- **Composition**: in a repeated cross-section the groups may change who is in them.
- **Control variables** must not be changed by the treatment.
- **Staggered treatment**: do not use the plain two-way regression. Use `did`, `fixest::sunab()`, or one DiD at a time.
- **Relatives**: [panel data](EXAMPLE_PANEL_DATA.Rmd), [instrumental variables](EXAMPLE_INSTRUMENTAL_VARIABLES.Rmd), [regression discontinuity](EXAMPLE_REGRESSION_DISCONTINUITY.Rmd), and [interrupted time series](EXAMPLE_ARIMAX.Rmd) when there is no control group.

A report (APA 7th edition) needs the treatment, the groups and the periods, the four means, the estimate with clustered standard errors and the level of the clusters, the argument for parallel trends with the evidence for it (or its absence), the other comparisons, and the sensitivity. ```

**Method** (data analysis):

> The effect of New Jersey's increase in the minimum wage (from \$4.25 to \$5.05 on 1 April 1992) on employment in fast-food restaurants was estimated with a difference-in-differences design. `r nrow(stores)` restaurants in New Jersey (*n* = `r sum(stores$nj == 1)`) and eastern Pennsylvania (*n* = `r sum(stores$nj == 0)`), where the minimum wage did not change, were interviewed before and after the increase (Card & Krueger, 1993). The outcome was the number of full-time equivalents (full-time employees and managers plus half of the part-time employees); restaurants that had closed permanently were counted as zero. We regressed the outcome on state, period and their product, with standard errors clustered by restaurant. The analysis assumes that employment in New Jersey would have changed as in Pennsylvania without the law; with two interviews this cannot be examined, and we report how large a violation would change the conclusion. Comparisons within New Jersey used the increase that each restaurant needed to reach the new minimum. Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`).

**Results:**

> Mean employment rose from `r f2(m["New Jersey", "before"])` to `r f2(m["New Jersey", "after"])` full-time equivalents in New Jersey and fell from `r f2(m["Pennsylvania", "before"])` to `r f2(m["Pennsylvania", "after"])` in Pennsylvania (Table 1). The difference in differences was `r f2(est)`, *SE* = `r f2(se_cl)`, 95% CI [`r f2(ci_did[[1]])`, `r f2(ci_did[[2]])`], *p* `r pval(pvalue(clustered)[["nj:after"]])`. Across the restaurants of both states, a wage gap of 10% was associated with a change of `r f2(0.1 * coef(gap_all)[["gap"]])` full-time equivalents (*SE* = `r f2(0.1 * se(gap_all)[["gap"]])`). On the log scale the difference was `r f2(v("log FTE", "estimate"))` (*p* `r pval(v("log FTE", "p"))`), and adjusted for employment at the first interview it was `r f2(coef(ancova)[["nj"]])` (*SE* = `r f2(se(ancova)[["nj"]])`). A difference in trends of `r f2(est - 1.96 * se_cl)` between the states, or random shocks to the states with a standard deviation of `r f2(sigma_limit)`, would suffice to make the result compatible with no effect. The data give no evidence for a loss of jobs, but the evidence for a gain is weak.

## 10. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | A treatment reaches one group and not another, and both are seen before and after |
| **The estimate** | Change in the treated group minus change in the control group |
| **The assumption** | Without the treatment both would have changed alike (parallel trends) |
| **How (R)** | `lm(y ~ group * after)`, or `feols(y ~ treated | unit + time, cluster = ~ unit)` |
| **Standard errors** | Clustered at the level of the treatment; few clusters are a problem |
| **Check** | Periods before the treatment, other control groups, placebo outcomes, other scales, sensitivity |
| **In waves** | Group-time effects (`did::att_gt()`, `fixest::sunab()`), not plain TWFE |

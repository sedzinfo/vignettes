Robust Regression: When a Few Countries Pull the Line

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Robust regression in R with MASS::rlm() and robustbase::lmrob(), on World Bank data for about 190 countries: life expectancy against health spending. Why least squares is fragile, outliers, leverage and influence, M-estimation with Huber and bisquare weights, MM-estimation and the breakdown point, what to do with outliers instead of deleting them, robust against sandwich standard errors, and APA reporting.

The question: do a few odd countries decide the answer?

Countries that spend more on health have longer-living people. But how much longer, for each extra dollar? To answer, we draw a line through about 190 countries and read its slope.

Some countries do not follow the pattern. The Central African Republic spends little and its people live much shorter than even that low spending predicts. A few small island states spend a lot and do not live longer. Ordinary least squares (OLS) gives every country the same say, and lets such countries drag the line towards them.

This page asks: how much does one tenfold step in health spending buy in life expectancy, when we do not let a handful of odd countries decide? The answer is robust regression: estimation that resists outliers.

Resists outliers in \(y\) Resists bad leverage points Efficient when data are clean
OLS (lm()) no no the best
M-estimation (rlm()) yes no high (about 95%)
MM-estimation (lmrob()) yes yes high (about 95%)
LTS (lqs()) yes yes low

1. The data

The World Bank publishes the World Development Indicators through an open API: free, no key and no login. We take two indicators for the year 2021 and the list of countries:

  • SH.XPD.CHEX.PC.CD: current health expenditure per capita, in current US dollars. The World Bank gets it from the WHO Global Health Expenditure Database. “Current” means money spent in that year on health goods and services (not on buildings); “per capita” means per person.
  • SP.DYN.LE00.IN: life expectancy at birth, in years: how long a newborn would live if the death rates of that year stayed the same for life. It comes from the United Nations World Population Prospects and national statistical offices.
  • The country list, with region and income group, which tells countries from aggregates such as “World” or “Sub-Saharan Africa”. The indicators contain both.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: why least squares is fragile

OLS chooses the line that makes the sum of squared residuals \(\sum_i e_i^2\) as small as possible, where \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\). Squaring is the problem: a country that is 20 years off the line costs \(400\), a hundred times as much as one that is 2 years off. To save that cost, the line moves towards it. One bad point can move the OLS line as far as it likes. To see this, we take one country with a high spending, Switzerland, and slide its life expectancy from 0 to 120 years. At each value we refit the line:

Code 
slide <- seq(0, 120, by = 4)

  z <- d
  z["Switzerland", "life"] <- value
  c(OLS = coef(lm(life ~ logspend, z))[[2]],

Step 2: three kinds of unusual point

“Outlier” is used for three different things, with three different checks. All three come from the OLS fit, with \(p = 2\) parameters and \(n\) points:

Kind What it is Measure
Outlier in \(y\) far from the line, up or down, at a usual \(x\) studentized residual \(t_i\): the residual divided by its standard error, with the point left out of the fit. Beyond \(\pm 2\) or \(\pm 3\) is unusual
High-leverage point far from the other points in \(x\), wherever its \(y\) is hat value \(h_i\), the diagonal of \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\). The average is \(p/n\); above \(2p/n\) is high
Influential point removing it changes the fit a lot Cook’s distance \(D_i = \dfrac{e_i^2}{p\,s^2}\dfrac{h_i}{(1 - h_i)^2}\): large when both the residual and the leverage are large. A common flag is \(4/n\)

A high-leverage point that sits on the trend is a good leverage point: it makes the line more precise. One that sits off the trend is a bad leverage point, and is the most dangerous kind: it has the leverage to pull the line, and the residual to pull it the wrong way. Because the line bends towards it, its own residual in OLS is small, and the point hides itself.

Step 3: M-estimation, the idea

Instead of squares, M-estimation (the “M” is for maximum likelihood type; Huber, 1964) minimises a loss function \(\rho\) of the scaled residuals:

\[\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \rho\!\left(\frac{e_i}{s}\right), \qquad e_i = y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top\beta\]

\(s\) is a robust scale: an estimate of the typical size of a residual that outliers do not inflate, usually the median absolute residual divided by 0.6745 (so that it equals the standard deviation for normal errors). With \(\rho(u) = u^2/2\) this is OLS. Robust methods replace \(\rho\) by a function that grows more slowly. The derivative \(\psi = \rho'\) is the influence function: how hard a residual of size \(u\) pulls on the line. The weight is \(w(u) = \psi(u)/u\).

\(\rho(u)\) \(\psi(u)\) \(w(u)\)
Least squares \(u^2/2\) \(u\) \(1\)
Huber, \(k = 1.345\) \(u^2/2\) if \(\lvert u\rvert \le k\), else \(k\lvert u\rvert - k^2/2\) \(u\) if \(\lvert u\rvert \le k\), else \(k\,\mathrm{sign}(u)\) \(1\) if \(\lvert u\rvert \le k\), else \(k/\lvert u\rvert\)
Tukey’s bisquare, \(c = 4.685\) \(\dfrac{c^2}{6}\left[1 - \left(1 - \dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^3\right]\) if \(\lvert u\rvert \le c\), else \(\dfrac{c^2}{6}\) \(u\left(1 - \dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^2\) if \(\lvert u\rvert \le c\), else \(0\) \(\left(1 - \dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^2\) if \(\lvert u\rvert \le c\), else \(0\)

The constants \(k = 1.345\) and \(c = 4.685\) are chosen so that, when the errors really are normal, the estimator is 95% as efficient as OLS: its standard errors are only about 2.5% larger. They are the defaults of rlm() and lmrob().

Code 
huber_rho <- function(u, k = 1.345) ifelse(abs(u) <= k, u^2 / 2, k * abs(u) - k^2 / 2)
huber_psi <- function(u, k = 1.345) pmax(-k, pmin(k, u))
huber_w   <- function(u, k = 1.345) ifelse(abs(u) <= k, 1, k / abs(u))
bisq_rho  <- function(u, c = 4.685) ifelse(abs(u) <= c, c^2 / 6 * (1 - (1 - (u / c)^2)^3), c^2 / 6)
bisq_psi  <- function(u, c = 4.685) ifelse(abs(u) <= c, u * (1 - (u / c)^2)^2, 0)
bisq_w    <- function(u, c = 4.685) ifelse(abs(u) <= c, (1 - (u / c)^2)^2, 0)

Read the three panels from top to bottom:

  • Loss. OLS (blue) squares the residual, so the loss climbs steeply. Huber’s loss turns into a straight line after \(k\): big residuals cost much less. The bisquare loss stops growing altogether after \(c\): beyond that, it does not matter how far away a point is.
  • Influence. OLS influence grows with the residual forever. Huber’s is bounded: no residual pulls harder than \(k\). Bisquare’s redescends: it rises, falls back, and is exactly zero beyond \(c\). Such a point has no say at all.
  • Weight. Every OLS residual has weight 1. Huber gives weight \(k/\lvert u\rvert\) beyond \(k\): smaller, but never zero. Bisquare gives zero weight to residuals beyond \(c = 4.685\) scaled units.

Step 4: how R finds the numbers, by iteratively reweighted least squares

No formula gives the minimum of \(\sum\rho(e_i/s)\) directly, but the weights turn it into a series of ordinary regressions (iteratively reweighted least squares, IRLS):

  1. Fit OLS and compute the residuals \(e_i\).
  2. Estimate the scale \(s\) from the residuals, and the scaled residuals \(u_i = e_i/s\).
  3. Give every point the weight \(w_i = w(u_i)\).
  4. Fit a weighted least squares line with those weights: minimise \(\sum w_i e_i^2\). (For weighted least squares, see the page on it.)
  5. Go back to step 2, until the coefficients stop changing.

A country that the line fits badly gets a small weight, so next time the line follows it less, its residual stays large and its weight small. The final weights show who was ignored. We will do the loop by hand in section 4.

Step 5: the limit of M-estimators, and the breakdown point

M-estimators bound the influence of a large residual, but do nothing about leverage: a point far out in \(x\) drags the line so that its own residual is small, and so it keeps a large weight. Worse, IRLS starts from OLS, which the bad point has already ruined.

The breakdown point measures this in plain words: the share of the data that can be replaced by anything, however absurd, before the estimate can be taken anywhere you like.

  • OLS: one point, so a breakdown point of \(1/n\), almost 0%.
  • Huber M-estimator: it resists bad \(y\) values but a bad \(x\) value can break it, so with leverage points it too has a breakdown point of about \(1/n\).
  • Estimators with the highest possible breakdown point, 50%: the least trimmed squares (LTS, Rousseeuw, 1984), which minimises the sum of the smallest half of the squared residuals, and S-estimators. They resist up to nearly half of the data being bad. They are far less efficient when the data are clean.
  • MM-estimators (Yohai, 1987) combine the two: an S-estimate gives a first fit and a robust scale \(s\) (that is the 50% breakdown part), then a bisquare M-step starting from it, tuned for 95% efficiency. This is why lmrob() is the usual first choice today (Maronna et al., 2019).

6. Do not just delete the outliers

When a point looks wrong, deleting it is tempting. It is rarely the best answer:

  1. Find out why it is unusual first. A typing error or a unit slip, like the one we made up, may be corrected. A measurement taken under a different condition may belong to another population. A genuine but rare case, such as a country in a war, is information, not noise.
  2. Deleting by a rule picked after looking is a researcher’s degree of freedom. If you delete the points that hurt your hypothesis, the answer is yours, not the data’s. If you delete, say which, why and by what rule, set before fitting.
  3. A 0/1 decision is blunt. A robust fit makes the decision smoothly: a country gets a weight between 0 and 1 by how far it is, with a rule that is fixed in advance (the tuning constant) and an automatic result.
  4. The outliers are often the interesting part. The countries the robust line cannot explain are the ones to study.

7. “Robust regression” is not “robust standard errors”

The two names sound alike and do different jobs:

Robust regression Robust (sandwich) standard errors
Changes the coefficients? yes, they are estimated differently no, they are the OLS ones
Changes the standard errors? yes, as a by-product yes: that is the whole point
Protects against outliers and bad leverage points unequal spread of the residuals (heteroscedasticity)
R rlm(), lmrob(), lqs() sandwich::vcovHC(), lmtest::coeftest()

The sandwich estimator of White (1980) keeps the OLS coefficients, and replaces the formula for their covariance \(s^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) by

\[\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = (X^\top X)^{-1}\left(\sum_i e_i^2 \,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\right)(X^\top X)^{-1}\]

in which each residual counts with its own size: no common variance is assumed. The HC3 version (MacKinnon & White, 1985) also divides by \((1 - h_i)^2\) and works better in small samples (Long & Ervin, 2000).

8. Other things to keep in mind

  • Median regression is another outlier-resistant method. It minimises \(\sum \lvert e_i\rvert\) (the weight of a residual is \(1/\lvert e_i\rvert\)), so a far residual pulls linearly, not quadratically. It resists outliers in \(y\) but, like Huber, not leverage points, and it can describe other quantiles, not just the centre. See quantile regression.
  • Heavy tails, not only outliers. If the errors follow a distribution with long tails, such as the t, the best estimator is robust by nature. Robust regression is more than a cleaning tool.
  • Robust methods do not fix a wrong model. If the relationship is curved, down-weighting does not straighten it. Here the log of spending did that part; see polynomial regression for curves.
  • Standard errors. rlm() and lmrob() give asymptotic standard errors; with small samples they are optimistic, and a bootstrap is a good check.
  • Tuning constants trade resistance against efficiency. Smaller \(k\) or \(c\) down-weights more, and costs more efficiency when the data are clean. The defaults are a good place to stay.
  • A robust fit is not a verdict. It tells which points the model cannot explain. Whether to report, drop or model them is for the researcher, and should be stated.
  • Compare with OLS, always. If OLS and the robust fit agree, as in section 4, outliers are not an issue. If they disagree, find out why: the difference is itself the finding.

## The question: do a few odd countries decide the answer?

Countries that spend more on health have longer-living people. But how much longer, for each extra dollar? To answer, we draw a line through about 190 countries and read its slope.

Some countries do not follow the pattern. The Central African Republic spends little and its people live much shorter than even that low spending predicts. A few small island states spend a lot and do not live longer. **Ordinary least squares (OLS)** gives every country the same say, and lets such countries drag the line towards them.

This page asks: **how much does one tenfold step in health spending buy in life expectancy, when we do not let a handful of odd countries decide?** The answer is **robust regression**: estimation that resists outliers.

| M-estimation (`rlm()`) | yes | no | high (about 95%) |
| MM-estimation (`lmrob()`) | yes | yes | high (about 95%) |
| LTS (`lqs()`) | yes | yes | low |

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: why least squares is fragile

OLS chooses the line that makes the sum of **squared** residuals $\sum_i e_i^2$ as small as possible, where $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$. Squaring is the problem: a country that is 20 years off the line costs $400$, a hundred times as much as one that is 2 years off. To save that cost, the line moves towards it. **One bad point can move the OLS line as far as it likes.** To see this, we take one country with a high spending, Switzerland, and slide its life expectancy from 0 to 120 years. At each value we refit the line:

### Step 2: three kinds of unusual point

"Outlier" is used for three different things, with three different checks. All three come from the OLS fit, with $p = 2$ parameters and $n$ points:

| Kind | What it is | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Outlier in $y$** | far from the line, up or down, at a usual $x$ | **studentized residual** $t_i$: the residual divided by its standard error, with the point left out of the fit. Beyond $\pm 2$ or $\pm 3$ is unusual |
| **High-leverage point** | far from the other points in $x$, wherever its $y$ is | **hat value** $h_i$, the diagonal of $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. The average is $p/n$; above $2p/n$ is high |
| **Influential point** | removing it changes the fit a lot | **Cook's distance** $D_i = \dfrac{e_i^2}{p\,s^2}\dfrac{h_i}{(1 - h_i)^2}$: large when both the residual and the leverage are large. A common flag is $4/n$ |

A high-leverage point that sits **on** the trend is a *good* leverage point: it makes the line more precise. One that sits **off** the trend is a *bad* leverage point, and is the most dangerous kind: it has the leverage to pull the line, and the residual to pull it the wrong way. Because the line bends towards it, its own residual in OLS is small, and the point hides itself.

### Step 3: M-estimation, the idea

Instead of squares, **M-estimation** (the "M" is for maximum likelihood type; Huber, 1964) minimises a **loss function** $\rho$ of the scaled residuals:

$$\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \rho\!\left(\frac{e_i}{s}\right), \qquad e_i = y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top\beta$$

$s$ is a **robust scale**: an estimate of the typical size of a residual that outliers do not inflate, usually the median absolute residual divided by 0.6745 (so that it equals the standard deviation for normal errors). With $\rho(u) = u^2/2$ this is OLS. Robust methods replace $\rho$ by a function that grows more slowly. The derivative $\psi = \rho'$ is the **influence function**: how hard a residual of size $u$ pulls on the line. The **weight** is $w(u) = \psi(u)/u$.

| | $\rho(u)$ | $\psi(u)$ | $w(u)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Least squares** | $u^2/2$ | $u$ | $1$ |
| **Huber**, $k = 1.345$ | $u^2/2$ if $\lvert u\rvert \le k$, else $k\lvert u\rvert - k^2/2$ | $u$ if $\lvert u\rvert \le k$, else $k\,\mathrm{sign}(u)$ | $1$ if $\lvert u\rvert \le k$, else $k/\lvert u\rvert$ |
| **Tukey's bisquare**, $c = 4.685$ | $\dfrac{c^2}{6}\left[1 - \left(1 - \dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^3\right]$ if $\lvert u\rvert \le c$, else $\dfrac{c^2}{6}$ | $u\left(1 - \dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^2$ if $\lvert u\rvert \le c$, else $0$ | $\left(1 - \dfrac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^2$ if $\lvert u\rvert \le c$, else $0$ |

The constants $k = 1.345$ and $c = 4.685$ are chosen so that, when the errors really are normal, the estimator is **95% as efficient** as OLS: its standard errors are only about 2.5% larger. They are the defaults of `rlm()` and `lmrob()`.

```{r rho_psi}
huber_rho <- function(u, k = 1.345) ifelse(abs(u) <= k, u^2 / 2, k * abs(u) - k^2 / 2)
huber_psi <- function(u, k = 1.345) pmax(-k, pmin(k, u))
huber_w   <- function(u, k = 1.345) ifelse(abs(u) <= k, 1, k / abs(u))
bisq_rho  <- function(u, c = 4.685) ifelse(abs(u) <= c, c^2 / 6 * (1 - (1 - (u / c)^2)^3), c^2 / 6)
bisq_psi  <- function(u, c = 4.685) ifelse(abs(u) <= c, u * (1 - (u / c)^2)^2, 0)
bisq_w    <- function(u, c = 4.685) ifelse(abs(u) <= c, (1 - (u / c)^2)^2, 0)

# the formulas above agree with the ones inside MASS
u <- seq(-8, 8, by = 0.1)
c(huber = max(abs(huber_w(u) - psi.huber(u))), bisquare = max(abs(bisq_w(u) - psi.bisquare(u))))
```

Read the three panels from top to bottom:

- **Loss.** OLS (blue) squares the residual, so the loss climbs steeply. Huber's loss turns into a straight line after $k$: big residuals cost much less. The bisquare loss stops growing altogether after $c$: beyond that, it does not matter how far away a point is.
- **Influence.** OLS influence grows with the residual forever. Huber's is **bounded**: no residual pulls harder than $k$. Bisquare's **redescends**: it rises, falls back, and is exactly zero beyond $c$. Such a point has no say at all.
- **Weight.** Every OLS residual has weight 1. Huber gives weight $k/\lvert u\rvert$ beyond $k$: smaller, but never zero. Bisquare gives zero weight to residuals beyond $c = 4.685$ scaled units.

### Step 4: how R finds the numbers, by iteratively reweighted least squares

No formula gives the minimum of $\sum\rho(e_i/s)$ directly, but the weights turn it into a series of ordinary regressions (**iteratively reweighted least squares**, IRLS):

1. Fit OLS and compute the residuals $e_i$.
2. Estimate the scale $s$ from the residuals, and the scaled residuals $u_i = e_i/s$.
3. Give every point the weight $w_i = w(u_i)$.
4. Fit a **weighted** least squares line with those weights: minimise $\sum w_i e_i^2$. (For weighted least squares, see [the page on it](EXAMPLE_WEIGHTED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd).)
5. Go back to step 2, until the coefficients stop changing.

A country that the line fits badly gets a small weight, so next time the line follows it less, its residual stays large and its weight small. The final **weights** show who was ignored. We will do the loop by hand in section 4.

### Step 5: the limit of M-estimators, and the breakdown point

M-estimators bound the influence of a large *residual*, but do nothing about *leverage*: a point far out in $x$ drags the line so that its own residual is small, and so it keeps a large weight. Worse, IRLS starts from OLS, which the bad point has already ruined.

The **breakdown point** measures this in plain words: *the share of the data that can be replaced by anything, however absurd, before the estimate can be taken anywhere you like*.

- OLS: one point, so a breakdown point of $1/n$, almost 0%.
- Huber M-estimator: it resists bad $y$ values but a bad $x$ value can break it, so with leverage points it too has a breakdown point of about $1/n$.
- Estimators with the **highest possible breakdown point, 50%**: the **least trimmed squares** (LTS, Rousseeuw, 1984), which minimises the sum of the smallest half of the squared residuals, and **S-estimators**. They resist up to nearly half of the data being bad. They are far less efficient when the data are clean.
- **MM-estimators** (Yohai, 1987) combine the two: an S-estimate gives a first fit and a robust scale $s$ (that is the 50% breakdown part), then a bisquare M-step starting from it, tuned for 95% efficiency. This is why `lmrob()` is the usual first choice today (Maronna et al., 2019).

### The same numbers by hand: IRLS in six lines

```{r irls}
X <- model.matrix(~ logspend, d)
y <- d$life
b <- coef(ols)                                     # step 1: start from OLS
for (i in 1:50) {
  e  <- y - X %*% b
  s  <- median(abs(e)) / 0.6745                    # step 2: robust scale
  w  <- huber_w(e / s)                             # step 3: Huber weights
  b_new <- solve(t(X) %*% (c(w) * X), t(X) %*% (c(w) * y))   # step 4: weighted least squares
  if (max(abs(b_new - b)) < 1e-8) break
  b <- b_new
}
rbind(by_hand = c(b), rlm = coef(huber))
```

## 6. Do not just delete the outliers

When a point looks wrong, deleting it is tempting. It is rarely the best answer:

1. **Find out why it is unusual first.** A typing error or a unit slip, like the one we made up, may be corrected. A measurement taken under a different condition may belong to another population. A genuine but rare case, such as a country in a war, is information, not noise.
2. **Deleting by a rule picked after looking** is a researcher's degree of freedom. If you delete the points that hurt your hypothesis, the answer is yours, not the data's. If you delete, say which, why and by what rule, set before fitting.
3. **A 0/1 decision is blunt.** A robust fit makes the decision smoothly: a country gets a weight between 0 and 1 by how far it is, with a rule that is fixed in advance (the tuning constant) and an automatic result.
4. **The outliers are often the interesting part.** The countries the robust line cannot explain are the ones to study.

Compare, for these data, the OLS line after deleting the flagged countries with the robust fit:


Deleting the `r n_dropped` flagged countries (Cook's distance above `r nz(flag_cook, 3)`, hat value above `r nz(flag_hat, 3)` or studentized residual beyond ±2.5) moves the OLS slope from `r f2(coef(ols)[[2]])` to `r f2(coef(ols_drop)[[2]])`, which is close to the robust values `r f2(coef(bisquare)[[2]])` (bisquare) and `r f2(coef(mm)[[2]])` (MM). The robust methods get there without a human choosing who goes, and keep every country in the sample (with a small weight if need be).

## 7. "Robust regression" is not "robust standard errors"

The two names sound alike and do different jobs:

| | **Robust regression** | **Robust (sandwich) standard errors** |
|---|---|---|
| Changes the coefficients? | **yes**, they are estimated differently | **no**, they are the OLS ones |
| Changes the standard errors? | yes, as a by-product | **yes**: that is the whole point |
| Protects against | outliers and bad leverage points | unequal spread of the residuals (**heteroscedasticity**) |
| R | `rlm()`, `lmrob()`, `lqs()` | `sandwich::vcovHC()`, `lmtest::coeftest()` |

The sandwich estimator of White (1980) keeps the OLS coefficients, and replaces the formula for their covariance $s^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ by

$$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = (X^\top X)^{-1}\left(\sum_i e_i^2 \,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\right)(X^\top X)^{-1}$$

in which each residual counts with its own size: no common variance is assumed. The **HC3** version (MacKinnon & White, 1985) also divides by $(1 - h_i)^2$ and works better in small samples (Long & Ervin, 2000).

```{r sandwich}
bp <- bptest(ols)
coeftest(ols)                                    # the usual OLS standard errors
coeftest(ols, vcov = vcovHC(ols, type = "HC3"))  # the same coefficients, sandwich standard errors
se_hc3 <- sqrt(diag(vcovHC(ols, type = "HC3")))
```

The Breusch-Pagan test of constant variance gives $\chi^2$(`r bp$parameter`) = `r f2(bp$statistic)`, *p* `r pval(bp$p.value)`. The slope is the same, `r f2(coef(ols)[[2]])`, with a standard error of `r f2(s_ols$coefficients[2, 2])` in the usual way and `r f2(se_hc3[[2]])` with HC3. This changes the **uncertainty**, and the line stays where the outliers left it. Now the damaged data:

```{r sandwich_bad}
ols_bad <- lm(life ~ logspend, data = d_bad)
se_bad  <- sqrt(diag(vcovHC(ols_bad, type = "HC3")))
ci_bad  <- coef(ols_bad)[[2]] + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(.975) * se_bad[[2]]
ci_mm   <- coef(bad$fits$MM)[[2]] + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(.975) * bd["MM", "se"]
```

OLS now gives a slope of `r f2(coef(ols_bad)[[2]])` with an HC3 standard error of `r f2(se_bad[[2]])`: a 95% interval of [`r f2(ci_bad[1])`, `r f2(ci_bad[2])`]. The interval is "honest" about the spread, but it is centred on the **wrong slope**, and it misses the value MM finds, `r f2(coef(bad$fits$MM)[[2]])` [`r f2(ci_mm[1])`, `r f2(ci_mm[2])`]. Sandwich standard errors answer "how uncertain is this estimate?"; they do not ask whether the estimate is any good.

The two can be combined: `lmrob()` already reports standard errors that are robust to outliers, and a sandwich estimator can be put on top of a weighted fit. For heteroscedasticity with a known pattern, [weighted](EXAMPLE_WEIGHTED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd) or [generalized](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd) least squares estimate the variance model directly.

## 8. Other things to keep in mind

- **Median regression is another outlier-resistant method.** It minimises $\sum \lvert e_i\rvert$ (the weight of a residual is $1/\lvert e_i\rvert$), so a far residual pulls linearly, not quadratically. It resists outliers in $y$ but, like Huber, not leverage points, and it can describe other quantiles, not just the centre. See [quantile regression](EXAMPLE_QUANTILE_REGRESSION.Rmd).
- **Heavy tails, not only outliers.** If the errors follow a distribution with long tails, such as the *t*, the best estimator is robust by nature. Robust regression is more than a cleaning tool.
- **Robust methods do not fix a wrong model.** If the relationship is curved, down-weighting does not straighten it. Here the log of spending did that part; see [polynomial regression](EXAMPLE_POLYNOMIAL_REGRESSION.Rmd) for curves.
- **Standard errors.** `rlm()` and `lmrob()` give asymptotic standard errors; with small samples they are optimistic, and a bootstrap is a good check.
- **Tuning constants** trade resistance against efficiency. Smaller $k$ or $c$ down-weights more, and costs more efficiency when the data are clean. The defaults are a good place to stay.
- **A robust fit is not a verdict.** It tells which points the model cannot explain. Whether to report, drop or model them is for the researcher, and should be stated.
- **Compare with OLS**, always. If OLS and the robust fit agree, as in section 4, outliers are not an issue. If they disagree, find out why: the difference is itself the finding.

## 10. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | The data have outliers, heavy tails, or points that OLS cannot be trusted with |
| **Idea** | Minimise $\sum\rho(e_i/s)$ with a loss $\rho$ that grows more slowly than the square; computed by iteratively reweighted least squares |
| **Huber** | Bounded influence; weights $\min(1, k/\lvert u\rvert)$, never zero. Resists outliers in $y$, not bad leverage |
| **Bisquare** | Redescending: weights drop to zero beyond $c$. Resists large outliers, but needs a good start |
| **MM** | An S-estimate (breakdown 50%) as a start, then a bisquare step (95% efficient). The usual first choice |
| **LTS** | Fits the best half of the data: breakdown 50%, no standard errors, less efficient |
| **How (R)** | `MASS::rlm(y ~ x, psi = psi.huber)` or `psi.bisquare`; `robustbase::lmrob(y ~ x)`; `MASS::lqs(y ~ x, method = "lts")` |
| **Diagnostics** | `hatvalues()`, `rstudent()`, `cooks.distance()`; the final weights `fit$w` (`rlm`) or `fit$rweights` (`lmrob`) |
| **Not the same as** | Sandwich standard errors: same coefficients, only the uncertainty changes |
| **Dangers** | Deleting points silently; hiding a wrong model behind a robust fit |

Deming Regression: Do the Blood Pressure Machine and the Stethoscope Agree?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Deming (errors-in-variables) regression in R, by hand and with the deming package, on 4,212 US adults whose blood pressure was measured both by a physician with a stethoscope and by a machine (NHANES 2017-2018): attenuation, why two ordinary regressions contradict each other, the error variance ratio from repeated readings, jackknife standard errors, testing slope 1 and intercept 0, sensitivity to the ratio, Bland-Altman limits of agreement and APA reporting.

The question: can the machine replace the physician?

For decades the US health survey NHANES measured blood pressure with a mercury column and a stethoscope. Mercury is poisonous, so the survey wanted to change to an automatic machine. But if the machine reads differently, every trend in the nation’s blood pressure would jump on the day of the change. So in 2017–2018 the survey did both: each person was measured by a physician with the stethoscope and by a technician with the machine, in random order (Ostchega et al., 2021).

This page asks the question of every comparison of two instruments:

  1. Does the machine read higher or lower by a constant amount? (constant bias)
  2. Does it read higher or lower in proportion, for example more wrong for high blood pressures? (proportional bias)

The natural idea is a regression of the machine on the stethoscope, and a test of whether the slope is 1 and the intercept 0. It gives a wrong answer. Ordinary regression assumes that \(x\) is measured without error, and here both instruments err. Deming regression is the regression for two variables that are both measured with error.

dem_slope <- function(x, y, lambda) {        # the formula is explained in section 3
  sxx <- var(x); syy <- var(y); sxy <- cov(x, y)
  b1 <- (syy - lambda * sxx + sqrt((syy - lambda * sxx)^2 + 4 * lambda * sxy^2)) / (2 * sxy)
  c(b0 = mean(y) - b1 * mean(x), b1 = b1)
}

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: two noisy readings of two hidden values

Each person has a true value that nobody sees. The two instruments read it with errors:

\[x_i = \xi_i + \delta_i, \qquad y_i = \eta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \xi_i\]

\(\xi_i\) (xi) is what the stethoscope would show without error, \(\eta_i\) (eta) what the machine would. The errors \(\delta_i\) and \(\varepsilon_i\) are independent of everything, with variances \(\sigma_\delta^2\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). The instruments agree perfectly, apart from the errors, if \(\beta_0 = 0\) and \(\beta_1 = 1\).

Step 2: why ordinary regression gives a slope that is too small

Ordinary regression uses \(x\), not \(\xi\). The covariance of \(x\) with \(y\) is not affected by the errors, because they are independent, but the variance of \(x\) is larger than that of \(\xi\). So the OLS slope tends to

\[b_{OLS} \longrightarrow \beta_1 \cdot \frac{\sigma_\xi^2}{\sigma_\xi^2 + \sigma_\delta^2}\]

The fraction is the reliability of \(x\). It is below 1, so the slope is too small: attenuation. More data do not help, because it is not a problem of sampling. And it has the same form as the regression to the mean of Galton’s heights.

Step 3: which distance should be small?

OLS assumes that all the error is in \(y\), so it minimises the vertical distances. If both are wrong, a point should be allowed to move in both directions to reach the line, and the more precise variable should move less. Deming regression minimises

\[\sum_i \left[ \frac{(x_i - \hat\xi_i)^2}{\sigma_\delta^2} + \frac{(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 \hat\xi_i)^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2} \right]\]

over the line and over the hidden \(\hat\xi_i\). Only the ratio of the two variances matters for the line:

\[\lambda = \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_\delta^2} = \frac{\text{error variance of } y}{\text{error variance of } x}\]

Step 4: the formula

With the sample variances \(s_{xx}\), \(s_{yy}\) and the covariance \(s_{xy}\), the minimum has a closed form:

\[b_1 = \frac{s_{yy} - \lambda s_{xx} + \sqrt{(s_{yy} - \lambda s_{xx})^2 + 4 \lambda s_{xy}^2}}{2 s_{xy}}, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x\]

Three special cases show what \(\lambda\) does:

\(\lambda\) Means The line becomes
\(\lambda \to \infty\) no error in \(x\) OLS of \(y\) on \(x\)
\(\lambda = 1\) equally large errors orthogonal regression, perpendicular distances
\(\lambda \to 0\) no error in \(y\) OLS of \(x\) on \(y\), turned around
Code

                        b0        b1

So the two contradicting answers of section 2 are the two extremes, and the truth lies between them. Where, depends on \(\lambda\).

Step 5: \(\lambda\) must come from somewhere else

The cloud of points alone cannot say which instrument is noisier: a wide cloud can come from either. We need outside information. The best source are repeated readings with each instrument: they differ only by error. For person \(i\) with readings \(x_{i1}, x_{i2}, x_{i3}\), the variance of the three around their own mean estimates the error variance, and we average it over the people:

\[\hat\sigma_\delta^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \text{var}(x_{i1}, x_{i2}, x_{i3})\]

This is why NHANES took three readings with each.

Step 6: the standard error

There is no simple formula that is exact, so we use the jackknife: leave out one person at a time, compute the slope again, and see how much it moves. With \(b_{(-i)}\) the slope without person \(i\), the pseudo-values are \(b_i^* = n\,b - (n - 1)\,b_{(-i)}\), and the standard error is their standard deviation divided by \(\sqrt n\).

5. Two proofs that OLS is the one that is wrong

With the data: make \(x\) noisier on purpose

We have three readings. A single reading is noisier than the mean of three, so if attenuation is real, the OLS slope must fall when we use one reading only, although the two instruments are still the same:

Code 
one_x <- bp$BPXSY1
one_y <- bp$BPXOSY1
proof <- rbind(
  `mean of 3 readings` = c(OLS = coef(lm(y ~ x))[[2]], Deming = dem_slope(x, y, lambda)[["b1"]]),
  `first reading only` = c(
    OLS = coef(lm(one_y ~ one_x))[[2]],
    Deming = dem_slope(one_x, one_y, lambda)[["b1"]]
  )
)
round(proof, 3)
                     OLS Deming
mean of 3 readings 0.872  0.985
first reading only 0.853  0.985

OLS falls from 0.872 to 0.853. Deming stays at 0.985 and 0.985.

6. The weak point: is \(\lambda\) right?

Everything depends on \(\lambda\), and the Scale of 3.0 warned us. If the error variances of the repeated readings were the whole story, the difference between the two instruments would vary by their sum:

Code 
difference <- y - x
expected   <- var_steth / 3 + var_machine / 3       # both are means of three readings
c(observed_variance = var(difference), expected_from_repeated_readings = expected,
  ratio = var(difference) / expected)
              observed_variance expected_from_repeated_readings                           ratio 
                      99.610126                       11.255144                        8.850187

The differences vary 8.9 times as much as the repeated readings say. The three readings of an instrument were taken in a row, by the same examiner, with the same cuff. They share everything that changes from one occasion to the next: the mood, the position, the cuff, how the physician hears. These errors are in each instrument’s mean, and the repeats cannot show them. So the repeated readings underestimate the errors, and we cannot know whether they underestimate both in the same proportion. What we can do is to ask how much it matters:

The exact value, and whether it is “significantly” different from 1, does depend on \(\lambda\): the confidence interval of section 4 takes it as known, and it is too narrow for that reason. The OLS slope of 0.87 would be right only if the stethoscope made no errors at all, which nobody believes.

The lesson for the design of a comparison: repeat the measurement on different occasions, by different examiners, so that the repeats contain all the errors.

8. Things to keep in mind

  • Use Deming when neither variable is the outcome and both measure the same thing with error. If you want to predict one reading from the other, OLS is right: the best prediction of \(y\) from a noisy \(x\) does regress towards the mean.
  • \(\lambda\) is an assumption unless it comes from repeats that contain all the sources of error. Report it, and show how the slope changes with it. A wrong \(\lambda\) also makes the tests wrong (Linnet, 1998).
  • Errors that grow with the level. Deming regression assumes the same error variance everywhere. Here the repeats of both instruments vary more at higher pressures. The weighted version lets the error SD of each instrument grow in proportion to the level, that is, it assumes a constant coefficient of variation (Linnet, 1990). The argument stdpat gives the pattern: a constant and a multiplier for \(x\), then for \(y\):

  • Test the pair (intercept, slope) against (0, 1) together if you need one test, or better, report the expected difference at values that matter, as we did for 180 mmHg.
  • With several predictors measured with error, use a structural equation model with latent variables, or multiple indicators (see the factor models of measurement invariance).
  • Survey weights. We compared instruments within persons, and did not estimate the blood pressure of the US population, for which NHANES needs its weights.

## The question: can the machine replace the physician?

For decades the US health survey NHANES measured blood pressure with a mercury column and a stethoscope. Mercury is poisonous, so the survey wanted to change to an automatic machine. But if the machine reads differently, every trend in the nation's blood pressure would jump on the day of the change. So in 2017–2018 the survey did both: **each person was measured by a physician with the stethoscope and by a technician with the machine**, in random order (Ostchega et al., 2021).

This page asks the question of every comparison of two instruments:

1. Does the machine read **higher or lower by a constant amount**? (constant bias)
2. Does it read higher or lower **in proportion**, for example more wrong for high blood pressures? (proportional bias)

The natural idea is a regression of the machine on the stethoscope, and a test of whether the slope is 1 and the intercept 0. It gives a **wrong answer**. Ordinary regression assumes that $x$ is measured without error, and here both instruments err. **Deming regression** is the regression for two variables that are both measured with error.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: two noisy readings of two hidden values

Each person has a true value that nobody sees. The two instruments read it with errors:

$$x_i = \xi_i + \delta_i, \qquad y_i = \eta_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \xi_i$$

$\xi_i$ (xi) is what the stethoscope would show without error, $\eta_i$ (eta) what the machine would. The errors $\delta_i$ and $\varepsilon_i$ are independent of everything, with variances $\sigma_\delta^2$ and $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$. The instruments agree perfectly, apart from the errors, if $\beta_0 = 0$ and $\beta_1 = 1$.

### Step 2: why ordinary regression gives a slope that is too small

Ordinary regression uses $x$, not $\xi$. The covariance of $x$ with $y$ is not affected by the errors, because they are independent, but the **variance of $x$** is larger than that of $\xi$. So the OLS slope tends to

$$b_{OLS} \longrightarrow \beta_1 \cdot \frac{\sigma_\xi^2}{\sigma_\xi^2 + \sigma_\delta^2}$$

The fraction is the **reliability** of $x$. It is below 1, so the slope is too small: **attenuation**. More data do not help, because it is not a problem of sampling. And it has the same form as the [regression to the mean](EXAMPLE_SIMPLE_LINEAR_REGRESSION.Rmd) of Galton's heights.

### Step 3: which distance should be small?

OLS assumes that all the error is in $y$, so it minimises the vertical distances. If both are wrong, a point should be allowed to move in both directions to reach the line, and the more precise variable should move less. Deming regression minimises

$$\sum_i \left[ \frac{(x_i - \hat\xi_i)^2}{\sigma_\delta^2} + \frac{(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 \hat\xi_i)^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2} \right]$$

over the line **and** over the hidden $\hat\xi_i$. Only the ratio of the two variances matters for the line:

$$\lambda = \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_\delta^2} = \frac{\text{error variance of } y}{\text{error variance of } x}$$

### Step 4: the formula

With the sample variances $s_{xx}$, $s_{yy}$ and the covariance $s_{xy}$, the minimum has a closed form:

$$b_1 = \frac{s_{yy} - \lambda s_{xx} + \sqrt{(s_{yy} - \lambda s_{xx})^2 + 4 \lambda s_{xy}^2}}{2 s_{xy}}, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x$$

Three special cases show what $\lambda$ does:

| $\lambda$ | Means | The line becomes |
|---|---|---|
| $\lambda \to \infty$ | no error in $x$ | OLS of $y$ on $x$ |
| $\lambda = 1$ | equally large errors | **orthogonal** regression, perpendicular distances |
| $\lambda \to 0$ | no error in $y$ | OLS of $x$ on $y$, turned around |

```{r special_cases}
rbind(`lambda = 10000`  = dem_slope(x, y, 10000),
      `lambda = 1`      = dem_slope(x, y, 1),
      `lambda = 0.0001` = dem_slope(x, y, 0.0001))
```

So the two contradicting answers of section 2 are the two extremes, and the truth lies **between** them. Where, depends on $\lambda$.

### Step 5: $\lambda$ must come from somewhere else

The cloud of points alone cannot say which instrument is noisier: a wide cloud can come from either. We need outside information. The best source are **repeated readings** with each instrument: they differ only by error. For person $i$ with readings $x_{i1}, x_{i2}, x_{i3}$, the variance of the three around their own mean estimates the error variance, and we average it over the people:

$$\hat\sigma_\delta^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \text{var}(x_{i1}, x_{i2}, x_{i3})$$

This is why NHANES took three readings with each.

### Step 6: the standard error

There is no simple formula that is exact, so we use the **jackknife**: leave out one person at a time, compute the slope again, and see how much it moves. With $b_{(-i)}$ the slope without person $i$, the pseudo-values are $b_i^* = n\,b - (n - 1)\,b_{(-i)}$, and the standard error is their standard deviation divided by $\sqrt n$.

## 5. Two proofs that OLS is the one that is wrong

```{r simulation_chart}
       subtitle = "Dashed line: the true slope", x = "estimated slope", y = "density") +
  theme_bw() + theme(legend.position = "bottom", legend.direction = "vertical")
```

OLS averages `r f2(mean(sim[, "OLS"]))`, which is the reliability, as step 2 promised. Deming averages `r f2(mean(sim[, "Deming"]))`. The narrowness of the OLS curve is worth a thought: it is a **precise** estimate of the wrong number.

## 6. The weak point: is $\lambda$ right?

Everything depends on $\lambda$, and the `Scale` of `r f1(fit$sigma)` warned us. If the error variances of the repeated readings were the whole story, the difference between the two instruments would vary by their sum:

```{r difference_variance}
difference <- y - x
expected   <- var_steth / 3 + var_machine / 3       # both are means of three readings
c(observed_variance = var(difference), expected_from_repeated_readings = expected,
  ratio = var(difference) / expected)
```

The differences vary `r f1(var(difference) / expected)` times as much as the repeated readings say. The three readings of an instrument were taken **in a row, by the same examiner, with the same cuff**. They share everything that changes from one occasion to the next: the mood, the position, the cuff, how the physician hears. These errors are in each instrument's mean, and the repeats cannot show them. So the repeated readings underestimate the errors, and we cannot know whether they underestimate both **in the same proportion**. What we can do is to ask how much it matters:

```{r sensitivity}
ratios <- c(0.25, 0.5, 1, lambda, 2, 4)
sens <- t(sapply(ratios, function(l) dem_slope(x, y, l)))
data.frame(lambda = round(ratios, 2), intercept = round(sens[, 1], 2), slope = round(sens[, 2], 3))
```

```{r sensitivity_chart}
grid <- exp(seq(log(1/20), log(20), length.out = 200))
path <- data.frame(lambda = grid, slope = sapply(grid, function(l) dem_slope(x, y, l)[["b1"]]))

ggplot(path, aes(lambda, slope)) +
  annotate("rect", xmin = 0.5, xmax = 2, ymin = -Inf, ymax = Inf, fill = "grey85", alpha = 0.6) +

  geom_hline(
    yintercept = c(slopes[[1]], rev_line[["slope"]]),
    colour = c("#eb6834", "#eda100"),
    linewidth = 0.8
  ) +
  geom_line(colour = "#2a78d6", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_point(data = data.frame(lambda = lambda, slope = b[["b1"]]), size = 3, colour = "#2a78d6") +
  scale_x_log10(breaks = c(0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 10)) +
  labs(
    title = "The Deming slope for every possible λ",
    subtitle = "Horizontal lines: the two OLS slopes. Grey band: one error variance up to twice the other",
    x = "λ = error variance of the machine / error variance of the stethoscope (log scale)",

  theme_bw()
```

As long as neither error variance is more than twice the other, the slope is between `r f2(dem_slope(x, y, 2)[["b1"]])` and `r f2(dem_slope(x, y, 0.5)[["b1"]])`, close to 1. The exact value, and whether it is "significantly" different from 1, does depend on $\lambda$: the confidence interval of section 4 takes it as known, and it is too narrow for that reason. The OLS slope of `r f2(slopes[[1]])` would be right only if the stethoscope made **no** errors at all, which nobody believes.

The lesson for the design of a comparison: repeat the measurement on **different occasions**, by different examiners, so that the repeats contain all the errors.

## 7. Agreement is more than a line

Deming regression compares the instruments **on average**. It says nothing about one person. For that, the usual tool is the plot of Bland and Altman (1986): the difference against the mean of the two.

```{r bland_altman}
middle <- (x + y) / 2
limits <- mean(difference) + c(-1.96, 1.96) * sd(difference)
trend  <- lm(difference ~ middle)
c(bias = mean(difference), lower = limits[1], upper = limits[2])
round(coef(summary(trend)), 3)
```

```{r bland_altman_chart}
ggplot(data.frame(middle, difference), aes(middle, difference)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.12, size = 1, colour = "grey30") +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "grey60") +
  geom_hline(yintercept = mean(difference), colour = "#2a78d6", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = limits, colour = "#eb6834", linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 0.8) +
  labs(
    title = "Machine minus stethoscope, against the mean of the two",
    subtitle = "Blue: mean difference. Orange: limits of agreement, which hold 95% of the differences",
    x = "mean of the two (mmHg)",
    y = "difference (mmHg)"
  ) +
  theme_bw()

crosses <- mean((x >= 130) != (y >= 130))
```

- The mean difference is `r f1(mean(difference))` mmHg. The differences do not depend clearly on the level (slope `r f3(coef(trend)[[2]])`, *p* `r pval(coef(summary(trend))[2, 4])`), which agrees with a Deming slope near 1.
- The **limits of agreement** run from `r f1(limits[1])` to `r f1(limits[2])` mmHg. For one person the two methods can differ a lot: for `r f1(100 * crosses)`% of the adults, one method says 130 mmHg or more and the other says less. 130 is the line that current US guidelines use for high blood pressure.

So for averages of thousands of people the machine can replace the stethoscope, with a correction of about one mmHg. For one visit of one patient neither instrument is precise. Both statements are needed, and they answer different questions.

## 8. Things to keep in mind

- **Use Deming when neither variable is the outcome** and both measure the same thing with error. If you want to **predict** one reading from the other, OLS is right: the best prediction of $y$ from a noisy $x$ does regress towards the mean.
- **$\lambda$ is an assumption** unless it comes from repeats that contain all the sources of error. Report it, and show how the slope changes with it. A wrong $\lambda$ also makes the tests wrong (Linnet, 1998).
- **Errors that grow with the level.** Deming regression assumes the same error variance everywhere. Here the repeats of both instruments vary more at higher pressures. The **weighted** version lets the error SD of each instrument grow in proportion to the level, that is, it assumes a constant coefficient of variation (Linnet, 1990). The argument `stdpat` gives the pattern: a constant and a multiplier for $x$, then for $y$:

```{r weighted}
level_bands <- cut(middle, quantile(middle, 0:4 / 4), include.lowest = TRUE)
spread_by_level <- data.frame(stethoscope = tapply(apply(X, 1, var), level_bands, mean),
                              machine     = tapply(apply(Y, 1, var), level_bands, mean))
round(sqrt(spread_by_level), 1)           # error SD of one reading, by quarter of blood pressure

weighted_fit <- deming(
  machine ~ steth,
  data = bp,
  stdpat = c(0, sqrt(var_steth / 3) / mean(x), 0, sqrt(var_machine / 3) / mean(y))
)
coef(weighted_fit)
```

- The weighted slope is `r f3(coef(weighted_fit)[[2]])`, against `r f3(b[["b1"]])`. The change, `r f3(abs(coef(weighted_fit)[[2]] - b[["b1"]]))`, is about `r f1(abs(coef(weighted_fit)[[2]] - b[["b1"]]) / se[["b1"]])` times the standard error: the choice of the error model is part of the uncertainty, and the confidence interval does not contain it.
- **Outliers** pull the Deming line as they pull OLS. `deming::pbreg()` (Passing–Bablok) and `deming::thielsen()` are versions based on ranks.
- **Test the pair** (intercept, slope) against (0, 1) together if you need one test, or better, report the expected difference at values that matter, as we did for 180 mmHg.
- **With several predictors** measured with error, use a structural equation model with latent variables, or multiple indicators (see the factor models of [measurement invariance](EXAMPLE_MEASUREMENT_INVARIANCE.rmd)).
- **Survey weights.** We compared instruments within persons, and did not estimate the blood pressure of the US population, for which NHANES needs its weights.

## 9. Reporting the results in APA style

A report (APA 7th edition) needs the two instruments, the number of readings, where $\lambda$ came from, the slope and intercept with their uncertainty, and the agreement for individuals. The numbers come from the R objects:

```{r apa_numbers}
apa_table <- data.frame(
  Method = c(
    "OLS, machine on stethoscope", "OLS, stethoscope on machine (turned around)",
    "Deming, λ from the repeated readings", "Deming, λ = 1"
  ),
  Intercept = f2(c(coef(ols)[[1]], rev_line[[1]], b[["b0"]], dem_slope(x, y, 1)[["b0"]])),
  Slope = f3(c(coef(ols)[[2]], rev_line[[2]], b[["b1"]], dem_slope(x, y, 1)[["b1"]])),
  At180 = f1(
    c(
      coef(ols)[[1]] + 180 * coef(ols)[[2]],
      rev_line[[1]] + 180 * rev_line[[2]],
      b[["b0"]] + 180 * b[["b1"]],
      sum(dem_slope(x, y, 1) * c(1, 180))
    ) -
      180
  )
)
```

**Method** (data analysis):

> We compared systolic blood pressure measured by a physician with a mercury sphygmomanometer and a stethoscope and by a technician with an oscillometric device, in random order, in `r num(nrow(bp))` adults of NHANES 2017–2018 with three readings by each method (Ostchega et al., 2021). The means of the three readings were compared with Deming regression, because both methods have measurement error. The ratio of the error variances (λ = `r f2(lambda)`, device to stethoscope) was estimated from the repeated readings; standard errors were obtained with the jackknife. Because the repeated readings were taken on one occasion, we also report the slope for λ between 0.5 and 2. Agreement for individuals is described by the limits of agreement (Bland & Altman, 1986). Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`) with the package deming (Therneau, 2024).

**Results:**

> The device read lower than the stethoscope on average (*M* = `r f1(mean(y))` against `r f1(mean(x))` mmHg), mean difference = `r f1(mean(difference))` mmHg, 95% limits of agreement [`r f1(limits[1])`, `r f1(limits[2])`]. The Deming slope was `r f3(b[["b1"]])`, 95% CI [`r f3(ci["b1", 1])`, `r f3(ci["b1", 2])`], and the intercept `r f2(b[["b0"]])` mmHg, 95% CI [`r f2(ci["b0", 1])`, `r f2(ci["b0", 2])`], which shows no clear proportional bias. For λ between 0.5 and 2 the slope was between `r f3(dem_slope(x, y, 2)[["b1"]])` and `r f3(dem_slope(x, y, 0.5)[["b1"]])`. Ordinary least squares, which ignores the error of the stethoscope, gave a slope of `r f3(coef(ols)[[2]])` (Table 1).

## 10. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | Two measurements of the same thing, both with error: instruments, laboratories, raters, self-report against measurement |
| **Why not OLS** | Error in $x$ flattens the slope (attenuation), and more data do not help |
| **The model** | $x = \xi + \delta$, $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \xi + \varepsilon$, with $\lambda = \sigma^2_\varepsilon / \sigma^2_\delta$ known |
| **How (R)** | `deming::deming(y ~ x, xstd = , ystd = )`; errors that grow with the level: `stdpat = c(0, cv_x, 0, cv_y)` |
| **λ** | From repeated measurements; $\lambda = 1$ is orthogonal regression |
| **Reading it** | Slope 1 and intercept 0 mean agreement on average; look at the expected difference at values that matter |
| **Standard errors** | Jackknife or bootstrap |
| **Also report** | Bland–Altman limits of agreement, for individuals |

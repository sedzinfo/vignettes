Multinomial Logistic Regression: How Did Federal Employees Leave in 2025?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Multinomial logistic regression in R with nnet::multinom(), on real OPM data: how about 330,000 US federal employees left their jobs in fiscal year 2025 (quit, retired, early retirement, deferred resignation, laid off, terminated), with relative risk ratios, predicted probabilities, the IIA assumption and APA reporting.

The question: six ways to leave

In 2025 the US federal government lost staff in several ways at once. In January, most federal employees were offered a deferred resignation: stop working, stay on the payroll until 30 September 2025, then resign. Agencies offered early retirement, carried out reductions in force (layoffs) and dismissed staff. And people kept quitting and retiring, as they do every year.

So everyone who left federal service in 2025 left in one of six ways. This page asks: among the people who left, what made one way of leaving more likely than another? Did the young quit and the old retire? Who took the deferred resignation? Who was laid off?

The outcome is a category with six values and no natural order: quitting is not “more” or “less” than being laid off. The type of outcome decides the type of regression:

Outcome Regression Example
Two categories logistic hired or not
Ordered categories ordinal logistic a performance rating from 1 to 5
Unordered categories multinomial logistic how someone left: quit, retired, laid off…

A multinomial logistic regression picks one category as the reference and fits a logistic regression of each of the other categories against it, all at the same time. The data are real: the US Office of Personnel Management (OPM) publishes a record for every federal employee who leaves.

1. The data

OPM’s Federal Workforce Data has a file for every month with one row per separation: a civilian employee leaving federal service. Each row says how the person left, their age group, years of service, agency, occupation, type of appointment and more. The files are free, need no login, and come through an API as Parquet files of one to four MB.

A monthly file holds the separations processed in that month, and some of them took effect months earlier: separations of September 2025 were still arriving in 2026. So we download every file from October 2023 to the latest one, and sort the separations by the date they took effect.

read_month <- function(year, month, version) {
  url  <- sprintf("https://data.opm.gov/api/v1/files/separations/%s/%s/%s/download", year, month, version)
  path <- tempfile(fileext = ".parquet")
  for (attempt in 1:3) {
    ok <- tryCatch(download.file(url, path, mode = "wb", quiet = TRUE) == 0, error = function(e) FALSE)
    if (ok) break
    Sys.sleep(10 * attempt)
  }
  if (!ok) stop("Could not download ", url)
  as.data.frame(read_parquet(path, col_select = columns))
}

Age and years of service go together, so these charts cannot tell whether age or service drives an exit. The model can: it estimates each effect holding the others constant.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: a reference category

Choose one way of leaving as the yardstick. We use Quit, an ordinary resignation: every other way of leaving is compared with quitting. Any category could be the reference; the predicted probabilities are the same, only the comparisons in the output change. In R the reference is the first level of the factor (levels(fy25$exit)[1] is Quit), and relevel() changes it.

Step 2: one equation for every other category

For person \(i\) and each way of leaving \(k\) other than quitting:

\[\eta_{ik} = \ln\frac{P(\text{exit}_i = k)}{P(\text{exit}_i = \text{Quit})} = \beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k}x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_{pk}x_{pi}\]

That is five equations, each with its own intercept and its own slopes. The left side is the log of the relative risk: how much more likely leaving by \(k\) is than quitting. Each equation looks like a logistic regression; the difference is that the five are estimated together, so that every person’s six probabilities add up to 1.

Step 3: from the equations to probabilities

\[P(\text{exit}_i = \text{Quit}) = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{j} e^{\eta_{ij}}} \qquad P(\text{exit}_i = k) = \frac{e^{\eta_{ik}}}{1 + \sum_{j} e^{\eta_{ij}}}\]

The sums run over the five categories other than Quit. Each probability is between 0 and 1, and the six add up to 1. This is the softmax function, the same formula that neural networks use to classify.

Step 4: what a coefficient means

\(e^{\beta}\) is the relative risk ratio (RRR): the number by which the ratio \(P(k)/P(\text{Quit})\) is multiplied when the predictor moves from its reference level to this level, with the other predictors held constant. An RRR of 2 means twice as likely to leave by \(k\) rather than by quitting. Some books and programs call it an odds ratio; either way it compares two categories with each other, not one category with all the others.

Step 5: how R finds the β’s

By maximum likelihood: R looks for the \(\beta\)’s that make the exits that people actually took as probable as possible,

\[\ln L = \sum_i \ln P(\text{exit}_i = \text{the exit that person } i \text{ took})\]

multinom() maximises it. It fits the model as a neural network without a hidden layer, which is why it lives in the package nnet.

Step 6: count the parameters

A predictor with \(c\) categories gets \(c - 1\) coefficients: age has 10 groups (9 coefficients), service 7 (6), supervisor and appointment 2 each (1 each) and occupation 3 (2). That is 19 slopes plus an intercept, 20 per equation, and 5 equations make 100 parameters. Multinomial models grow fast: every category needs enough people for a whole set of coefficients of its own.

4. Fitting the model

Every predictor is a category, so the 329,911 people fall into a limited number of combinations of age group, service, supervisor, appointment, occupation and exit. Fitting the model to the count of people in each combination gives the same estimates as fitting the people one by one: 300 people with the same values add the same term to the log-likelihood 300 times, which is what weights = Freq does. It takes a second instead of minutes.

7. An assumption to check: independence of irrelevant alternatives

The model assumes that the ratio of two probabilities, for example \(P(\text{Retired}) / P(\text{Quit})\), does not depend on what other ways of leaving there are: the independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA). The textbook example: a city adds a blue bus identical to its red bus. IIA predicts that the blue bus takes passengers from cars and from the red bus in proportion; in reality it takes them almost only from the red bus.

The deferred resignation was a new way of leaving in 2025. Under IIA it drew people from the other exits in proportion. A simple check: fit the model again without it, that is without the category and without the people who took it, and compare the coefficients that remain. If IIA holds, they hardly change.

The coefficients of retirement and early retirement move a lot: for 25 to 29 years of service, the RRR of retiring rather than quitting goes from 671 to 1,980. Those of layoffs and terminations move much less (a typical change of 12% and 11%, against 110% for retirement). So IIA is doubtful for the exits that are close to the deferred resignation. Models that relax it, the nested logit (similar exits share a “nest”) and the mixed logit, are in the package mlogit (Croissant, 2020). The formal version of this check, the Hausman–McFadden test (mlogit::hmftest()), would flag even trivial differences with this many people; the size of the changes tells more.

Three more things to keep in mind:

  • People in the same agency are not independent. Layoffs and early-retirement offers were decided agency by agency, so the standard errors above are too small. Agency as a predictor, or a multilevel multinomial model, would take this into account.
  • Only people who left. The model compares ways of leaving among the people who left; it says nothing about the people who stayed. Whether supervisors were more likely to leave at all needs the employment files too, which OPM also publishes.
  • The data keep changing. OPM calls the newest data preliminary, and late reports keep arriving: run this page again in a few months and the counts will be a little higher.

## The question: six ways to leave

In 2025 the US federal government lost staff in several ways at once. In January, most federal employees were offered a **deferred resignation**: stop working, stay on the payroll until 30 September 2025, then resign. Agencies offered **early retirement**, carried out **reductions in force** (layoffs) and **dismissed** staff. And people kept **quitting** and **retiring**, as they do every year.

So everyone who left federal service in 2025 left in one of six ways. This page asks: **among the people who left, what made one way of leaving more likely than another?** Did the young quit and the old retire? Who took the deferred resignation? Who was laid off?

The outcome is a **category with six values and no natural order**: quitting is not "more" or "less" than being laid off. The type of outcome decides the type of regression:

| Outcome | Regression | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Two categories | [logistic](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED.rmd) | hired or not |
| Ordered categories | [ordinal logistic](EXAMPLE_ORDINAL_LOGISTIC.Rmd) | a performance rating from 1 to 5 |
| **Unordered categories** | **multinomial logistic** | **how someone left: quit, retired, laid off...** |

A multinomial logistic regression picks one category as the **reference** and fits a logistic regression of each of the other categories against it, all at the same time. The data are real: the US Office of Personnel Management (OPM) publishes a record for every federal employee who leaves.

## 1. The data

OPM's [Federal Workforce Data](https://data.opm.gov/) has a file for every month with **one row per separation**: a civilian employee leaving federal service. Each row says how the person left, their age group, years of service, agency, occupation, type of appointment and more. The files are free, need no login, and come through an [API](https://data.opm.gov/get-data/api-access) as Parquet files of one to four MB.

A monthly file holds the separations **processed** in that month, and some of them took effect months earlier: separations of September 2025 were still arriving in 2026. So we download every file from October 2023 to the latest one, and sort the separations by the date they **took effect**.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: a reference category

Choose one way of leaving as the yardstick. We use **Quit**, an ordinary resignation: every other way of leaving is compared with quitting. Any category could be the reference; the predicted probabilities are the same, only the comparisons in the output change. In R the reference is the first level of the factor (`levels(fy25$exit)[1]` is `r levels(fy25$exit)[1]`), and `relevel()` changes it.

### Step 2: one equation for every other category

For person $i$ and each way of leaving $k$ other than quitting:

$$\eta_{ik} = \ln\frac{P(\text{exit}_i = k)}{P(\text{exit}_i = \text{Quit})} = \beta_{0k} + \beta_{1k}x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_{pk}x_{pi}$$

That is five equations, each with its own intercept and its own slopes. The left side is the log of the **relative risk**: how much more likely leaving by $k$ is than quitting. Each equation looks like a [logistic regression](EXAMPLE_IMBALANCED_LOGISTIC.Rmd); the difference is that the five are estimated together, so that every person's six probabilities add up to 1.

### Step 3: from the equations to probabilities

$$P(\text{exit}_i = \text{Quit}) = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_{j} e^{\eta_{ij}}} \qquad P(\text{exit}_i = k) = \frac{e^{\eta_{ik}}}{1 + \sum_{j} e^{\eta_{ij}}}$$

The sums run over the five categories other than Quit. Each probability is between 0 and 1, and the six add up to 1. This is the **softmax** function, the same formula that neural networks use to classify.

### Step 4: what a coefficient means

$e^{\beta}$ is the **relative risk ratio** (RRR): the number by which the ratio $P(k)/P(\text{Quit})$ is multiplied when the predictor moves from its reference level to this level, with the other predictors held constant. An RRR of 2 means twice as likely to leave by $k$ **rather than by quitting**. Some books and programs call it an odds ratio; either way it compares two categories with each other, not one category with all the others.

### Step 5: how R finds the β's

By maximum likelihood: R looks for the $\beta$'s that make the exits that people actually took as probable as possible,

$$\ln L = \sum_i \ln P(\text{exit}_i = \text{the exit that person } i \text{ took})$$

`multinom()` maximises it. It fits the model as a neural network without a hidden layer, which is why it lives in the package `nnet`.

### Step 6: count the parameters

A predictor with $c$ categories gets $c - 1$ coefficients: age has 10 groups (9 coefficients), service 7 (6), supervisor and appointment 2 each (1 each) and occupation 3 (2). That is 19 slopes plus an intercept, 20 per equation, and 5 equations make **100 parameters**. Multinomial models grow fast: every category needs enough people for a whole set of coefficients of its own.

## 4. Fitting the model

Every predictor is a category, so the `r num(nrow(fy25))` people fall into a limited number of combinations of age group, service, supervisor, appointment, occupation and exit. Fitting the model to the **count** of people in each combination gives the same estimates as fitting the people one by one: 300 people with the same values add the same term to the log-likelihood 300 times, which is what `weights = Freq` does. It takes a second instead of minutes.

```{r fit}
g <- as.data.frame(table(fy25[vars]))   # one row per combination; Freq = the number of people
g <- subset(g, Freq > 0)
nrow(g)

fit <- multinom(exit ~ age + service + supervisor + permanent + occupation,
                data = g, weights = Freq, maxit = 1000, trace = FALSE, Hess = TRUE)
summary(fit)
```

`maxit = 1000` lets the estimation run longer than the default 100 steps, `trace = FALSE` hides the log of the steps, and `Hess = TRUE` keeps what `summary()` needs for the standard errors.

### How to read the output

```{r output_parts}
s    <- summary(fit)
b    <- coef(fit)                      # one row per way of leaving, one column per term
se   <- s$standard.errors
z    <- b / se
p    <- 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))             # summary() prints no p values: compute them
rrr  <- exp(b)                         # relative risk ratios
low  <- exp(b - qnorm(0.975) * se)     # their 95% confidence intervals
high <- exp(b + qnorm(0.975) * se)
```

| Part | Meaning | Here |
|---|---|---|
| `Coefficients` | One row per way of leaving, compared with Quit; one column per predictor level. Each number is $b$: the change in the log relative risk | Row `Laid off`, column `supervisorYes`: *b* = `r f2(b["Laid off", "supervisorYes"])` |
| `Std. Errors` | The standard error of each $b$; smaller is more precise | `r f2(se["Laid off", "supervisorYes"])` |
| `Residual Deviance` | $-2 \ln L$: how badly the model fits; smaller is better. Used for the tests in section 6 | `r num(round(deviance(fit)))` |
| `AIC` | The deviance plus 2 for every parameter, for comparing models fitted to the same data | `r num(round(fit$AIC))` |

- **There are no *p* values.** Compute them as above: $z = b / SE$, and $p$ from the normal distribution. For `supervisorYes` in the row `Laid off`, *z* = `r f2(z["Laid off", "supervisorYes"])`, *p* `r pval(p["Laid off", "supervisorYes"])`.
- **The intercepts** are the log relative risks of the reference person: under 25, less than a year of service, not a supervisor, on a permanent appointment, in an occupation that is neither STEM nor health.
- **Huge numbers**, such as an RRR of `r rr(rrr["Early retirement", "age55-59"])` for early retirement at ages 55 to 59, mean that the reference group almost never leaves that way: nobody under 25 can retire early. They are common with categorical predictors and say little on their own; the predicted probabilities of section 5 are easier to read.
- **Every coefficient compares with Quit.** `supervisorYes` in the row `Laid off` says nothing about being laid off rather than retiring. For that comparison, subtract the two rows, b(Laid off) − b(Retired), or change the reference with `relevel()` and fit again.

The relative risk ratios of the three predictors that are not age or service:

## 6. Testing the predictors and the model

The **likelihood-ratio test** compares the deviances of two nested models. First the whole model against a model with no predictors, then each predictor: fit the model without it and see how much worse it gets.

```{r tests}
null <- multinom(exit ~ 1, data = g, weights = Freq, trace = FALSE)
model_test <- anova(null, fit)
model_test

tests <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c("age", "service", "supervisor", "permanent", "occupation"), function(v) {
  a <- anova(update(fit, as.formula(paste(". ~ . -", v))), fit)
  data.frame(predictor = v, chi_square = round(a$"LR stat."[2], 1), df = a$"   Df"[2],
             p = format.pval(a$"Pr(Chi)"[2], eps = 0.001))
}))
tests

mcfadden <- 1 - deviance(fit) / deviance(null)   # McFadden's pseudo-R squared
mcfadden
ranked <- unname(c(age = "age", service = "years of service", supervisor = "supervisor status",
                   permanent = "appointment type", occupation = "occupation")[tests$predictor[order(-tests$chi_square)]])
```

- **Every predictor is significant**: with `r num(nrow(fy25))` people even a tiny effect is. The $\chi^2$ values show which predictors matter most: `r ranked[1]`, `r ranked[2]` and `r ranked[3]`; `r ranked[5]` matters least. The degrees of freedom are the coefficients a predictor adds to all five equations: 9 age groups × 5 = 45.
- **McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$** = `r f2(mcfadden)`: one minus the deviance of the model divided by the deviance of the model with no predictors. It is not a share of explained variance and it runs much lower than the $R^2$ of a linear regression; use it to compare models fitted to the same data.

How often does the model name the right exit, if it picks the most likely one for each person?

```{r classification}
predicted <- factor(colnames(fitted(fit))[max.col(fitted(fit), ties.method = "first")], levels = exits)
accuracy  <- weighted.mean(predicted == g$exit, g$Freq)
base_rate <- max(prop.table(table(fy25$exit)))   # always guessing the most common exit
c(model = accuracy, most_common_exit = base_rate)

# how each exit was classified: % of the row
round(100 * prop.table(xtabs(Freq ~ exit + predicted, data = cbind(g, predicted)), 1))
```

The model names the right exit for `r pct(accuracy)` of the people, against `r pct(base_rate)` for always guessing the most common one. But it almost never predicts an early retirement or a layoff, even though it knows who is most at risk of them: these exits are rare, so for almost nobody are they the **most** likely one. Accuracy is a poor guide when some categories are rare, as [Logistic Regression With a Rare Outcome](EXAMPLE_IMBALANCED_LOGISTIC.Rmd) shows; the predicted probabilities carry the information.

## 7. An assumption to check: independence of irrelevant alternatives

The model assumes that the ratio of two probabilities, for example $P(\text{Retired}) / P(\text{Quit})$, does not depend on what other ways of leaving there are: the **independence of irrelevant alternatives** (IIA). The textbook example: a city adds a blue bus identical to its red bus. IIA predicts that the blue bus takes passengers from cars and from the red bus in proportion; in reality it takes them almost only from the red bus.

The deferred resignation was a new way of leaving in 2025. Under IIA it drew people from the other exits in proportion. A simple check: fit the model again **without** it, that is without the category and without the people who took it, and compare the coefficients that remain. If IIA holds, they hardly change.

```{r iia}
g2   <- droplevels(subset(g, exit != "Deferred resignation"))
fit2 <- update(fit, data = g2)

show <- c("age55-59", "age60-64", "service25-29", "service30+", "supervisorYes", "permanentNo")
iia  <- expand.grid(term = show, exit = rownames(coef(fit2)), stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
iia$all_six   <- exp(coef(fit)[cbind(iia$exit, iia$term)])
iia$without   <- exp(coef(fit2)[cbind(iia$exit, iia$term)])
knitr::kable(data.frame(exit = iia$exit, term = iia$term, rr(iia$all_six), rr(iia$without)),
             col.names = c("Way of leaving (against Quit)", "Term", "RRR, six exits",
                           "RRR without the deferred resignation"), align = "llrr")
moved <- function(k) median(abs(log(iia$without / iia$all_six))[iia$exit == k])   # typical change, log scale
```

The coefficients of **retirement** and **early retirement** move a lot: for 25 to 29 years of service, the RRR of retiring rather than quitting goes from `r rr(iia$all_six[iia$exit == "Retired" & iia$term == "service25-29"])` to `r rr(iia$without[iia$exit == "Retired" & iia$term == "service25-29"])`. Those of **layoffs** and **terminations** move much less (a typical change of `r pct(exp(moved("Laid off")) - 1)` and `r pct(exp(moved("Terminated")) - 1)`, against `r pct(exp(moved("Retired")) - 1)` for retirement). So IIA is doubtful for the exits that are close to the deferred resignation. Models that relax it, the nested logit (similar exits share a "nest") and the mixed logit, are in the package `mlogit` (Croissant, 2020). The formal version of this check, the Hausman–McFadden test (`mlogit::hmftest()`), would flag even trivial differences with this many people; the size of the changes tells more.

Three more things to keep in mind:

- **People in the same agency are not independent.** Layoffs and early-retirement offers were decided agency by agency, so the standard errors above are too small. Agency as a predictor, or a [multilevel](EXAMPLE_MULTILEVEL.Rmd) multinomial model, would take this into account.
- **Only people who left.** The model compares ways of leaving among the people who left; it says nothing about the people who stayed. Whether supervisors were more likely to **leave at all** needs the employment files too, which OPM also publishes.
- **The data keep changing.** OPM calls the newest data preliminary, and late reports keep arriving: run this page again in a few months and the counts will be a little higher.

## 9. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | The outcome is a category with three or more values and no order |
| **Why not separate logistic regressions** | Their probabilities do not add up to 1, and there is no single test of a predictor |
| **Why not [ordinal logistic regression](EXAMPLE_ORDINAL_LOGISTIC.Rmd)** | The categories have no order |
| **How (R)** | `nnet::multinom(y ~ x, data)`; with counts per combination of values, `weights = n` |
| **Output** | One equation per category against the reference; `exp()` of a coefficient is a relative risk ratio |
| **Easiest to read** | Predicted probabilities: `predict(fit, newdata, type = "probs")` |
| **Tests** | Likelihood-ratio tests with `anova()`; McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$ |
| **Assumptions** | Independent observations; independence of irrelevant alternatives; enough people in every category for its own set of coefficients |
| **If IIA fails** | A nested or mixed logit, with `mlogit` |

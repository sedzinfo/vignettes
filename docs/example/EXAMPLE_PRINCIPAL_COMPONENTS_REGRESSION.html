Principal Components Regression: What Does the Yield Curve Say About Next Year’s Economy?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Principal components regression (PCR) in R, by hand and with pls::pcr(), on eight US interest rates that correlate above .94 and the growth of the US economy a year later (FRED, 1976-2019): why least squares fails with collinear predictors, level, slope and curvature, scores and loadings, choosing the number of components by cross-validation in blocks, back to coefficients, the trap of the component with the largest variance, ridge for comparison, Newey-West errors and APA reporting.

The question: eight predictors that say the same thing

The United States borrows money for three months, for a year, for five, for ten. Each of these loans has its own interest rate, and together they form the yield curve. People who watch the economy look at its shape. When the long rates are much higher than the short ones, the economy usually grows. When the short rates climb above the long ones, the curve is inverted, and a recession often follows (Estrella & Hardouvelis, 1991).

So: can the eight interest rates of today predict how much the economy will grow in the next year?

A regression with eight predictors is easy to run and hopeless to read. The eight rates rise and fall together; two neighbours correlate above .99. Least squares cannot tell them apart, and its coefficients explode.

Principal components regression (PCR) goes around the problem in two steps. First it replaces the eight rates by a few new variables that summarise them and do not correlate with each other. Then it uses these in an ordinary regression. This example is a kind one, because the new variables have a meaning that anybody can see. It also shows the weak point of the method, in its purest form.

1. The data

FRED, the database of the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, gives every series as a CSV file, free and with no login. We take the monthly interest rates for eight maturities, average them over each quarter, and the real gross domestic product (GDP) of the quarter (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, n.d.; U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, n.d.).

4. The method step by step, with formulas

Step 1: principal components

Centre the columns of \(X\) (subtract the means). We look for a weighted sum of the eight rates, \(t_1 = X v_1\), with weights of length 1, that varies as much as possible. That is the first component. The second, \(t_2 = X v_2\), varies as much as possible among all the weighted sums that are uncorrelated with the first. And so on, until there are eight. The weights \(v_j\) are the loadings, the values \(t_{ij}\) the scores. They come from the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of \(X\):

\[S = \frac{X^\top X}{n-1}, \qquad S v_j = \lambda_j v_j, \qquad \text{Var}(t_j) = \lambda_j\]

Code 
Xc      <- scale(X, scale = FALSE)                 # centred, not divided by the SD
eig     <- eigen(cov(X))
V       <- eig$vectors
# +1 or -1 for each component
turn <- sign(c(V[1, 1], V[8, 2] - V[1, 2], V[1, 3] + V[8, 3], rep(1, 5)))
V       <- V %*% diag(turn)
dimnames(V) <- list(colnames(X), paste0("PC", 1:8))
Tscores <- Xc %*% V
share   <- eig$values / sum(eig$values)
round(100 * share, 3)
[1] 98.551  1.344  0.081  0.018  0.003  0.002  0.001  0.001
Code 
max(abs(abs(prcomp(X)$x) - abs(Tscores)))          # the same as prcomp()
[1] 1.131742e-12

The sign of a component is arbitrary (if \(v\) is a solution, so is \(-v\)), and we turned the first three so that they are easy to read. All the rates are in the same unit, percent per year, so we did not standardise them. With predictors in different units one must (scale. = TRUE), or the component follows the variable with the largest numbers.

Code 
years <- c(0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10)

The eight rates have turned into three ideas, known to every bond trader since Litterman and Scheinkman (1991):

  • PC1, the level (98.6% of the variance): nearly equal weights. High when all rates are high.
  • PC2, the slope (1.3%): negative weights for the short, positive for the long rates. High when the curve is steep, negative when it is inverted.
  • PC3, the curvature (0.08%): positive at both ends, negative in the middle.

The remaining five together have 0.02% of the variance.

Step 2: regress the outcome on the scores

Take the first \(a\) components and run an ordinary regression:

\[y = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 t_1 + \dots + \gamma_a t_a + e\]

The scores are uncorrelated, so the coefficient of a component does not change when others are added or removed. There is no collinearity left.

Step 3: translate back to the original predictors

The scores are weighted sums of the rates, so the regression on the scores is a regression on the rates, with coefficients

\[\hat\beta_{PCR} = V_a \hat\gamma = \sum_{j=1}^{a} \hat\gamma_j v_j\]

With all eight components this is exactly the least-squares solution. With fewer, the directions in which the predictors hardly vary are left out, and those were the directions that made the coefficients explode: the variance of \(\hat\gamma_j\) is \(\sigma^2 / \big((n-1)\lambda_j\big)\), huge when \(\lambda_j\) is tiny.

Step 4: choose the number of components

PCR has one setting, \(a\). It is chosen by cross-validation: which number of components predicts quarters that were not used in the fit best?

Look at the two rows of the first table above. The level, with 98.6% of the variance of the predictors, correlates with growth at 0.04: it says nothing. The slope, with 1.3% of the variance, correlates at 0.41, and the curvature, with 0.08%, at -0.23.

This is the weak point of PCR. The components are made without looking at the outcome. The component with the largest variance need not be the one that matters, and the method has no way of knowing (Jolliffe, 1982). Somebody who keeps “the components that explain 95% of the variance”, a common rule, would have kept the level only, and found nothing.

How many components? Cross-validation in blocks

Neighbouring quarters are nearly copies of each other, so leaving out single quarters at random would be no test at all. We cut the years into ten blocks, leave out one, and also the four quarters on each side of it, whose outcomes overlap with the block:

Code 
block <- cut(seq_len(n), 10, labels = FALSE)
cv_error <- function(predict_fun) {
  pred <- numeric(n)
  for (k in 1:10) {
    test <- which(block == k)
    # the block and its neighbours
    near <- which(sapply(seq_len(n), function(i) min(abs(i - test))) <= 4)
    pred[test] <- predict_fun(setdiff(seq_len(n), near), test)
  }
  sqrt(mean((y - pred)^2))
}
pcr_predict <- function(a) function(train, test) {
  if (a == 0) return(rep(mean(y[train]), length(test)))
  m <- pcr(y ~ X, ncomp = a, data = list(y = y[train], X = X[train, ]))
  drop(predict(m, newdata = list(X = X[test, , drop = FALSE]), ncomp = a))
}
spread <- X[, "10 years"] - X[, "3 months"] # the classic single predictor
spread_predict <- function(train, test) {
  b <- coef(lm(y[train] ~ spread[train])); b[1] + b[2] * spread[test]
}
set.seed(21)
ridge_predict <- function(train, test) {
  m <- cv.glmnet(
    X[train, ],
    y[train],
    alpha = 0,
    standardize = FALSE,
    lambda = 10^seq(3, -4, length.out = 80)
  )
  drop(predict(m, X[test, , drop = FALSE], s = "lambda.min"))
}
cv <- c(
  sapply(0:8, function(a) cv_error(pcr_predict(a))),
  cv_error(spread_predict),
  cv_error(ridge_predict)
)

1985-2018      136  0.42 0.11

It is not. The slope mattered much more until 1984 than after. A model that was chosen on the whole period knows how the story ended. A real forecaster did not.

7. Things to keep in mind

  • Choose the components by how they predict, not by their variance. Cross-validate, look at the correlation of every component with the outcome, and do not trust the rule of 80% or 95% of the variance.
  • Nor choose them one by one by their p values. With many components, picking the significant ones is stepwise regression with all its problems. Taking the first \(a\) in their order is a safer restriction.
  • Standardise unless all the predictors are in the same unit and their differences in variance mean something.
  • The components belong to the sample. In cross-validation they must be computed from the training rows only, as pcr() does, and for new data the old means and loadings are used.
  • PCR does not select predictors. Every component contains all of them. To leave some out, use the lasso.
  • Ridge regression is the smooth version of the same idea: it shrinks a direction more the smaller its variance (see regularized regression). PCR keeps it or drops it.
  • Partial least squares chooses its components by looking at the outcome, which repairs the weak point shown here.
  • Factor analysis is not PCR. A component is a weighted sum of the observed variables. A factor is a hidden cause of them, with its own model of measurement error.
  • Overlapping outcomes make a short sample look long. The 170 quarters here are about 42 independent years, with 14 quarters of falling GDP ahead, in a handful of recessions.

We predicted the growth of US real GDP over the following four quarters from the interest rates of eight maturities (3 months to 10 years; quarterly means, 1976Q3–2018Q4, N = 170). ## The question: eight predictors that say the same thing

The United States borrows money for three months, for a year, for five, for ten. Each of these loans has its own interest rate, and together they form the **yield curve**. People who watch the economy look at its shape. When the long rates are much higher than the short ones, the economy usually grows. When the short rates climb above the long ones, the curve is *inverted*, and a recession often follows (Estrella & Hardouvelis, 1991).

So: **can the eight interest rates of today predict how much the economy will grow in the next year?**

A regression with eight predictors is easy to run and hopeless to read. The eight rates rise and fall together; two neighbours correlate above .99. Least squares cannot tell them apart, and its coefficients explode.

**Principal components regression** (PCR) goes around the problem in two steps. First it replaces the eight rates by a few **new** variables that summarise them and do not correlate with each other. Then it uses these in an ordinary regression. This example is a kind one, because the new variables have a meaning that anybody can see. It also shows the weak point of the method, in its purest form.

## 3. Why least squares fails

```{r ols}
ols <- lm(y ~ X)
ols_table <- coef(summary(ols))[-1, ]
rownames(ols_table) <- colnames(X)
round(ols_table, 2)
vif <- diag(solve(R))                      # variance inflation factors
round(vif)
c(R2 = summary(ols)$r.squared)
```

Read the column of estimates. The 2-year rate: `r f1(ols_table["2 years", 1])`. The 5-year rate: `r f1(ols_table["5 years", 1])`. So the economy should grow by `r f1(ols_table["2 years", 1])` points more when the 2-year rate is one point higher and everything else stays the same. But everything else **never** stays the same. When the 2-year rate rises, the 1-year and the 3-year rates rise with it.

The **variance inflation factor** says how many times the variance of a coefficient is larger than it would be with uncorrelated predictors. A usual warning level is 10. Here it goes up to `r formatC(max(vif), format = "f", digits = 0, big.mark = ",")`. The fit is not the problem: the predictions of least squares are still meaningful as long as the rates move together. But the coefficients are noise, and they change wildly if a few quarters are left out:

```{r unstable}
half <- time < 1998

    second_half = coef(lm(y ~ X, subset = !half))[-1]

)
```

## 4. The method step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: principal components

Centre the columns of $X$ (subtract the means). We look for a weighted sum of the eight rates, $t_1 = X v_1$, with weights of length 1, that **varies as much as possible**. That is the first component. The second, $t_2 = X v_2$, varies as much as possible among all the weighted sums that are uncorrelated with the first. And so on, until there are eight. The weights $v_j$ are the **loadings**, the values $t_{ij}$ the **scores**. They come from the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of $X$:

$$S = \frac{X^\top X}{n-1}, \qquad S v_j = \lambda_j v_j, \qquad \text{Var}(t_j) = \lambda_j$$

```{r components}
Xc      <- scale(X, scale = FALSE)                 # centred, not divided by the SD
eig     <- eigen(cov(X))
V       <- eig$vectors
# +1 or -1 for each component
turn <- sign(c(V[1, 1], V[8, 2] - V[1, 2], V[1, 3] + V[8, 3], rep(1, 5)))
V       <- V %*% diag(turn)
dimnames(V) <- list(colnames(X), paste0("PC", 1:8))
Tscores <- Xc %*% V
share   <- eig$values / sum(eig$values)
round(100 * share, 3)
max(abs(abs(prcomp(X)$x) - abs(Tscores)))          # the same as prcomp()
```

The sign of a component is arbitrary (if $v$ is a solution, so is $-v$), and we turned the first three so that they are easy to read. All the rates are in the same unit, percent per year, so we did **not** standardise them. With predictors in different units one must (`scale. = TRUE`), or the component follows the variable with the largest numbers.

```{r loadings}
years <- c(0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10)
load_long <- data.frame(
  years = rep(years, 3),
  loading = as.vector(V[, 1:3]),
  component = factor(
    rep(c("PC1: level", "PC2: slope", "PC3: curvature"), each = 8),
    levels = c("PC1: level", "PC2: slope", "PC3: curvature")
  )
)
ggplot(load_long, aes(years, loading, colour = component)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "grey60") +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1) + geom_point(size = 2.2) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("#2a78d6", "#eb6834", "#1baf7a"), name = NULL) +
  labs(title = "The weights of the first three components", x = "maturity (years)", y = "loading") +
  theme_bw() + theme(legend.position = "bottom")
```

The eight rates have turned into three ideas, known to every bond trader since Litterman and Scheinkman (1991):

- **PC1, the level** (`r pct(share[1])` of the variance): nearly equal weights. High when all rates are high.
- **PC2, the slope** (`r pct(share[2])`): negative weights for the short, positive for the long rates. High when the curve is steep, negative when it is inverted.
- **PC3, the curvature** (`r pct(share[3], 2)`): positive at both ends, negative in the middle.

The remaining five together have `r pct(sum(share[4:8]), 2)` of the variance.

### Step 2: regress the outcome on the scores

Take the first $a$ components and run an ordinary regression:

$$y = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 t_1 + \dots + \gamma_a t_a + e$$

The scores are uncorrelated, so the coefficient of a component does not change when others are added or removed. There is no collinearity left.

### Step 3: translate back to the original predictors

The scores are weighted sums of the rates, so the regression on the scores **is** a regression on the rates, with coefficients

$$\hat\beta_{PCR} = V_a \hat\gamma = \sum_{j=1}^{a} \hat\gamma_j v_j$$

With all eight components this is exactly the least-squares solution. With fewer, the directions in which the predictors hardly vary are left out, and those were the directions that made the coefficients explode: the variance of $\hat\gamma_j$ is $\sigma^2 / \big((n-1)\lambda_j\big)$, huge when $\lambda_j$ is tiny.

### Step 4: choose the number of components

PCR has one setting, $a$. It is chosen by **cross-validation**: which number of components predicts quarters that were not used in the fit best?

## 5. Fitting the model

### How are the components related to growth?

```{r component_regression}
pcr_by_hand <- lm(y ~ Tscores[, 1:3])
names(pcr_by_hand$coefficients) <- c("(Intercept)", "level", "slope", "curvature")
gamma <- coef(pcr_by_hand)
r_components <- drop(cor(Tscores, y))
round(rbind(share_of_variance_of_X = share, correlation_with_growth = r_components), 4)
summary(pcr_by_hand)
```

The outcome overlaps: the growth of the next four quarters, seen from two neighbouring quarters, shares three of them. So the residuals are autocorrelated and the standard errors above are too small. The Newey–West standard errors (see [generalized least squares](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd)) allow for it:

```{r newey_west}
nw    <- NeweyWest(pcr_by_hand, lag = 6, prewhite = FALSE)
hac   <- coeftest(pcr_by_hand, vcov = nw)
ci    <- coefci(pcr_by_hand, vcov. = nw)
hac
```

Look at the two rows of the first table above. The **level**, with `r pct(share[1])` of the variance of the predictors, correlates with growth at `r f2(r_components[1])`: it says nothing. The **slope**, with `r pct(share[2])` of the variance, correlates at `r f2(r_components[2])`, and the **curvature**, with `r pct(share[3], 2)`, at `r f2(r_components[3])`.

This is the weak point of PCR. The components are made **without looking at the outcome**. The component with the largest variance need not be the one that matters, and the method has no way of knowing (Jolliffe, 1982). Somebody who keeps "the components that explain 95% of the variance", a common rule, would have kept the level only, and found nothing.

### How many components? Cross-validation in blocks

Neighbouring quarters are nearly copies of each other, so leaving out single quarters at random would be no test at all. We cut the years into ten **blocks**, leave out one, and also the four quarters on each side of it, whose outcomes overlap with the block:

```{r cross_validation}
block <- cut(seq_len(n), 10, labels = FALSE)
cv_error <- function(predict_fun) {
  pred <- numeric(n)
  for (k in 1:10) {
    test <- which(block == k)
    # the block and its neighbours
    near <- which(sapply(seq_len(n), function(i) min(abs(i - test))) <= 4)
    pred[test] <- predict_fun(setdiff(seq_len(n), near), test)
  }
  sqrt(mean((y - pred)^2))
}
pcr_predict <- function(a) function(train, test) {
  if (a == 0) return(rep(mean(y[train]), length(test)))
  m <- pcr(y ~ X, ncomp = a, data = list(y = y[train], X = X[train, ]))
  drop(predict(m, newdata = list(X = X[test, , drop = FALSE]), ncomp = a))
}
spread <- X[, "10 years"] - X[, "3 months"] # the classic single predictor
spread_predict <- function(train, test) {
  b <- coef(lm(y[train] ~ spread[train])); b[1] + b[2] * spread[test]
}
set.seed(21)
ridge_predict <- function(train, test) {
  m <- cv.glmnet(
    X[train, ],
    y[train],
    alpha = 0,
    standardize = FALSE,
    lambda = 10^seq(3, -4, length.out = 80)
  )
  drop(predict(m, X[test, , drop = FALSE], s = "lambda.min"))
}
cv <- c(
  sapply(0:8, function(a) cv_error(pcr_predict(a))),
  cv_error(spread_predict),
  cv_error(ridge_predict)
)
names(cv) <- c(paste(0:8, "components"), "spread 10 years - 3 months", "ridge")
cbind(error = round(cv, 3))
best <- which.min(cv[2:9])
```

```{r cv_chart}
cv_data <- data.frame(a = 0:8, rmse = cv[1:9])
ggplot(cv_data, aes(a, rmse)) +
  geom_line(colour = "#2a78d6", linewidth = 0.9) +
  geom_point(colour = "#2a78d6", size = 2.5) +
  geom_hline(
    yintercept = cv[["spread 10 years - 3 months"]],
    linetype = "dashed",
    colour = "#eb6834"
  ) +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = 0:8) +
  labs(
    title = "Error of the forecast in left-out blocks of years",
    subtitle = "Dashed: a regression on the difference of the 10-year and the 3-month rate",
    x = "number of components",
    y = "root mean squared error (points of growth)"
  ) +
  theme_bw()
```

- **No component** (always predict the mean): `r f2(cv[1])` points. **One** (the level) is worse: `r f2(cv[2])`. With **`r best`** the error is smallest, `r f2(cv[best + 1])`. With all 8, which is least squares, `r f2(cv[9])`.
- The **spread** between the 10-year and the 3-month rate, the one-number version of the slope, has `r f2(cv[["spread 10 years - 3 months"]])`. **Ridge** regression, which shrinks all the directions instead of cutting some off, has `r f2(cv[["ridge"]])`.
- Nothing is impressive: the best model improves on the plain mean by `r pct(1 - cv[best + 1] / cv[1], 0)`. The economy is hard to predict.

```{r scores_chart}
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")
```

Is the relation stable?

```{r stability}
periods <- list(`1976-2018` = rep(TRUE, n), `1976-1984` = time < 1985, `1985-2018` = time >= 1985)
stable <- t(sapply(periods, function(p) {
  m <- lm(y[p] ~ Tscores[p, 1:3])
  c(quarters = sum(p), slope = coef(m)[[3]], R2 = summary(m)$r.squared)
}))
round(stable, 2)
```

It is not. The slope mattered much more until 1984 than after. A model that was chosen on the whole period knows how the story ended. A real forecaster did not.

## 7. Things to keep in mind

- **Choose the components by how they predict, not by their variance.** Cross-validate, look at the correlation of **every** component with the outcome, and do not trust the rule of 80% or 95% of the variance.
- **Nor choose them one by one by their *p* values.** With many components, picking the significant ones is [stepwise regression](EXAMPLE_STEPWISE_REGRESSION.Rmd) with all its problems. Taking the first $a$ in their order is a safer restriction.
- **Standardise** unless all the predictors are in the same unit and their differences in variance mean something.
- **The components belong to the sample.** In cross-validation they must be computed from the training rows only, as `pcr()` does, and for new data the **old** means and loadings are used.
- **PCR does not select predictors.** Every component contains all of them. To leave some out, use the lasso.
- **Ridge regression** is the smooth version of the same idea: it shrinks a direction more the smaller its variance (see [regularized regression](EXAMPLE_REGULARIZED_REGRESSION.Rmd)). PCR keeps it or drops it.
- **Partial least squares** chooses its components by looking at the outcome, which repairs the weak point shown here.
- **Factor analysis is not PCR.** A component is a weighted sum of the observed variables. A factor is a hidden cause of them, with its own model of measurement error.
- **Overlapping outcomes** make a short sample look long. The `r n` quarters here are about `r round(n / 4)` independent years, with `r sum(y < 0)` quarters of falling GDP ahead, in a handful of recessions.

**Method** (data analysis):

> We predicted the growth of US real GDP over the following four quarters from the interest rates of eight maturities (3 months to 10 years; quarterly means, `r quarters[1]`–`r tail(quarters, 1)`, *N* = `r n`). Because the rates were highly correlated (*r* = `r nz(min(R[lower.tri(R)]))` to `r nz(max(R[lower.tri(R)]), 3)`; largest variance inflation factor = `r formatC(max(vif), format = "f", digits = 0, big.mark = ",")`), we used principal components regression on the centred, unstandardised rates. The number of components was chosen by cross-validation with ten blocks of consecutive quarters, leaving out four quarters on each side of the block. Standard errors are Newey–West with six lags, because the outcomes of neighbouring quarters overlap. Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`) with the package pls (Mevik & Wehrens, 2007).

**Results:**

> The first three components explained `r pct(sum(share[1:3]), 2)` of the variance of the rates and corresponded to the level, the slope and the curvature of the yield curve (Table 1). Cross-validation favoured `r best` components (RMSE = `r f2(cv[best + 1])` percentage points, against `r f2(cv[1])` for the mean and `r f2(cv[9])` for least squares with all the rates). The level was not related to growth, *b* = `r f3(gamma["level"])`, *p* `r pval(hac["level", 4])`. The coefficient of the slope was `r f2(stable["1976-1984", "slope"])` until 1984 and `r f2(stable["1985-2018", "slope"])` afterwards.

## 9. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | Many, highly correlated predictors, and the aim is prediction or a few interpretable summaries |
| **Step 1** | Principal components of $X$: uncorrelated weighted sums, ordered by variance |
| **Step 2** | Regress $y$ on the first $a$ scores |
| **Back** | $\hat\beta = V_a \hat\gamma$: coefficients for the original predictors |
| **How (R)** | `pls::pcr(y ~ X, ncomp = , scale = , validation = "CV")`, or `prcomp()` and `lm()` |
| **Choose $a$** | Cross-validation, with the components computed inside |
| **Weak point** | The components ignore $y$; small-variance components can matter most |
| **Relatives** | Ridge (shrinks instead of cutting), [partial least squares](EXAMPLE_PARTIAL_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd) (looks at $y$) |
| **Related pages** | [Regularized regression](EXAMPLE_REGULARIZED_REGRESSION.Rmd), [stepwise regression](EXAMPLE_STEPWISE_REGRESSION.Rmd), [generalized least squares](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED_LEAST_SQUARES.Rmd) |

Zero-Inflated Poisson and Negative Binomial: Why Do Some PhD Students Publish Nothing?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Zero-inflated Poisson and negative binomial regression in R, by hand and with pscl::zeroinfl(), on the articles of 915 PhD students in biochemistry: two kinds of zeros, how to read the two parts, expected counts and the probability of a structural zero, rootograms, comparison with Poisson, negative binomial and hurdle models by AIC, BIC, Vuong’s test and a parametric bootstrap, why many zeros do not mean zero inflation, and APA reporting.

The question: two kinds of nothing

Of 915 PhD students in biochemistry, 275 published no article in the last three years of their studies. Why not?

There may be two kinds of them. Some were never going to publish: they planned a career outside research, or their laboratory does not publish with students. For them the number is zero by nature. Others could have published, and worked on it, but it happened that nothing appeared in those three years. The data show the same 0 for both.

Zero-inflated models (Lambert, 1992) say exactly this: the zeros come from two sources. One part of the model says who is in the group of certain zeros. The other is an ordinary count model (Poisson or negative binomial) for everybody else, which produces zeros too. They are popular, easy to fit, and easy to over-interpret. Many zeros do not prove that there are two kinds of them. This page fits the models and then asks whether they are needed.

The mean is 1.69. A Poisson distribution with this mean would have 168 zeros; there are 275. But it would also have a variance of 1.69, and the variance is 3.71, overdispersed. Too many zeros are one sign of overdispersion, and a long tail to the right is another. Both can come from the same cause: students differ in more ways than we have measured.

3. The model step by step, with formulas

Step 1: the count part

For the students who can publish, a count distribution with mean \(\mu_i = \exp(x_i^\top\beta)\). Either Poisson, or negative binomial with variance \(\mu + \mu^2/\theta\).

Step 2: the zero part

With probability \(\pi_i\) a student belongs to the group that never publishes (a structural zero):

\[\log\frac{\pi_i}{1 - \pi_i} = z_i^\top\gamma\]

Step 3: put together

\[P(y_i = 0) = \pi_i + (1 - \pi_i)\, f(0 \mid \mu_i), \qquad P(y_i = k) = (1 - \pi_i)\, f(k \mid \mu_i) \;\text{ for } k \ge 1\]

A zero can come from either source. Anything else can only come from the count part. The mean is \(E(y_i) = (1 - \pi_i)\,\mu_i\).

Step 4: the difference from a hurdle model

A hurdle model has one kind of zero, and its count part cannot produce zeros. So its zero part says “any article or none”, and the zero part of a zero-inflated model says “in the group that never publishes”. The signs are opposite, and the meanings differ. This is the most common mistake in reading the output.

Step 5: maximum likelihood, by hand

Code 
X <- model.matrix(~ female + married + kids + prestige + mentor, data = phd)
y <- phd$articles
k <- ncol(X)
minus_loglik <- function(par) {                              # zero-inflated Poisson
  mu <- exp(drop(X %*% par[1:k])); p <- plogis(drop(X %*% par[k + 1:k]))
  -sum(log(ifelse(y == 0, p + (1 - p) * dpois(0, mu), (1 - p) * dpois(y, mu))))
}
pois  <- glm(articles ~ female + married + kids + prestige + mentor, family = poisson, data = phd)
start <- c(coef(pois), -1, rep(0, k - 1))
hand  <- optim(start, minus_loglik, method = "BFGS", control = list(maxit = 1000, reltol = 1e-12))
zip <- zeroinfl(
  articles ~ female + married + kids + prestige + mentor,
  data = phd,
  dist = "poisson"
)

Poisson expects 191 zeros instead of 275. But the plain negative binomial expects 278, without any zero inflation. It has one parameter more than Poisson, and it repairs the zeros and the tail at the same time. ZIP expects 273.

A rootogram compares all the counts (Kleiber & Zeileis, 2016). The bars are the observed frequencies, hanging from the expected ones, on a square-root scale. A bar that ends above the zero line means too few observed, one that goes below it too many.

The Poisson model fails everywhere. ZIP repairs the zero and misses the rest: it expects too few students with one article (the bar goes below the line), too many with three, and too few with six or seven. The NB and the ZINB both fit.

From Poisson to ZIP the AIC falls by 81. From Poisson to NB it falls by 178, with one parameter instead of six. The ZINB is 9.9 points better than the NB by AIC, and 19.0 points worse by BIC, which charges more for its six extra parameters. The two criteria disagree, and the evidence for a second kind of zero is weak.

Vuong’s test, and why not to use it here

Code 
vt <- capture.output(vuong(nb, zinb))
cat(vt, sep = "\n")
Vuong Non-Nested Hypothesis Test-Statistic: 
(test-statistic is asymptotically distributed N(0,1) under the
 null that the models are indistinguishible)
-------------------------------------------------------------
              Vuong z-statistic             H_A  p-value
Raw                   -2.241831 model2 > model1 0.012486
AIC-corrected         -1.015385 model2 > model1 0.154961
BIC-corrected          1.939690 model1 > model2 0.026209

Vuong’s test compares two models that are not nested. It is printed in many papers as a “test of zero inflation”. But the NB is a special case of the ZINB (with \(\pi = 0\), which is at the edge of the possible values), so the conditions of the test do not hold, and its p value means little (Wilson, 2015). And the raw, AIC-corrected and BIC-corrected versions give three different answers.

A parametric bootstrap

The honest test simulates. Take the fitted NB (no inflation), generate new data from it, fit both models, and see how often the gain in likelihood is as large as ours by chance alone:

Code 
gain <- 2 * (as.numeric(logLik(zinb)) - as.numeric(logLik(nb)))
set.seed(81)
gains <- replicate(200, {
  d <- phd
  d$articles <- rnbinom(nrow(d), mu = fitted(nb), size = nb$theta)
  a <- try(suppressWarnings(glm.nb(formula(nb), data = d)), silent = TRUE)
  z <- try(suppressWarnings(zeroinfl(formula(zinb), data = d, dist = "negbin")), silent = TRUE)
  if (inherits(a, "try-error") || inherits(z, "try-error")) {
    NA
  } else {
    2 * (as.numeric(logLik(z)) - as.numeric(logLik(a)))
  }
})
p_boot <- (1 + sum(gains >= gain, na.rm = TRUE)) / (1 + sum(!is.na(gains)))
cbind(
  value = c(
    our_gain = gain,
    mean_gain_without_inflation = mean(gains, na.rm = TRUE),
    p = p_boot,
    failed = sum(is.na(gains))
  )
)
                                 value
our_gain                    21.9349029
mean_gain_without_inflation  9.0123679
p                            0.0199005
failed                       0.0000000

Our gain is 21.9. Without any inflation the gain is 9.0 on average, and a gain as large as ours has the probability p = .020. So the ZINB describes something that the NB does not. The next section asks what.

By the ZINB, about 54 of the 915 students are certain zeros, which is 19% of those with no article. What the zero part has found may simply be a bend. The log of the expected count is not a straight line in the mentor’s articles: it rises quickly at first and then slowly. The NB has to use one slope for all, and the zero part offers a way to bend the curve near zero. Give the NB a bend of its own, and compare again:

A warning from simulation

What does one conclude who compares only Poisson with ZIP, when the truth is a negative binomial without any certain zeros?

Code 
set.seed(82)
false_zip <- replicate(200, {
  d <- phd
  d$articles <- rnbinom(nrow(d), mu = fitted(nb), size = nb$theta)
  p0 <- glm(formula(pois), family = poisson, data = d)
  z0 <- suppressWarnings(zeroinfl(formula(zip), data = d))
  c(zip_wins = AIC(z0) < AIC(p0), certain_zeros = mean(predict(z0, type = "zero")))
})
rowMeans(false_zip)
     zip_wins certain_zeros 
    1.0000000     0.1787387

ZIP beats Poisson in 100% of the data sets, and it declares 18% of the students to be certain zeros on average. There were none. Beating Poisson proves overdispersion, not two kinds of zeros.

7. Things to keep in mind

  • First the negative binomial. Many zeros do not mean zero inflation (Warton, 2005). Use a zero-inflated model when theory says that there are certain zeros (people who do not own a car and car accidents, men and pregnancies), not because the bar at zero is high.
  • Check the curve of the predictors before you explain zeros.
  • Zero inflation or hurdle? If nobody can tell the two kinds of zeros apart in principle, the hurdle model is easier to explain. The fits are often the same.
  • Read the zero part with care. In zeroinfl() it models the probability of a certain zero; in hurdle() the probability of a positive count.
  • Say what you report. The coefficients of the count part are for those who can publish. The effect on the mean of everybody is another number: \(E(y) = (1 - \pi)\mu\).
  • Large standard errors in the zero part are the rule. With few zeros, or a large \(\mu\), the zero part is hardly identified.
  • Different predictors in the two parts are allowed: zeroinfl(y ~ x | z). With | 1 there is one constant probability.
  • Exposure: an offset(log(time)) goes into the count part.
  • Clustered data: glmmTMB fits zero-inflated models with random effects.

## The question: two kinds of nothing

Of 915 PhD students in biochemistry, 275 published **no article** in the last three years of their studies. Why not?

There may be two kinds of them. Some were never going to publish: they planned a career outside research, or their laboratory does not publish with students. For them the number is zero by nature. Others could have published, and worked on it, but it happened that nothing appeared in those three years. The data show the same 0 for both.

**Zero-inflated** models (Lambert, 1992) say exactly this: the zeros come from two sources. One part of the model says who is in the group of *certain* zeros. The other is an ordinary count model (Poisson or negative binomial) for everybody else, which produces zeros too. They are popular, easy to fit, and easy to over-interpret. **Many zeros do not prove that there are two kinds of them.** This page fits the models and then asks whether they are needed.

## 3. The model step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: the count part

For the students who can publish, a count distribution with mean $\mu_i = \exp(x_i^\top\beta)$. Either Poisson, or negative binomial with variance $\mu + \mu^2/\theta$.

### Step 2: the zero part

With probability $\pi_i$ a student belongs to the group that never publishes (a **structural** zero):

$$\log\frac{\pi_i}{1 - \pi_i} = z_i^\top\gamma$$

### Step 3: put together

$$P(y_i = 0) = \pi_i + (1 - \pi_i)\, f(0 \mid \mu_i), \qquad P(y_i = k) = (1 - \pi_i)\, f(k \mid \mu_i) \;\text{ for } k \ge 1$$

A zero can come from either source. Anything else can only come from the count part. The mean is $E(y_i) = (1 - \pi_i)\,\mu_i$.

### Step 4: the difference from a hurdle model

A [hurdle model](EXAMPLE_HURDLE_TWO_PART.Rmd) has **one** kind of zero, and its count part cannot produce zeros. So its zero part says "any article or none", and the zero part of a zero-inflated model says "in the group that never publishes". **The signs are opposite, and the meanings differ.** This is the most common mistake in reading the output.

### Step 5: maximum likelihood, by hand

```{r by_hand}
X <- model.matrix(~ female + married + kids + prestige + mentor, data = phd)
y <- phd$articles
k <- ncol(X)
minus_loglik <- function(par) {                              # zero-inflated Poisson
  mu <- exp(drop(X %*% par[1:k])); p <- plogis(drop(X %*% par[k + 1:k]))
  -sum(log(ifelse(y == 0, p + (1 - p) * dpois(0, mu), (1 - p) * dpois(y, mu))))
}
pois  <- glm(articles ~ female + married + kids + prestige + mentor, family = poisson, data = phd)
start <- c(coef(pois), -1, rep(0, k - 1))
hand  <- optim(start, minus_loglik, method = "BFGS", control = list(maxit = 1000, reltol = 1e-12))
zip <- zeroinfl(
  articles ~ female + married + kids + prestige + mentor,
  data = phd,
  dist = "poisson"
)
c(by_hand = -hand$value, zeroinfl = as.numeric(logLik(zip)))
both <- rbind(by_hand = hand$par[c(1:2, k + 6)], zeroinfl = unname(coef(zip))[c(1:2, k + 6)])
colnames(both) <- c("(Intercept)", "female", "zero part: (Intercept)")
round(both, 3)
```

## 5. Do we need it? Four ways to ask

### The zeros, and everything else

```{r zeros}

zeros <- c(

  Poisson = sum(dpois(0, fitted(pois))),
  ZIP = sum(predict(zip, type = "prob")[, 1]),
  NB = sum(dnbinom(0, mu = fitted(nb), size = nb$theta)),

```

Poisson expects `r f0(zeros[["Poisson"]])` zeros instead of `r f0(zeros[["observed"]])`. But the **plain negative binomial expects `r f0(zeros[["NB"]])`**, without any zero inflation. It has one parameter more than Poisson, and it repairs the zeros and the tail at the same time. ZIP expects `r f0(zeros[["ZIP"]])`.

A **rootogram** compares all the counts (Kleiber & Zeileis, 2016). The bars are the observed frequencies, hanging from the expected ones, on a square-root scale. A bar that ends above the zero line means too few observed, one that goes below it too many.

The Poisson model fails everywhere. ZIP repairs the zero and misses the rest: it expects too few students with one article (the bar goes below the line), too many with three, and too few with six or seven. The NB and the ZINB both fit.

### Information criteria

```{r criteria}
models <- list(Poisson = pois, ZIP = zip, NB = nb, ZINB = zinb, `hurdle NB` = hurdle_nb)
crit <- data.frame(
  model = names(models),
  parameters = sapply(models, function(m) attr(logLik(m), "df")),
  logLik = sapply(models, function(m) as.numeric(logLik(m))),
  AIC = sapply(models, AIC),
  BIC = sapply(models, BIC),
  row.names = NULL
)
print(
  transform(crit, logLik = round(logLik, 1), AIC = round(AIC, 1), BIC = round(BIC, 1)),
  row.names = FALSE
)
get <- function(m, col) crit[crit$model == m, col]
```

From Poisson to ZIP the AIC falls by `r f0(get("Poisson", "AIC") - get("ZIP", "AIC"))`. From Poisson to **NB** it falls by `r f0(get("Poisson", "AIC") - get("NB", "AIC"))`, with one parameter instead of six. The ZINB is `r f1(get("NB", "AIC") - get("ZINB", "AIC"))` points better than the NB by AIC, and `r f1(get("ZINB", "BIC") - get("NB", "BIC"))` points **worse** by BIC, which charges more for its six extra parameters. The two criteria disagree, and the evidence for a second kind of zero is weak.

### Vuong's test, and why not to use it here

```{r vuong}
vt <- capture.output(vuong(nb, zinb))
cat(vt, sep = "\n")
```

Vuong's test compares two models that are not nested. It is printed in many papers as a "test of zero inflation". But the NB **is** a special case of the ZINB (with $\pi = 0$, which is at the edge of the possible values), so the conditions of the test do not hold, and its *p* value means little (Wilson, 2015). And the raw, AIC-corrected and BIC-corrected versions give three different answers.

### A parametric bootstrap

The honest test simulates. Take the fitted NB (no inflation), generate new data from it, fit both models, and see how often the gain in likelihood is as large as ours by chance alone:

```{r bootstrap}
gain <- 2 * (as.numeric(logLik(zinb)) - as.numeric(logLik(nb)))
set.seed(81)
gains <- replicate(200, {
  d <- phd
  d$articles <- rnbinom(nrow(d), mu = fitted(nb), size = nb$theta)
  a <- try(suppressWarnings(glm.nb(formula(nb), data = d)), silent = TRUE)
  z <- try(suppressWarnings(zeroinfl(formula(zinb), data = d, dist = "negbin")), silent = TRUE)
  if (inherits(a, "try-error") || inherits(z, "try-error")) {
    NA
  } else {
    2 * (as.numeric(logLik(z)) - as.numeric(logLik(a)))
  }
})
p_boot <- (1 + sum(gains >= gain, na.rm = TRUE)) / (1 + sum(!is.na(gains)))
cbind(
  value = c(
    our_gain = gain,
    mean_gain_without_inflation = mean(gains, na.rm = TRUE),
    p = p_boot,
    failed = sum(is.na(gains))
  )
)
```

Our gain is `r f1(gain)`. Without any inflation the gain is `r f1(mean(gains, na.rm = TRUE))` on average, and a gain as large as ours has the probability *p* `r pval(p_boot)`. So the ZINB describes something that the NB does not. The next section asks what.

Adding zero inflation to it changes the AIC by `r f1(AIC(zinb_bend) - AIC(nb_bend))`. The "two kinds of zeros" `r if (AIC(zinb_bend) - AIC(nb_bend) > -2) "have disappeared. They were a wrong curve." else "are still preferred, but by less."`

### A warning from simulation

What does one conclude who compares only Poisson with ZIP, when the truth is a negative binomial without any certain zeros?

```{r false_inflation}
set.seed(82)
false_zip <- replicate(200, {
  d <- phd
  d$articles <- rnbinom(nrow(d), mu = fitted(nb), size = nb$theta)
  p0 <- glm(formula(pois), family = poisson, data = d)
  z0 <- suppressWarnings(zeroinfl(formula(zip), data = d))
  c(zip_wins = AIC(z0) < AIC(p0), certain_zeros = mean(predict(z0, type = "zero")))
})
rowMeans(false_zip)
```

ZIP beats Poisson in `r pct(mean(false_zip["zip_wins", ]))` of the data sets, and it declares `r pct(mean(false_zip["certain_zeros", ]))` of the students to be certain zeros on average. There were **none**. Beating Poisson proves overdispersion, not two kinds of zeros.

## 7. Things to keep in mind

- **First the negative binomial.** Many zeros do not mean zero inflation (Warton, 2005). Use a zero-inflated model when theory says that there are certain zeros (people who do not own a car and car accidents, men and pregnancies), not because the bar at zero is high.
- **Check the curve** of the predictors before you explain zeros.
- **Zero inflation or hurdle?** If nobody can tell the two kinds of zeros apart in principle, the hurdle model is easier to explain. The fits are often the same.
- **Read the zero part with care.** In `zeroinfl()` it models the probability of a certain **zero**; in `hurdle()` the probability of a **positive** count.
- **Say what you report.** The coefficients of the count part are for those who can publish. The effect on the mean of everybody is another number: $E(y) = (1 - \pi)\mu$.
- **Large standard errors in the zero part** are the rule. With few zeros, or a large $\mu$, the zero part is hardly identified.
- **Different predictors** in the two parts are allowed: `zeroinfl(y ~ x | z)`. With `| 1` there is one constant probability.
- **Exposure**: an `offset(log(time))` goes into the count part.
- **Clustered data**: `glmmTMB` fits zero-inflated models with random effects.

## 9. Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | Counts with zeros from two sources: some units can never have a positive count |
| **The model** | $P(0) = \pi + (1 - \pi) f(0)$, $P(k) = (1 - \pi) f(k)$; $E(y) = (1 - \pi)\mu$ |
| **How (R)** | `pscl::zeroinfl(y ~ x | z, data, dist = "poisson")` or `"negbin"` |
| **Count part** | $e^\beta$: ratio of expected counts, for those who can |
| **Zero part** | Positive coefficient: more likely a **certain zero** |
| **Compare with** | The negative binomial: AIC, BIC, rootograms, parametric bootstrap |
| **Warning** | Overdispersion or a wrong curve can look like zero inflation |
| **Related pages** | [Hurdle models](EXAMPLE_HURDLE_TWO_PART.Rmd), [generalized linear models](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED.rmd) (Poisson, negative binomial) |

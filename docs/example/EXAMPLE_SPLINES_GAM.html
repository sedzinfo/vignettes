Splines and GAMs: At What Temperature Do the Fewest People Die?

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source
Splines and generalized additive models (GAM) in R with mgcv, on 14 years of daily deaths and temperature in Chicago (1987-2000): basis functions, penalised splines by hand, effective degrees of freedom, GCV and REML, confounding by season, delayed effects, negative binomial outcomes, relative risks and the temperature of least mortality with intervals, checks of the basis, concurvity, autocorrelation, the 1995 heat wave and APA reporting.

The question: too cold, too hot, and in between

People die of heat, and people die of cold. Between the two there must be a temperature at which the fewest people die. What is it? And how much worse are the extremes?

No formula is known for this curve. It should be high on the left, low in the middle and high on the right, but it need not be a parabola: heat may act suddenly above some threshold, and cold slowly. So we want a method that finds the shape by itself. But we also need more than a smooth line through a cloud of points (as LOESS gives). In winter there are also influenza, short days and indoor air. The season must be in the model next to the temperature, like a covariate in a regression.

Splines are flexible curves made of ordinary regression columns. A generalized additive model (GAM) is a regression in which some predictors enter as splines:

\[\log E(\text{deaths}) = \beta_0 + s_1(\text{temperature}) + s_2(\text{time}) + \text{day of the week}\]

Each \(s\) is a smooth curve whose shape and wiggliness are estimated from the data. The rest is a generalized linear model, as in this page.

3. Splines step by step, with formulas

Step 1: polynomials in pieces

Cut the range of the temperature at some points, the knots, and use a cubic polynomial in each piece, joined so smoothly that the eye does not see the joints: equal value, slope and curvature on both sides. That is a cubic spline. It can bend where it is needed, and a bend on one side does not disturb the other side, as it would in a polynomial.

Step 2: a spline is a sum of columns

Any cubic spline can be written as a weighted sum of fixed curves, the basis functions:

\[s(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \beta_j\, B_j(x)\]

So once the knots are fixed, a spline is a linear model: each \(B_j(x)\) is a column, and least squares finds the \(\beta\)’s. The basis of B-splines has functions that are zero except over a few pieces:

Code 
grid  <- seq(min(chicago$temp), max(chicago$temp), length.out = 300)

Step 3: how many knots? Use many, and pay for the wiggles

With few knots the curve is stiff, with many it follows the noise. And where to put them? The modern answer is: take more than enough knots, and add a penalty for wiggliness to the sum of squares:

\[\sum_i \big[y_i - s(x_i)\big]^2 + \lambda \int s''(x)^2\, dx\]

\(s''\) is the curvature. \(\lambda\) decides the price: with \(\lambda = 0\) we get the wild curve of all the knots, with \(\lambda \to \infty\) a straight line. The penalty is a quadratic form in the coefficients, \(\lambda\, \beta^\top S \beta\), so the solution is still a formula, like ridge regression:

\[\hat\beta = (B^\top B + \lambda S)^{-1} B^\top y\]

For B-splines with equally spaced knots, a simple and good \(S\) makes neighbouring coefficients differ smoothly. It is built from their second differences (P-splines; Eilers & Marx, 1996):

Code 
x <- chicago$temp; y <- chicago$death
n_basis <- 20
step  <- (max(x) - min(x)) / (n_basis - 3)
# equally spaced knots, beyond the ends too
ts <- seq(min(x) - 3 * step, max(x) + 3 * step, by = step)
B_    <- splineDesign(ts, x, outer.ok = TRUE)
D <- diff(diag(ncol(B_)), differences = 2) # second differences of the coefficients
S     <- crossprod(D)
fit_by_hand <- function(lambda) {
  A    <- solve(crossprod(B_) + lambda * S)
  beta <- A %*% crossprod(B_, y)
  edf  <- sum(diag(A %*% crossprod(B_)))                             # effective degrees of freedom
  rss  <- sum((y - B_ %*% beta)^2)
  list(beta = beta, edf = edf, GCV = length(y) * rss / (length(y) - edf)^2)
}
lambdas <- 10^seq(-2, 7, by = 0.25)
trials  <- lapply(lambdas, fit_by_hand)
gcv     <- sapply(trials, `[[`, "GCV")
best    <- which.min(gcv)
c(best_lambda = lambdas[best], edf = trials[[best]]$edf)
best_lambda         edf 
   3.162278   12.689426

Step 4: effective degrees of freedom

The penalty ties the coefficients together, so 20 columns do not count as 20 parameters. The effective degrees of freedom (edf), the trace of the matrix that turns \(y\) into \(\hat y\), count how many parameters the curve really uses: 2 for a straight line, 20 for the unpenalised spline, and here 12.7.

Step 5: choosing \(\lambda\)

By hand we took the \(\lambda\) with the smallest GCV (generalised cross-validation), which imitates leaving out one day at a time. mgcv can do the same. The better default today is REML: the penalty is read as a statement that the coefficients come from a normal distribution, and \(\lambda\) becomes a variance ratio that is estimated like that of a mixed model. REML overfits less often (Wood, 2011).

Code 
shown <- c(which(lambdas == 10^-2), best, length(lambdas))

Step 6: from one spline to a GAM

A GAM puts several splines side by side, each with its own \(\lambda\), and a link function and distribution around them. The counts of deaths vary a bit more than a Poisson distribution allows, so we use the negative binomial (family = nb), which adds one parameter for the extra variation. The \(s\) curves are centred: each has mean zero over the data, and the intercept takes the level.

curve_of <- function(model, terms, newdata) {
  X <- predict(model, newdata, type = "lpmatrix") # the columns of the model at these values
  # keep only these terms
  X[, !grepl(paste(gsub("([()])", "\\\\\\1", terms), collapse = "|"), colnames(X))] <- 0

  se <- sqrt(rowSums((X %*% vcov(model)) * X))

    newdata,
    fit = 100 * (exp(fit) - 1),
    low = 100 * (exp(fit - 1.96 * se) - 1),

  )
}

5. The answer: the temperature with the fewest deaths

The question is about a temperature that lasts: a week and a day at the same temperature. In the additive model this is the sum of the two curves, \(s_1(x) + s_2(x)\), at the same \(x\). We compute it for a grid, find its lowest point, and express everything relative to it, as a relative risk (RR). The uncertainty of a difference between two points of a curve comes from the model matrix and the covariance matrix of the coefficients:

\[\log \text{RR}(x) = (X_x - X_{\min})\,\hat\beta, \qquad SE = \sqrt{(X_x - X_{\min})\, V\, (X_x - X_{\min})^\top}\]

Code 
lasting <- function(model, from = -20, to = 31) {
  g <- data.frame(temp = seq(from, to, by = 0.5))
  g$week <- g$temp
  g$time <- 2500
  g$dow <- "Wednesday"
  X <- predict(model, g, type = "lpmatrix")
  X[, !grepl("s\\(temp\\)|s\\(week\\)", colnames(X))] <- 0
  f <- drop(X %*% coef(model))
  i <- which.min(f)
  d <- sweep(X, 2, X[i, ]) # each row minus the row of the minimum
  logrr <- drop(d %*% coef(model))
  se <- sqrt(rowSums((d %*% vcov(model)) * d))
  set.seed(3)
  draws <- rmvn(2000, coef(model), vcov(model)) # 2,000 curves that are compatible with the data
  minima <- g$temp[apply(X %*% t(draws), 2, which.min)]
  list(
    curve = data.frame(
      temp = g$temp,
      rr = exp(logrr),
      low = exp(logrr - 1.96 * se),
      high = exp(logrr + 1.96 * se)
    ),
    minimum = g$temp[i],
    interval = quantile(minima, c(0.025, 0.975))
  )
}

8. Things to keep in mind

  • Additive means that the curve of one predictor is the same at all values of the others. If heat is worse after a hot week, the model needs an interaction, a surface: te(temp, week).
  • Beyond the data, a penalised spline continues roughly straight. It is an extrapolation like any other.
  • edf is not the number of bends, and the p value of a smooth does not say where the curve matters. Report the picture with its band, and differences between meaningful points.
  • The band is pointwise, for the curve around its mean. For a difference from a reference point, use the model matrix as in section 5: the band is zero wide at the reference.
  • Delays. We used one day and one week. Distributed lag models (dlnm) estimate a curve for every delay (Gasparrini, 2011).
  • Not causal by itself. Influenza epidemics come in cold weeks, and the smooth of time cannot remove an epidemic of two weeks. Part of the “cold” curve may be influenza.
  • Deaths of one city say what the city is used to. Such curves differ between climates: people in warm places are hurt by cold that others do not notice (Curriero et al., 2002).
  • Speed. For large data use bam() instead of gam().

## The question: too cold, too hot, and in between

People die of heat, and people die of cold. Between the two there must be a temperature at which the fewest people die. What is it? And how much worse are the extremes?

No formula is known for this curve. It should be high on the left, low in the middle and high on the right, but it need not be a parabola: heat may act suddenly above some threshold, and cold slowly. So we want a method that finds the **shape** by itself. But we also need more than a smooth line through a cloud of points (as [LOESS](EXAMPLE_LOESS.Rmd) gives). In winter there are also influenza, short days and indoor air. The season must be in the model **next to** the temperature, like a covariate in a regression.

**Splines** are flexible curves made of ordinary regression columns. A **generalized additive model** (GAM) is a regression in which some predictors enter as splines:

$$\log E(\text{deaths}) = \beta_0 + s_1(\text{temperature}) + s_2(\text{time}) + \text{day of the week}$$

Each $s$ is a smooth curve whose shape and wiggliness are estimated from the data. The rest is a generalized linear model, as in [this page](EXAMPLE_GENERALIZED.rmd).

## 3. Splines step by step, with formulas

### Step 1: polynomials in pieces

Cut the range of the temperature at some points, the **knots**, and use a cubic polynomial in each piece, joined so smoothly that the eye does not see the joints: equal value, slope and curvature on both sides. That is a **cubic spline**. It can bend where it is needed, and a bend on one side does not disturb the other side, as it would in a [polynomial](EXAMPLE_POLYNOMIAL_REGRESSION.Rmd).

### Step 2: a spline is a sum of columns

Any cubic spline can be written as a weighted sum of fixed curves, the **basis functions**:

$$s(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \beta_j\, B_j(x)$$

So once the knots are fixed, a spline is a linear model: each $B_j(x)$ is a column, and least squares finds the $\beta$'s. The basis of B-splines has functions that are zero except over a few pieces:

```{r basis}
grid  <- seq(min(chicago$temp), max(chicago$temp), length.out = 300)
knots <- seq(-27, 34, length.out = 9)                                  # 8 pieces, for the drawing
B     <- splineDesign(c(rep(knots[1], 3), knots, rep(knots[9], 3)), grid, outer.ok = TRUE)
basis <- data.frame(
  temp = grid,
  value = as.vector(B),
  j = factor(rep(seq_len(ncol(B)), each = length(grid)))
)

ggplot(basis, aes(temp, value, group = j)) +

       x = "temperature (°C)", y = "value of the column") +
  theme_bw()
```

### Step 3: how many knots? Use many, and pay for the wiggles

With few knots the curve is stiff, with many it follows the noise. And where to put them? The modern answer is: take **more than enough** knots, and add a penalty for wiggliness to the sum of squares:

$$\sum_i \big[y_i - s(x_i)\big]^2 + \lambda \int s''(x)^2\, dx$$

$s''$ is the curvature. $\lambda$ decides the price: with $\lambda = 0$ we get the wild curve of all the knots, with $\lambda \to \infty$ a straight line. The penalty is a quadratic form in the coefficients, $\lambda\, \beta^\top S \beta$, so the solution is still a formula, like [ridge regression](EXAMPLE_REGULARIZED_REGRESSION.Rmd):

$$\hat\beta = (B^\top B + \lambda S)^{-1} B^\top y$$

For B-splines with equally spaced knots, a simple and good $S$ makes **neighbouring coefficients** differ smoothly. It is built from their second differences (P-splines; Eilers & Marx, 1996):

```{r by_hand}
x <- chicago$temp; y <- chicago$death
n_basis <- 20
step  <- (max(x) - min(x)) / (n_basis - 3)
# equally spaced knots, beyond the ends too
ts <- seq(min(x) - 3 * step, max(x) + 3 * step, by = step)
B_    <- splineDesign(ts, x, outer.ok = TRUE)
D <- diff(diag(ncol(B_)), differences = 2) # second differences of the coefficients
S     <- crossprod(D)
fit_by_hand <- function(lambda) {
  A    <- solve(crossprod(B_) + lambda * S)
  beta <- A %*% crossprod(B_, y)
  edf  <- sum(diag(A %*% crossprod(B_)))                             # effective degrees of freedom
  rss  <- sum((y - B_ %*% beta)^2)
  list(beta = beta, edf = edf, GCV = length(y) * rss / (length(y) - edf)^2)
}
lambdas <- 10^seq(-2, 7, by = 0.25)
trials  <- lapply(lambdas, fit_by_hand)
gcv     <- sapply(trials, `[[`, "GCV")
best    <- which.min(gcv)
c(best_lambda = lambdas[best], edf = trials[[best]]$edf)
```

### Step 4: effective degrees of freedom

The penalty ties the coefficients together, so `r n_basis` columns do not count as `r n_basis` parameters. The **effective degrees of freedom** (edf), the trace of the matrix that turns $y$ into $\hat y$, count how many parameters the curve really uses: 2 for a straight line, `r n_basis` for the unpenalised spline, and here `r f1(trials[[best]]$edf)`.

### Step 5: choosing $\lambda$

By hand we took the $\lambda$ with the smallest GCV (generalised cross-validation), which imitates leaving out one day at a time. `mgcv` can do the same. The better default today is **REML**: the penalty is read as a statement that the coefficients come from a normal distribution, and $\lambda$ becomes a variance ratio that is estimated like that of a [mixed model](EXAMPLE_MULTILEVEL.Rmd). REML overfits less often (Wood, 2011).

### Step 6: from one spline to a GAM

A GAM puts several splines side by side, each with its own $\lambda$, and a link function and distribution around them. The counts of deaths vary a bit more than a Poisson distribution allows, so we use the **negative binomial** (`family = nb`), which adds one parameter for the extra variation. The $s$ curves are centred: each has mean zero over the data, and the intercept takes the level.

### Model A: temperature alone


- **`Parametric coefficients`** are as in any GLM with a log link: $e^{`r f3(coef(model_a)[["dowMonday"]])`}$ = `r f3(exp(coef(model_a)[["dowMonday"]]))`, so Mondays have `r f1(100 * (exp(coef(model_a)[["dowMonday"]]) - 1))`% more deaths than Sundays.
- **`edf`** of `s(temp)` = `r f1(summary(model_a)$edf)`: how wiggly the curve is. 1 would be a straight line.
- **`p-value`** of a smooth tests whether the whole curve is flat. It is approximate, and with `r num(nrow(chicago))` days nearly everything is "significant". Look at the curve instead.
- **`Deviance explained`** plays the role of $R^2$: `r f1(100 * summary(model_a)$dev.expl)`%.

There is no table of coefficients for the curve. It has coefficients, but nobody reads them. **A smooth is read as a picture.**

```{r curve_function}
# the curve of one or more smooths (in % of deaths, relative to the average of the curve), with its
# 95% band
curve_of <- function(model, terms, newdata) {
  X <- predict(model, newdata, type = "lpmatrix") # the columns of the model at these values
  # keep only these terms
  X[, !grepl(paste(gsub("([()])", "\\\\\\1", terms), collapse = "|"), colnames(X))] <- 0
  fit <- drop(X %*% coef(model))
  se <- sqrt(rowSums((X %*% vcov(model)) * X))
  data.frame(
    newdata,
    fit = 100 * (exp(fit) - 1),
    low = 100 * (exp(fit - 1.96 * se) - 1),
    high = 100 * (exp(fit + 1.96 * se) - 1)
  )
}
at <- data.frame(temp = seq(-22, 32, by = 0.5))
at$week <- 10
at$time <- 2500
at$dow <- "Wednesday" # the other terms are set to zero anyway
three <- rbind(cbind(curve_of(model_a, "s(temp)", at), model = "A: temperature alone"),
               cbind(curve_of(model_b, "s(temp)", at), model = "B: + season and trend"),
               cbind(curve_of(model_c, "s(temp)", at), model = "C: + the week before"))
```

## 5. The answer: the temperature with the fewest deaths

The question is about a temperature that **lasts**: a week and a day at the same temperature. In the additive model this is the sum of the two curves, $s_1(x) + s_2(x)$, at the same $x$. We compute it for a grid, find its lowest point, and express everything relative to it, as a **relative risk** (RR). The uncertainty of a difference between two points of a curve comes from the model matrix and the covariance matrix of the coefficients:

$$\log \text{RR}(x) = (X_x - X_{\min})\,\hat\beta, \qquad SE = \sqrt{(X_x - X_{\min})\, V\, (X_x - X_{\min})^\top}$$

```{r combined}
lasting <- function(model, from = -20, to = 31) {
  g <- data.frame(temp = seq(from, to, by = 0.5))
  g$week <- g$temp
  g$time <- 2500
  g$dow <- "Wednesday"
  X <- predict(model, g, type = "lpmatrix")
  X[, !grepl("s\\(temp\\)|s\\(week\\)", colnames(X))] <- 0
  f <- drop(X %*% coef(model))
  i <- which.min(f)
  d <- sweep(X, 2, X[i, ]) # each row minus the row of the minimum
  logrr <- drop(d %*% coef(model))
  se <- sqrt(rowSums((d %*% vcov(model)) * d))
  set.seed(3)
  draws <- rmvn(2000, coef(model), vcov(model)) # 2,000 curves that are compatible with the data
  minima <- g$temp[apply(X %*% t(draws), 2, which.min)]
  list(
    curve = data.frame(
      temp = g$temp,
      rr = exp(logrr),
      low = exp(logrr - 1.96 * se),
      high = exp(logrr + 1.96 * se)
    ),
    minimum = g$temp[i],
    interval = quantile(minima, c(0.025, 0.975))
  )
}
answer <- lasting(model_c)
rr <- function(t) answer$curve[answer$curve$temp == t, ]
c(minimum = answer$minimum, answer$interval)
round(rbind(rr(-15), rr(-10), rr(0), rr(10), rr(28), rr(30)), 3)
```

```{r combined_chart}
ggplot(answer$curve, aes(temp, rr)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 1, colour = "grey60") +
  geom_ribbon(aes(ymin = low, ymax = high), fill = "#2a78d6", alpha = 0.2) +
  geom_line(colour = "#2a78d6", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = answer$minimum, linetype = "dashed", colour = "#eb6834") +
  labs(title = "Relative risk of death when a temperature lasts a week and a day",
       subtitle = "Compared with the temperature with the fewest deaths (dashed line)",
       x = "temperature (°C)", y = "relative risk") +
  theme_bw()
```

- The fewest people die at **`r answer$minimum` °C**, with an interval from `r answer$interval[[1]]` to `r answer$interval[[2]]` °C (from 2,000 simulated curves).
- At 30 °C the risk is `r f2(rr(30)$rr)` times as high, 95% CI [`r f2(rr(30)$low)`, `r f2(rr(30)$high)`]. At −15 °C, `r f2(rr(-15)$rr)` times, [`r f2(rr(-15)$low)`, `r f2(rr(-15)$high)`].
- The two sides have different shapes: the cold side rises slowly over 40 degrees, the hot side turns up sharply above about 25 °C. A parabola would have missed both.

## 6. Checking the model

### Was the basis large enough?

`k` is the **upper limit** of the wiggliness. If the edf come close to it, the curve was cut short, and `k` should be raised:

```{r k_check}
k.check(model_c)
```

`k'` is the limit and `edf` what was used. The smooths of temperature are near their limit of 9, so we double the limit and see if the answer changes:

```{r larger_k}
model_k <- gam(
  death ~ s(temp, k = 20) + s(week, k = 20) + s(time, k = 150) + dow,
  family = nb,
  data = chicago,
  method = "REML"
)
answer_k <- lasting(model_k)
rbind(
  k10 = c(edf = summary(model_c)$s.table[1:2, "edf"], minimum = answer$minimum, AIC = AIC(model_c)),
  k20 = c(
    edf = summary(model_k)$s.table[1:2, "edf"],
    minimum = answer_k$minimum,
    AIC = AIC(model_k)
  )
)
```

The minimum moves from `r answer$minimum` to `r answer_k$minimum` °C, and the AIC changes by `r f1(AIC(model_k) - AIC(model_c))`. So the simulated interval of section 5 was too narrow: it takes the basis as given. The smooth of `time` fails the test of `k.check` (its *p* value is small), which brings us to the next point.

### Autocorrelation

```{r acf}
lag1 <- sapply(list(A = model_a, B = model_b, C = model_c), function(m) {
  acf(resid(m, type = "pearson"), plot = FALSE)$acf[2]
})
lag1
```

The residuals of neighbouring days correlate at `r f2(lag1[["A"]])` in model A and `r f2(lag1[["C"]])` in model C. What is left is small, but it makes the bands a little too narrow. If `time` were given many more degrees of freedom, it would take it all, and with it part of the effect of temperature. How smooth the season should be is a **judgement** in this kind of study, and the honest thing is to vary it:

```{r season_sensitivity}
season <- do.call(
  rbind,
  lapply(c(30, 60, 100, 150, 200), function(k) {
    m <- gam(
      death ~ s(temp) + s(week) + s(time, k = k) + dow,
      family = nb,
      data = chicago,
      method = "REML"
    )
    a <- lasting(m)
    data.frame(
      k,
      edf_time = summary(m)$s.table["s(time)", "edf"],
      minimum = a$minimum,
      RR_cold = a$curve$rr[a$curve$temp == -15],
      RR_hot = a$curve$rr[a$curve$temp == 30],
      AIC = AIC(m)
    )
  })
)
round(season, 2)
```

The lowest point and the hot side do not depend on it. The cold side does: the relative risk at −15 °C goes from `r f2(season$RR_cold[1])` with a stiff season to `r f2(season$RR_cold[5])` with a flexible one. Heat comes in short bursts that no season can imitate; cold lasts, and shares its time with the winter.

### Concurvity

In a GAM two smooths can carry the same information, as correlated predictors do. It is called **concurvity**, on a scale from 0 to 1:

```{r concurvity}
round(concurvity(model_c, full = FALSE)$estimate, 2)
```

Each column says how much of the smooth could be explained by the one in the row. The temperatures of the day and of the week before overlap, as they must, which makes their separate curves less certain than their sum. And the smooth of time could explain most of the weekly temperature, because the season sets it: what the model calls the effect of a cold week is learned from weeks that were colder than usual for their season.

### The heat wave of July 1995

One week may decide the hot side of the curve. Without the week of 13 to 19 July 1995:

## 7. Splines without penalties, and other choices

Splines are just columns, so they work in any regression function. `ns(x, df)` makes a **natural** cubic spline, which is a straight line beyond the last knots, and is the usual choice when the number of degrees of freedom is fixed in advance:

```{r natural_spline}
glm_ns <- glm(
  death ~ ns(temp, df = 4) + ns(week, df = 4) + ns(time, df = 7 * 14) + dow,
  family = quasipoisson,
  data = chicago
)
g <- data.frame(temp = seq(-20, 31, by = 0.5))
g$week <- g$temp
g$time <- 2500
g$dow <- "Wednesday"
p_ns <- predict(glm_ns, g)
g$temp[which.min(p_ns)]
```

With 4 degrees of freedom for each temperature and 7 per year for the season, the lowest point is at `r g$temp[which.min(p_ns)]` °C, against `r answer$minimum` °C: four degrees of freedom are too stiff for the sharp turn at the hot end. This is the weakness of fixed degrees of freedom. The result depends on a number that somebody had to choose, and the penalised model estimates it.

| | Polynomial | LOESS | Spline, fixed df | Penalised spline (GAM) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wiggliness set by | degree | span | number of knots | estimated (REML) |
| Other predictors | yes | hardly | yes | yes |
| Other outcomes (counts, yes/no) | yes | no | yes | yes |
| Tests, intervals | exact | approximate | exact | approximate |
| At the ends | wild | unstable | natural: straight | straight-ish |

## 8. Things to keep in mind

- **Additive** means that the curve of one predictor is the same at all values of the others. If heat is worse after a hot week, the model needs an interaction, a surface: `te(temp, week)`.
- **Beyond the data**, a penalised spline continues roughly straight. It is an extrapolation like any other.
- **edf is not the number of bends**, and the *p* value of a smooth does not say where the curve matters. Report the picture with its band, and differences between meaningful points.
- **The band is pointwise, for the curve around its mean.** For a difference from a reference point, use the model matrix as in section 5: the band is zero wide at the reference.
- **Delays.** We used one day and one week. Distributed lag models (`dlnm`) estimate a curve for every delay (Gasparrini, 2011).
- **Not causal by itself.** Influenza epidemics come in cold weeks, and the smooth of time cannot remove an epidemic of two weeks. Part of the "cold" curve may be influenza.
- **Deaths of one city** say what the city is used to. Such curves differ between climates: people in warm places are hurt by cold that others do not notice (Curriero et al., 2002).
- **Speed.** For large data use `bam()` instead of `gam()`.

**Method** (data analysis):

> We modelled the daily number of deaths (accidents excluded) in Chicago from 1987 to 2000 (*N* = `r num(nrow(chicago))` days) with a generalized additive model with a negative binomial distribution and a log link. The predictors were smooth functions (thin plate regression splines; Wood, 2017) of the mean temperature of the day, of the mean temperature of the seven preceding days and of time (basis dimension 150, for season and trend), and the day of the week. Smoothness was selected by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The relative risk of a temperature that lasts is the sum of the two temperature curves, relative to the temperature at which it is lowest; its confidence interval is based on the covariance matrix of the coefficients, and that of the temperature of minimum mortality on 2,000 simulations from their distribution. Analyses were conducted in R (Version `r paste(R.version$major, R.version$minor, sep = ".")`; R Core Team, `r R.version$year`) with the package mgcv (Wood, 2017).

**Results:**

> Deaths were related to the temperature of the day and to that of the preceding week (Table 1); the model explained `r f1(100 * summary(model_c)$dev.expl)`% of the deviance. The temperature with the lowest mortality was `r answer$minimum` °C, 95% CI [`r f1(answer$interval[[1]])`, `r f1(answer$interval[[2]])`]. Compared with it, the relative risk was `r f2(rr(-15)$rr)`, 95% CI [`r f2(rr(-15)$low)`, `r f2(rr(-15)$high)`], at −15 °C and `r f2(rr(30)$rr)`, 95% CI [`r f2(rr(30)$low)`, `r f2(rr(30)$high)`], at 30 °C (Table 2).
